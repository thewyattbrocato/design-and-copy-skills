No, that's not enough to show the program worked. It's good evidence that the program was well received and that people learned the material. It doesn't show that the program changed how well agents do their jobs.

**What your numbers show**
- **Completion (12/12):** Onboarding is usually mandatory, so near-100% completion is expected. It says little about quality.
- **Survey (4.6/5):** This measures how people felt about the course. Satisfaction often correlates weakly with learning or performance.
- **Quiz (91%):** This shows people retained content right after the course. It doesn't show they can apply it with real customers. Without a pre-test or a comparison group, you also can't tell how much of the 91% came from the course.

**What's missing**
- **A baseline:** Compared with what? The previous onboarding, or the same agents before training?
- **On-the-job outcomes:** Whether agents actually perform better.
- **Sample context:** With 12 people, one or two outliers move the averages a lot. That doesn't make the results meaningless, but you shouldn't claim more than they support.

**What I'd measure, compared with the prior cohort or cohorts**
- Time to proficiency, such as weeks until they hit target handle time or ticket volume
- QA or quality scores in the first 30, 60, and 90 days
- CSAT on their tickets
- First-contact resolution and escalation rate
- Questions they ask their mentors or leads in the first weeks
- 90-day retention, if you have it
- Manager ratings of how prepared the agents were

If you have historical data on earlier cohorts, you can probably pull most of this now.

**What to tell your director**
Something like this:

> "Early indicators are strong: 100% completion, 4.6/5 satisfaction, and a 91% average quiz score. Those show the program is well received and people are learning the content. They don't yet show it improves job performance. With a cohort of 12, I'd treat this as encouraging, not proof. To close that gap, I'll compare this cohort's ramp time, QA scores, and CSAT at 30/60/90 days against the previous cohort, and I'll collect manager feedback on readiness. I can report back on that at [date]."

This is more credible than overclaiming. If she repeats the claim upward and the performance data doesn't back it up, that reflects badly on both of you. If the data does back it up, you'll have a much stronger story.
