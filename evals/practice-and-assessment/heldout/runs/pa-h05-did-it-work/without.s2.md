No, that isn't enough to show the program worked. It shows the program was delivered and well received, which is worth reporting, but it doesn't show agents got better at their jobs.

**What your numbers show**
- **12/12 completion:** agents attended and finished. Completion is usually expected for a two-week onboarding, so it says little about quality.
- **4.6/5 survey:** agents liked it. Satisfaction is a weak predictor of job performance, and with 12 people one or two generous raters move the average.
- **91% quiz average:** agents retained the material right after the course, assuming the quiz tests what they need on the job. It doesn't show they can apply it with real customers.

These are all measures of reaction and short-term learning. None of them shows behavior change or business results, and none has a comparison point. You don't know whether 91% or 4.6 is better than what the old onboarding produced.

**What would make the case**
1. **A comparison.** Compare against the previous cohort or cohorts on the same metrics. Better still, compare against a concurrent group that didn't get the new program, if one exists.
2. **On-the-job performance.** Pull metrics for the new cohort at 30, 60, and 90 days:
   - Time to ramp (how long until they hit target productivity)
   - CSAT
   - First-contact resolution
   - Average handle time
   - Escalation rate
   - QA scores
3. **Retention.** Check whether new hires stay longer, or at least whether early attrition dropped.
4. **Manager feedback.** Ask team leads whether the agents arrived better prepared than before.
5. **Quiz validity.** Ask whether the quiz predicts anything. For example, check whether high scorers perform better on the floor.

**What to tell your director**
Something like: "The first cohort completed the program, rated it highly (4.6/5), and scored 91% on the final quiz, so the program is landing well and agents are absorbing the content. That's early evidence, not proof of impact. To show it works, I'll track this cohort's 30/60/90-day performance and ramp time against previous cohorts, and I'll bring you that comparison by [date]."

That framing is honest and credible. It also protects you if she or someone above her asks "compared to what?"

**Caveats to state up front**
- n=12 is small, so differences will be noisy.
- Without a comparison group, you can't separate the program's effect from the quality of the hires, the team they joined, or seasonal volume.
