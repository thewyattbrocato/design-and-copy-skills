**No, that isn't enough to show the program worked.** The three numbers measure different things, and none of them shows that agents do their jobs better.

- **Completion (12/12)** shows people finished the course. It says nothing about whether they learned or performed.
- **Survey 4.6/5** measures how participants felt about the course. Liking a course doesn't mean it changed behavior.
- **Quiz 91%** shows people can answer questions right after training. Without a pre-training score, you can't tell how much they learned. And quiz knowledge doesn't guarantee good handling of live tickets.

Your director is probably asking whether the program improved support performance. You need evidence on that.

**What to measure**

- **Time to proficiency:** how long until new agents hit your target QA score or ticket volume, compared with earlier cohorts.
- **Job metrics at 30, 60, and 90 days:** QA scores, first-contact resolution, average handle time, CSAT, escalation rate, and reopen rate.
- **Early attrition:** how many agents leave in the first 90 days.
- **Manager ratings** of readiness, if you have them.

**How to compare**

- Use a baseline. Pull the same metrics from the 2–3 cohorts before the new program, or from agents who started under the old onboarding.
- If you can, compare against a concurrent group, such as a cohort that got the old training at the same time.
- Get a pre-course quiz score if you don't have one, so you can show the gain from pre to post.

**What to tell her now**

Be honest about what the data shows so far. Something like:

> "All 12 agents completed the program, participants rated it 4.6/5, and the final quiz averaged 91%. Those show the program is well received and the content was learned. They don't yet show it improved job performance. I'm tracking time to proficiency, QA scores, and CSAT for this cohort at 30/60/90 days, compared against the last three cohorts. I'll have that comparison by [date]."

Also flag the sample size. With 12 people, one or two outliers can move an average a lot, so report the spread along with the mean, and avoid strong causal claims until you have more cohorts or a comparison group.
