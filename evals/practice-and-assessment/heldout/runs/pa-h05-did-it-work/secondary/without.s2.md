No, those three numbers don't show the program worked. They show that the agents finished, liked it, and passed a test at the end. That's useful, but it doesn't answer whether the program changed how agents perform on the job.

**Why these numbers fall short**

- **Completion (12/12)** only shows people stayed through the course. It doesn't say they learned anything.
- **Survey (4.6/5)** measures reaction. Satisfied learners aren't necessarily more effective agents, and the survey probably went only to people who finished, which skews it upward.
- **Quiz (91%)** has no baseline. If agents already knew most of this before starting, 91% shows little. Also, a quiz given at the end of a course usually tests material that was just taught.
- **n = 12** is too small to separate program effects from ordinary variation between people.

**What would show it worked**

Pick two or three job-performance measures you can compare before and after, ideally against a comparison group:

1. **Time to proficiency:** how long until new agents hit your target QA score or handle a set share of tickets independently. This is probably the strongest measure for onboarding.
2. **Quality:** QA scores, first-contact resolution, or escalation rate for agents in the first 30–90 days, compared with earlier cohorts or agents hired just before the program.
3. **Customer outcomes:** CSAT on their tickets, compared with the same window for previous cohorts.
4. **Retention:** 90-day attrition compared with prior cohorts. This matters to directors, and it's often the metric that gets budget approved.
5. **Manager ratings** at 30/60/90 days, using a simple rubric filled out before anyone knows which cohort an agent came from.

If you have time, add a **pre/post knowledge check** with the same questions at the start and end. That shows learning gain, which the 91% can't.

**What to tell your director**

Something like:

> The 12 agents in the last cohort all completed the two-week program. They rated it 4.6/5 and averaged 91% on the final quiz, so the content is landing. The real test is whether it improves on-the-job performance. I'm tracking time to proficiency, 90-day QA scores, and 90-day attrition against the prior two cohorts. I'll have the first 90-day read on [date]. Given the sample size, I'd treat early results as directional.

That's honest about what you have, shows you know what you still need to measure, and gives her a date. If she wants a yes/no answer now, the accurate one is "the program is working on learning and satisfaction; we don't yet have evidence on job performance."
