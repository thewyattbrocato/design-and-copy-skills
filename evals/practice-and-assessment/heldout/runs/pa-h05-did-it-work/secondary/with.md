**Short answer: no, not yet.** These three numbers show that the cohort finished, liked the course, and did well on the course's own quiz. None of them shows that agents work differently or better on the job, which is what your director is probably asking.

## What each number does and doesn't show

- **12 of 12 finished:** This measures attendance and completion. It says nothing about learning.
- **Survey 4.6/5:** This measures how people felt right after the course. It is a satisfaction score, not evidence of a skill.
- **Quiz 91%:** This measures recall of course content, taken on the last day, probably with items drawn from the course itself. It doesn't show agents can handle a real ticket, and it doesn't show they'll still know it in a month.

## What to tell her

Something like:

> "The first cohort completed the two-week program (12 of 12). They rated it 4.6/5 and averaged 91% on the final quiz, so the material landed. We have not yet shown it changed how agents handle tickets. To check that, I'm comparing [metric, e.g. first-contact resolution / QA score / reopen rate] for trained agents against [baseline period or untrained group], and running a practice check on the same skill about 30 days after the course. I'll have results by [date]."

That is accurate, and it doesn't oversell. If the director pushes for a "yes, it worked," the honest answer is that the data so far shows the course was completed and understood, not that it changed performance.

## What would count as proof

1. **Name the claim before you look.** For example: "Agents resolve [ticket type] correctly on first contact, scored against [QA rubric], at [target]."
2. **Use a metric the team already tracks**, with a baseline from before the program: [current first-contact resolution], [current QA score], [current reopen rate]. Don't invent a baseline. If you don't have one, pull it from existing records.
3. **Add a comparison.** Without one, you can't tell training from anything else that changed (new tooling, a seasonal ticket mix, a new manager). Options: compare with agents who started before the program, or stagger the next cohort so some agents start later and act as a comparison group.
4. **Run a delayed, observed check.** Around 30 days out, give each agent a few new scenarios (not the quiz items) and score them against a rubric written in advance. Report it as counts, such as "10 of 12 met all three criteria," and list which criterion the others missed.
5. **Report the survey beside the performance data**, not in place of it. If people rate it highly but few use the job aid or hit the standard, that gap is the finding.

With 12 agents, keep the reporting plain: give counts, not percentages or significance tests. A result like "9 of 12 improved on [metric] versus [baseline]" is useful; a claimed p-value would not be.

## One check before the next cohort

Confirm the program targets a skill gap, not a tool, process, or staffing problem. If agents are slow because a tool is broken or the escalation path is unclear, fixing that will move the numbers more than training will. Ask the team leads what agents currently get wrong and why, and compare that list with what the course teaches.
