**Go with B, but cut the lead's load by having reps practice with each other.** The lead's time is the real constraint, so design around it rather than dropping the role-plays.

**Why B:** Objection handling is a live skill. A 40-question MC test checks whether reps recognize the right answer, not whether they can respond under pressure on a call. It will be easy to score and will look rigorous, but it won't tell you who can handle "your panels are too expensive" or "I need to talk to my spouse."

**The lead-time math:** Three live role-plays for 15 reps is 45 role-plays at 20 minutes, about 15 hours of lead time, plus feedback. That's too much for a stretched lead to run alone, but it's manageable if you split the work.

**A version that works:**
1. **Lead runs one live demo** with the whole group (30-45 min). They model a strong response to two or three common objections and write a short shared rubric: acknowledge, ask a clarifying question, respond with a Halvorsen-specific point, close with a next step.
2. **Reps pair up for the three 20-minute role-plays** using the rubric and a list of objection cards. Each rep plays the customer once and the rep once, with a peer scoring them.
3. **The lead joins one round per rep**, or reviews a recording the rep submits, and gives feedback on that. That's about 5 minutes per rep, roughly 75 minutes total for the lead.
4. **Prep time** stays as planned. The whole thing fits in the 2-hour block.

**If the VP needs a scalable artifact:** Keep a short MC or written check (10 questions, not 40) as a knowledge gate before the role-plays. It's cheap to grade and shows the reps know the material. Make the role-play rubric the pass/fail standard for the skill itself, and have the lead sign off on it.

**What to tell the VP:** A 40-question test scales in grading, but it doesn't measure objection handling. Peer role-plays with a shared rubric scale too, and the lead only needs to touch a fraction of them.
