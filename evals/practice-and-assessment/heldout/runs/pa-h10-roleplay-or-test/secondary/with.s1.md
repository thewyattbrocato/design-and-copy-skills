**Go with B, but don't run it as three one-on-one sessions with the team lead.** Restructure it so the lead's time drops to roughly 5 hours total.

**Why B:** Objection handling is a live skill. A multiple-choice test checks whether reps can recognize a good answer, not whether they can respond under pressure with a prospect on the line. If the test is your only check, you'll learn who memorized the playbook, not who can handle "we already have a solar installer" on a call. Your VP's concern is valid, but scale matters less here than whether the training changes behavior.

**Why not B as written:** Lead time for 15 reps × three 20-minute sessions is about 15 hours of role-play, plus feedback and setup. That's what the team lead is worried about.

**Suggested format:**

1. **Peer role-plays in trios (most of the practice).** Each rep plays the prospect, the rep, and an observer in rotation. Give observers a one-page rubric: did the rep acknowledge the objection, ask a question before answering, and close back to a next step? Peers give feedback right away.
2. **One live role-play per rep with the lead (about 20 minutes each, 5 hours total).** This is the calibration check. The lead gives feedback on the same rubric and can spot habits peers miss.
3. **Optional: a short written check (10–15 questions) for product facts and objection rationale.** This is where A is useful. Use it for knowledge, not for the skill.

**Before you commit, check two things:**
- Whether the lead can run one live session per rep in the time available. If not, pair the live sessions with recorded calls the lead reviews asynchronously.
- Whether you can measure results later. Track objection handling on real calls in the first 30–60 days. That's the only evidence that tells you which option worked, and it's a good answer to the VP's "does it scale" question.
