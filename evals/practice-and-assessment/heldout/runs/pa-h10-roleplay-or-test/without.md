**Do B, but don't have the team lead run all of it.** Objection handling is a spoken skill: tone, pacing, and reacting to what the customer says. A multiple-choice test measures whether a rep can recognize the right answer, not whether they can deliver it on a live call. A rep can score 95% and still freeze when a homeowner says "I need to talk to my spouse." A results page would also give you a false sense that the reps are ready.

The VP is right that A scales, but it scales a measurement that doesn't predict call performance.

**Cost of B.** Three 20-minute role-plays for 15 reps is about 15 hours of team lead time. That's heavy, but it's spread over the onboarding window, and you can cut it:

- **Lead runs one role-play per rep, peers run the other two.** That's about 5 hours of lead time. Reps pair up, one plays the homeowner and one plays the rep, using a short scorecard and a list of the common objections (price, "I'll think about it," spouse, roof condition, "I got a cheaper quote"). Reps learn from playing the customer, too.
- **Run the lead's sessions in groups of 3–4.** Everyone watches, and the lead's feedback reaches the whole group. Sessions get more efficient, and watching others fail safely is useful.
- **Keep the scorecard short.** Four or five criteria are enough, for example: acknowledged the concern, asked a clarifying question, gave a relevant response, and moved toward a next step. Feedback then takes two minutes, not ten.

**What to give the VP.** Add a short 10-question knowledge check on product and pricing facts, auto-scored, and run it before the role-plays. It's cheap and scales. It also catches reps who don't know the facts, so the lead's time goes to delivery. The VP gets scalable data and a results page, and the skill that matters is still tested live.

If the lead truly can't give even 5 hours, I'd cut the peer role-plays down to two rounds before I'd replace the live practice with the test.
