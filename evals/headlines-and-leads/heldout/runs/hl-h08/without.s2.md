**Title: Everyone Wants to Make a Mug First: What I've Learned Teaching Beginners at Third Fire**

When we started Saturday classes for adult beginners last spring, I had a plan. I'd teach the basics, we'd make a few simple things, and somewhere down the line we'd work up to mugs. That plan didn't survive the first class.

Almost everyone who walks in wants to make a mug first. I get it. A mug is the thing you use every morning, the thing you imagine holding. But the mug is also one of the hardest things to get right, and I've spent this first year learning how to be honest about that without taking the wind out of anyone's sails.

**Why the mug is so hard**

A mug asks a beginner to do several things at once. The walls need to be even, because thick bottoms and thin rims are the most common beginner problem. The shape has to hold liquid and feel good in the hand. Then there's the handle, which is a separate piece of clay you have to attach to the body at the right moment, in the right condition, with the right pressure. If the handle and the mug are at different stages of dryness, they dry at different rates, and the join can crack in the kiln or weeks later.

Each of those skills takes time to build. A mug asks for all of them on day one.

**What I do now**

I stopped fighting the mug and started teaching toward it. In our groups of eight, I tell people up front that yes, we're going to make mugs, and that we'll get there by making other things first. Cylinders, small bowls, and cups without handles all teach the same core skills: centering, opening the clay, pulling up even walls, and trimming a clean foot. A good cylinder is a mug waiting for a handle.

People are surprisingly willing to hear this when I explain the reason. Nobody wants to spend six weeks on a mug that leaks or cracks. They'd rather learn why it goes wrong.

**Things that have surprised me**

*Centering is the real first lesson.* Most beginners think the hard part is shaping. It's actually getting the clay centered on the wheel. When the clay is off-center, everything after it is a fight. I've learned to let people spend more time here than feels productive, because it pays off for the rest of the class.

*Thick walls are a good sign, not a failure.* Beginners almost always leave too much clay at the bottom and in the walls. It's the natural response to being afraid of collapsing the piece. I'd rather see a heavy pot than a collapsed one, because trimming can fix thickness. Nothing fixes a wall that has already folded in on itself.

*Collapse is part of the process.* Everyone's piece falls over at some point. I've started saying in the first session that it's going to happen, so it feels like part of the process rather than a verdict on anyone's talent. The mood in the room changes when people know that going in.

*Eight is a good number.* With eight students I can get around to everyone, and the group is big enough that people learn from watching each other's mistakes. Someone else's wobble teaches as much as my demo does.

*Waiting is hard for adults.* Pottery asks you to wait through drying, bisque firing, glazing, and a second firing. Beginners are often startled that a piece takes weeks to come back. I now explain the whole timeline in the first class, so nobody feels like their work disappeared.

**What I'd tell a new student**

Your first mug probably won't be your best. It might be lopsided, a little heavy, or have a handle that looks like it was added by someone in a hurry. That's fine. It will still be yours, and you'll learn more from it than from any perfect one.

If you want to make a mug, come make a mug. I'll help you get there, and I'll be honest that the path goes through a lot of cylinders first. By the time you attach your first handle, you'll understand why it works, and that understanding is what I'm really teaching.

Our next Saturday session is coming up, and there's room for a few more beginners. Come try it.
