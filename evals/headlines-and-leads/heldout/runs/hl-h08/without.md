# What I Learned Teaching Complete Beginners to Make Pottery

Last spring we started Saturday classes at Third Fire Pottery for adult beginners, in groups of eight. I've taught and worked in clay for a long time, but teaching people who have never touched it taught me a lot. Here's what stuck with me from the first year.

## Almost everyone wants to make a mug

On the first day I ask what people hope to make. Nearly everyone says a mug. It makes sense. A mug is something you use every morning, it fits your hand, and it feels like *yours*. I love that instinct.

It's also the hardest thing we could start with.

A mug asks for almost every skill at once. You need to center the clay and open it evenly. You need walls that are thin enough to feel good but thick enough to survive. You need a base that doesn't crack, a rim that's comfortable to drink from, and a handle that's attached well and sits right in the hand. Each of those is a lesson on its own, and a mug wants all of them in the same piece.

The handle is where I see the most heartbreak. A handle is a different thickness from the body, and it has to be attached at the right moment, when both parts are the same level of dry. If they aren't, it can crack at the joint in the drying or in the kiln. You can do everything right and still lose a mug on day nine, in a shelf of pots that is already out of your hands.

## So we don't start there

I used to worry that steering beginners away from mugs would feel like a bait and switch. What I found is that if I'm honest about *why*, people are relieved.

We start with cylinders and simple bowls. They're less romantic, but they teach the skills a mug is built from: how to center, how to pull a wall, how to feel thickness through your fingertips, and how to trim a foot. By the time people come back to the mug, they aren't fighting the clay and the form at once. Many of them make a decent one sooner than people who jumped straight in, and they understand why it works.

I also let people know up front that their first mug probably won't be their best. That sets the right expectation, and it takes away the shame of the lopsided one.

## Clay moves slowly, and that's the lesson

Beginners often expect to make something in a session and walk out with it. Pottery doesn't work that way. A piece goes through drying, a first firing, glazing, and a second firing, and each stage changes it. It might be two or three weeks before someone holds the finished thing.

I used to see that as a drawback. Now I think it's one of the best things about the craft. Waiting teaches people to pay attention to the piece and to be patient. The kiln also has opinions, and a glaze rarely comes out exactly as imagined. Learning to be curious about that, rather than disappointed, is a real shift. The people who enjoy the class most are the ones who start treating the surprises as part of the fun.

## Small groups matter more than I expected

Eight is a number I picked partly for practical reasons, like wheel space and how many people I can watch at once. It turned out to matter in another way. A group that size becomes a little community by the third or fourth week. People compare notes, laugh at each other's collapsed pots, and cheer for the first clean pull. The learning is better when beginners can see that everyone else is struggling too.

## What I'd tell a beginner now

- **Expect to fail a lot.** Collapsed walls and off-center lumps are how everyone learns. They aren't signs you're bad at this.
- **Wedge your clay properly.** It's boring, and it saves you endless frustration later.
- **Slow down.** Almost every beginner mistake I see comes from rushing, usually with too much speed or pressure.
- **Keep your first pots.** Even the ugly ones. You'll be glad to see how far you've come.

## What's next

Teaching this class has made me a better potter, because explaining something simply makes you notice what you actually do. I'm looking forward to more Saturdays, more first mugs, and watching people learn to love the process as much as the result.

If you've been curious about clay, come try it. You don't need experience. Just bring patience and a willingness to make a mess.
