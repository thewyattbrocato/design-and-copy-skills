# Third Fire Pottery Blog Post

## The Mug Dilemma: What a Year of Teaching Beginners Taught Me

When we launched Saturday morning pottery classes at Third Fire last spring, I thought I knew what to expect. I'd been throwing clay for fifteen years, had taught a workshop here and there, and felt pretty confident about guiding eight complete beginners through the fundamentals. Turns out, teaching is its own craft—and the first thing I learned was humbling: almost everything I'd assumed about how people learn pottery was wrong.

The most immediate surprise? Every single student, within the first fifteen minutes of their first class, asked about making a mug. Not a bowl. Not a pinch pot or a simple cylinder. A mug—with a handle, a nice weight, something functional and personal. I get it. A mug is deeply appealing. It's useful. It's the kind of object that makes you feel like you've accomplished something real, like pottery isn't just clay dust and frustration but an actual *thing* you can use for your morning coffee.

Here's what I didn't anticipate: the mug is also, by a significant margin, the hardest project for a beginner to pull off successfully.

It requires a centered, even-walled cylinder—which demands months of practice to do consistently. Then you add a handle, which is its own technical problem: it needs to be structurally sound, proportional, and attached securely. By the time you're adding a handle, half of beginners have already struggled through the cylinder part so much that they're exhausted. And that's before we talk about glazing, firing, and the thousand ways a mug can crack or slump in the kiln.

I realized by week three that I needed to change my teaching approach. Instead of discouraging people from mugs—which felt mean, and made them tune out—I started reframing the journey. I told them: "The mug is the goal, but the path to a good mug goes through everything else. You're not wasting time on bowls and cylinders. You're building the skills that will make your mug actually work."

That shift changed everything. Suddenly, the repetitive cylinder exercises weren't boring prerequisites. They were training. And people's whole relationship to the work transformed.

What struck me most over the past year was how much pottery is about permission—permission to fail, to take time, to make something ugly and learn from it. In our world of instant gratification, throwing clay is almost shocking in its refusal to cooperate with our timelines. You can't rush clay. You can't finesse it. You have to respect its properties, understand what it's telling you, and meet it halfway.

I watched people come in stressed from their week and leave calmer, even when their piece fell apart mid-throw. I watched someone who'd called themselves "not creative" spend eight weeks on a bowl and eventually create something genuinely beautiful. I watched a group that started as eight strangers become a community—people who showed up for each other, celebrated each other's firings, and gave each other honest feedback.

The other thing I learned: beginners teach you how to see clay again. My students asked questions about techniques I'd done on autopilot for years. Why do you wet your hands? Why does the clay stick to the bat? Why can't you just smooth out that crack? These questions forced me to articulate the *why* behind the process, and in doing that, I fell in love with pottery again in a different way.

By the time our first round of students had their work fired and glazed, we had a range of results. Some mugs didn't make it. Some bowls were perfect. Some pieces surprised everyone—including the person who made them. One student brought a photo of her two mugs (it took her two attempts) and they're genuinely lovely. Another student made a wonky little vessel that looks like nothing in particular, but she loves it, and that's what matters.

This year taught me that the best thing I can give beginners isn't techniques or finished pieces—it's time and permission. Permission to make things badly. Permission to be patient with the process. Permission to want that mug, but to understand that the real transformation isn't about the mug at all.

It's about what happens to you when you sit down at the wheel.
