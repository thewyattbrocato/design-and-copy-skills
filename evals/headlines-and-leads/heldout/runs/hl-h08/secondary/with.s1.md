# Everyone Wants to Make a Mug (And Why That Teaches You Everything)

When we launched Saturday classes at Third Fire Pottery last spring, I thought I'd prepared for every question a beginner might ask. I'd cleared space in the studio, organized clay, written out lesson plans. What I hadn't fully anticipated was how quickly I'd learn that teaching ceramics—real teaching, not just demonstrating technique—is its own craft entirely.

A year in, I've taught dozens of complete beginners, watched them transform lumps of clay into objects they made with their own hands, and learned as much about myself as they've learned about clay. But the single biggest lesson came from an unexpected place: the universal human desire to make a mug.

It happens every single session. Someone will walk in on their first Saturday, full of possibility and curiosity, and by the end of the first class when we've covered basic centering and opening, they'll ask: "So when can I make a mug?" They don't want to make a bowl. They don't dream of a vase or a planter. They want a mug—something functional, something they'll hold in their hands on an ordinary morning. I get it. A mug is intimate. It's purpose-built for human hands. It promises practicality wrapped in the thrill of having made something yourself.

This is where my learning began.

The mug is, in fact, the hardest thing to get right. I didn't fully appreciate this until I started teaching. A bowl forgives you. You can throw it off-center, and it's still a bowl. A planter with wonky walls? That's character. But a mug has specifications. The walls need to be even so it doesn't tip. The base needs to be thick enough to hold weight but thin enough to trim without disaster. The handle—oh, the handle—is its own negotiation between physics and desire.

I've watched smart, capable people get genuinely frustrated by mugs. And I've watched something shift in them when I explain that this frustration isn't a personal failing. It's just that they've chosen the hardest apprenticeship project without realizing it. There's something oddly beautiful about giving people permission to struggle.

What's become clear is that beginners don't just want to make a mug. They want to skip ahead. They want the satisfaction without the foundation. And that, it turns out, is the real thing I'm teaching.

Throwing clay is about learning to surrender to what the material wants while also insisting on what you want. You have to be attentive—to the clay's moisture, its centering, the angle of your hands. But you also have to commit, to make a decision and follow through even when you're uncertain. Most people in their first year of pottery are far better at one than the other. The beginners who progress fastest aren't the ones with the steadiest hands—they're the ones who can hold both things at once.

I've also learned that the studio needs to feel safe for failure. When someone pulls a collapsed mug off the wheel in pieces, I need them to understand that this is data, not disaster. This is how you know what to adjust. Over a year, I've seen the regulars who stuck with Saturday classes develop a kind of clay-based resilience. They come back. They try again. They're building something that extends far beyond the pottery wheel.

The other thing I underestimated was community. Eight people, same time every Saturday, all of them nervous and hopeful and making ridiculous mistakes together. By month three, they're helping each other troubleshoot. By month six, they're genuinely rooting for each other's progress. They've made little clay pinch pots to hold their wedding rings or their car keys, bowls they use for actual food, and yes—some of them—mugs that actually hold coffee.

What I've learned by teaching ceramics is that people want to make things. Not to buy things, but to make them. There's something in that impulse that's worth protecting and encouraging. It's vulnerable work. Every piece is a small autobiography of your attention and your effort.

So yes, when someone asks about making a mug on week two, I smile. I explain that we'll get there. And I'm grateful for the reminder of why they came—not to master pottery, but to make something, to change clay into an object that bears their fingerprints. That's the real practice. Everything else is just technique.
