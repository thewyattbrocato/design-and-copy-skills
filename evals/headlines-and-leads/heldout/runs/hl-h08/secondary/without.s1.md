# The Mug Paradox: What a Year of Teaching Pottery to Beginners Taught Me

When we launched Saturday classes at Third Fire last spring, I thought I knew what to expect. I'd been working with clay for years, and teaching seemed like a natural next step. What I didn't anticipate was how much the beginner mind would teach me—especially about patience, expectation, and the mysterious gravitational pull of the humble mug.

Within the first few weeks, a pattern emerged so consistent it felt like a law of physics: almost every student's first words were some variation of "I want to make a mug." Not a bowl. Not a simple pinch pot. A mug. Something functional, glazed, ready to hold their morning coffee. I'd nod and smile, and silently prepare myself for one of the hardest conversations I'd have all semester.

Here's what I've learned: the mug is deceptively complicated. It requires even wall thickness, a belly that won't slump in the kiln, a foot that sits flat, a handle that's both structurally sound and comfortable to hold, and a rim that doesn't catch on teeth. It's a masterclass in multiple techniques executed at different stages, with dozens of opportunities to fail in ways that range from the subtle to the catastrophic. I've seen countless mugs crack during drying because the wall thickness varied by a millimeter. I've watched promising mugs collapse under the weight of a poorly attached handle. I've glazed beautiful forms that emerged from the kiln with a base so uneven they'd rock on a table like a seesaw.

Yet I'd never want to discourage someone from trying. So instead, I've learned to redirect. We spend those first weeks on foundations: centering, opening, throwing basic symmetrical forms. We make bowls and cylinders, experimenting with how clay moves under our hands. We fail, repeatedly, in small ways that are easier to recover from. And then, usually around week four or five, something shifts. The hand memory develops. The clay starts to listen. And that's when students are ready—really ready—to tackle the mug.

What's surprised me most is how this mirrors something deeper about learning clay work. Beginners often want the end result before they've earned the process. They show up with a vision of what they'll make, and it's frequently more ambitious than their current skill level allows. Part of my job has become helping them see that the "detour" through simpler forms isn't a delay—it's the actual journey. The mug they make in week twelve, after building foundational skill, will be infinitely better than the one they'd have attempted in week two.

I've also learned that beginners are more resourceful and resilient than I expected. Someone will inevitably ask, "What if I just...?" and propose some unconventional fix. Half the time it won't work. A quarter of the time it creates interesting failure that teaches something. And occasionally, it actually works, and I learn something new about the material. I've stopped being the authority in the room and started being more of a guide who occasionally gets surprised.

Teaching has made me more aware of my own assumptions about clay. When you've been throwing for years, certain things become invisible—why you center the way you do, why you open at a particular angle, how you know when the wall is thin enough. Beginners ask "why?" constantly, and it's forced me to break down intuitive knowledge into actual steps and reasons. Sometimes I realize I've been doing something a certain way out of habit rather than necessity. That's genuinely valuable.

The groups themselves have been wonderful. Eight people learning together creates this specific kind of camaraderie. There's enough of you that energy builds, but small enough that everyone gets attention. I see friendships form over shared clay disasters. People cheer each other on through the kiln openings. Someone always brings coffee.

As I head into year two of these classes, I'm more convinced than ever that teaching beginners is about managing not just skill, but expectations. It's about honoring what someone wants to make while gently insisting they earn it. And it's about recognizing that the mug—that impossible, glorious mug—isn't really the point at all. It's what happens to your hands and your patience and your relationship with failure along the way.

We're already accepting sign-ups for the spring session. The first person who signs up will probably ask about making mugs.

I'll smile and say, "Let me teach you how first."
