**Openers**

B. *Quote-led:* "'We stopped guessing which projector belongs to which room,' says Dana R., facilities lead at a mid-sized public school district in the Midwest."

**My pick: #3 with opener A**

Use the quote as the headline, with this subhead: *"A Midwest school district's facilities team on labeling classroom equipment before summer inventory."*

It's the only line in the notes that sounds like a person, and it names a problem any facilities reader has had. The subhead says who and what, so the headline doesn't have to. Opener A then gives the scale, so the quote isn't repeated. Put the full quote in the second paragraph, credited to Dana R.

**The test: #3 against #1**

These two differ in the way that teaches you something. One leads with a person's voice. The other leads with the numbers (five people, 41 classrooms, about two weeks). If you test two numbers-led headlines against each other, you only learn about wording.

1. **Change only the headline.** Version A gets #3 and Version B gets #1. Both versions use the same subhead, opener, photo, and position in the newsletter. The subhead above works under either headline.
2. **Split randomly.** Use your email tool's random split, not one by customer type or list order. Send both at the same time.
3. **Pick the success measure before you send.** Use clicks through to the full story, or read depth if the story is on the web. Don't use opens, because the headline sits inside the email and can't affect opens.
4. **Decide what counts as a tie.** With a small list, a gap of a few clicks is noise. Write down the margin you'd need to see before you send. If neither version clears it, treat the test as a tie and don't over-read it.
5. **Write down the lesson in advance.** If A wins, readers respond to a peer's voice, so lead future customer stories with quotes. If B wins, they respond to concrete scale, so lead with results. Apply the lesson to the next customer story, not just this one.
