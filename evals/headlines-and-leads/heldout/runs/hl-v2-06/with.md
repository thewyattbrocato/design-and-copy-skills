**Headlines**

1. "We Stopped Guessing Which Projector Belongs to Which Room" *(Dana R.'s own words)*
2. 41 Classrooms, Five People, About Two Weeks: How One District Labeled It All *(the numbers)*
3. From Masking Tape and a Spreadsheet to Every Classroom Labeled *(old way vs. new)*
4. How Do You Label Every Classroom's Equipment Before Summer Inventory? *(the reader's question)*
5. A Five-Person Facilities Team Labeled 41 Classrooms Last June *(plain news)*
6. How Dana R.'s Facilities Team Labeled 41 Classrooms with Tagwren *(named person and product)*
7. Which Projector Goes in Which Room? One District Stopped Guessing *(problem, then change)*
8. Inside a Two-Week Push to Label 41 Classrooms *(process)*
9. The Masking-Tape Era Is Over for One Midwest School District *(drops the number. It claims the tape is gone, which the notes don't say, so check with Dana R. or cut it.)*

**Opening sentences**

- **A (quote):** "We stopped guessing which projector belongs to which room," says Dana R., facilities lead at a mid-sized Midwest public school district.
- **B (the job):** Last June, a five-person facilities team had about two weeks to label equipment in 41 classrooms.
- **C (the old way):** Before last June, this district's classroom equipment records lived in a spreadsheet and on handwritten masking-tape labels.
- **D (plain summary):** A mid-sized Midwest school district labeled equipment in 41 classrooms last June, using Tagwren and a five-person team.

**My pick**

Headline 1, with the subhead "A five-person facilities team labeled equipment in 41 classrooms in about two weeks." Open with C.

- Your readers already own or use label printers, and the quote names a problem they'll recognize in their own words.
- It makes sense on its own.
- The subhead supplies the scale, so the headline doesn't need to.
- C avoids repeating the quote and sets up the contrast the story is built on.

**The test**

Test headline 1 (quote) against headline 2 (numbers). They differ on one question: does your audience respond more to a human voice or to scale?

*Setup*
- Split the list randomly. Both versions go out at the same time.
- Change only the headline. Keep the newsletter subject line, subhead, opening, body, images and call to action identical.
- Because the subhead is shared, use a neutral one such as "How a school district's facilities team labeled its classroom equipment with Tagwren." Otherwise the quote version gets the numbers in its subhead and the comparison is muddied.
- Write your prediction down before sending, for example "the quote will get more people to read to the end." That way you learn something even if you're wrong.

*What to measure*
- Newsletter open rate is no use here, because the headline is inside the issue. It can't affect opens.
- The primary measure is the click-through on the story's call to action (a Tagwren page, a demo, a "label your own inventory" link). That is the nearest thing to a real interest signal.
- Secondary measures are clicks into the full story, if it's a teaser, and scroll depth or time on page.
- Ignore any difference in total clicks that isn't on the call to action.

*Reading the result*
- Decide your threshold before you send. With a small list, a gap of a few clicks is noise, so treat it as a tie.
- If it's a tie, pick the quote version, since it's cheaper to reuse in other places.
- If the numbers win clearly, your readers want proof of scale. Lead with figures in future stories.
- If the quote wins clearly, they want a peer's voice. Lead with customer quotes.
- Either way, write down the result and test the same question on the next story's headline. One test teaches little, but two that agree are a pattern.

Before publishing, confirm the exact wording of the quote with Dana R. and the attribution line, "Dana R., facilities lead."
