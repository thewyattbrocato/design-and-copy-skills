## Opening options

**A. Quote first.** "We stopped guessing which projector belongs to which room." That's how Dana R., facilities lead at a mid-sized Midwest school district, describes what changed last June.

**B. The old way.** Before last June, this district's equipment inventory lived in two places: a spreadsheet, and strips of handwritten masking tape stuck to the equipment itself.

**C. Numbers first.** Five people. Forty-one classrooms. About two weeks. Last June, one school district's facilities team set out to label the equipment in every one of those rooms with Tagwren.

**D. Question.** Which projector belongs to which room? For one district's facilities team, answering that used to mean checking a spreadsheet and reading masking tape.

## My pick

I'd go with **headline 1 and opening B or C**. Dana's line is the most specific and human thing in your notes. It also names the problem in terms any facilities person will recognize. Because the headline already uses the quote, don't repeat it in the opening. B sets up the before state. C gives the scale quickly. I lean B, since the contrast with tape and a spreadsheet is what the quote pays off.

## How to run the test

**Compare headline 1 (quote) against headline 3 (numbers).** They differ in one way: a human voice and a problem versus concrete scale. Whichever wins tells you something you can reuse in future issues. If you tested headline 1 against headline 2, you'd learn almost nothing, because they share the quote.

**Change only the headline.** Use the same opening, same photo, same placement and same send time in both versions. If anything else differs, you won't know what caused the result.

**Split randomly.** Let your email tool assign recipients, not you. Don't split by customer type or list order.

**Pick the metric before you send.** Where the headline appears decides what you can measure:
- If it's the **email subject line**, use open rate.
- If it's the **story headline inside the newsletter**, open rate tells you nothing. Use clicks through to the full story, or read time if you have it.

**Write down your guess first,** for example "the quote will win on clicks." That keeps you from explaining away the result afterward.

**Be honest about size.** With a small list, a gap of a few percentage points can easily be noise. Treat a small gap as a tie and a large one as a hint. Either way, repeat the same kind of test next quarter before you call it a rule.

**Hold the loser back.** Don't use the losing headline elsewhere, such as on social or the website, until you've read the results. Otherwise the two groups may see both versions.
