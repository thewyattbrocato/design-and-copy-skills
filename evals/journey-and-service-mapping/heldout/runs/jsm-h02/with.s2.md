**Straight answer: it's not ready for a VP.** It's a tidy template, but it doesn't show anything your team learned, and it doesn't lead to a decision.

**What works**
- It covers the whole path from first ad to referral, which is the right span.
- It focuses on one person, which is the right call.
- It's short and easy to read.

**What will hurt you in the room, most serious first**

1. **Every stage is a happy face.** Curious, interested, excited, happy, satisfied, loyal, delighted. A VP will see right away that no customer's experience looks like that. Nothing goes wrong anywhere. Meal-kit businesses live or die on skipped weeks, cancellations, late or damaged boxes, and the "what do I cook tonight" decision. A map with no friction tells the VP there's nothing to fix, which undercuts the "Opportunities" line at the bottom.

2. **No evidence is shown.** I can't tell whether Brenda comes from interviews, survey data, or a brainstorm. "34, marketing manager" reads as invented unless it's sourced. There's also no status line saying whether this is a draft or built from research. The first question you'll get is "how do we know this?"

3. **The stages are your funnel, not Brenda's steps.** Awareness through Advocacy describes what the company wants. A map from Brenda's side would use steps like "decide if it's worth the price," "find time to cook on a Tuesday," or "decide whether to skip next week." Those steps are where retention is won or lost. Usage and Retention in particular are really a weekly loop, not two stages in a line.

4. **There's only one row.** You have stage, action, and feeling. There are no touchpoints beyond a channel name, no pain points, and no questions Brenda is asking. Nothing shows what your teams do in response, so no team can see where it owns a problem.

5. **The opportunities attach to nothing.** "Improve personalization, enhance the app, increase engagement" would fit any subscription business. None of them points to a moment on the map, and there's no owner, measure, or date. The VP can't decide anything from them.

6. **No moments are marked.** All seven stages look equally important. The map should flag one or two where the experience probably breaks, such as the first box's meal-versus-time reality or the skip/cancel decision, and say why.

**How I'd fix it before next week**
- Add a frame line: Brenda, the start and end of the story, and the decision this map informs (for example, where to invest in retention).
- Add a status line. If it's assumption-based, say "draft from team assumptions, to be checked against customer data."
- Rename the stages from Brenda's perspective, and show Usage → Retention as a loop.
- Replace the emoji with honest words, mixed where needed ("excited, then anxious about the price"). Don't plot a curve unless you've measured it.
- Add rows for what she's thinking, what goes wrong, and the specific touchpoint.
- Mark two moments, name the evidence or the check that would confirm each, and turn the opportunities into 2–3 hypotheses. Each should name a moment, an owner, a measure, and a date.

You don't need new research to do this. Your support tickets, cancellation reasons, and skip-rate data probably cover most of it, and anything you can't source can be labeled as an assumption.
