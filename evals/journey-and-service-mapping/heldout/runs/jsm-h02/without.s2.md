Straight answer: it's a decent skeleton, but I wouldn't present it to a VP as it stands. The biggest problem is that it reads as a best-case story, not a journey map.

**What's wrong**

1. **Every stage is a smiley face.** Curious, Interested, Excited, Happy, Satisfied, Loyal, Delighted is a steady climb with no dips. A VP will notice that right away. A journey map is useful because it shows where customers struggle, and this one shows none. Meal kits are known for high churn, and this map has no churn.

2. **Nothing in it is a problem to solve.** Where does Brenda hesitate, get annoyed, or drop off? Some likely candidates:
   - Sticker shock at checkout, or promo pricing that expires.
   - A box that arrives late, or when she's not home.
   - Packaging waste.
   - Recipes that take longer than the stated time, which matters a lot for someone short on time.
   - Weeks she has to skip or pause.
   - Cancelling.

3. **The stages describe what you do, not what she does.** "Gets emails and offers" is your action. What Brenda does is something like skipping weeks, ignoring emails, or comparing offers from competitors. The same goes for "Visits website" and "Picks a plan."

4. **Retention and Advocacy are assumed.** "Loyal" and "Delighted" are outcomes you want, not things you've shown happen. The weakest point in the journey is probably weeks 3 to 8 after the first box, and the map skips it.

5. **The persona is thin.** "34, marketing manager, loves cooking, short on time" is a label, not a person. It also has a built-in tension that you could use: she loves cooking but has no time. Where does the product serve one and fail the other? The map never says.

6. **The opportunities are generic.** "Improve personalization, enhance the app, increase engagement" could be written for any subscription business. They aren't tied to a specific stage or pain point, and they have no metric, owner, or priority.

7. **There's no evidence.** I can't tell whether this comes from interviews, survey data, or support tickets, or whether it's assumed. A VP will ask.

**What to do before next week**

- Add rows for **actions, touchpoints, pain points, and emotion as a curve**, not a flat set of emojis. Make the curve dip in at least two or three places.
- Add a **"moment of truth"** stage or callout, such as the first cook, the first skipped week, or the first time she considers canceling.
- Back it with whatever data you have: churn by week, NPS, support ticket themes, reviews, and cancellation reasons. Even a few customer quotes help a lot.
- Rewrite the opportunities as specific bets, for example "Reduce week-4 churn by letting users swap recipes in one tap. Metric: week-4 retention." Tie each to the stage where it matters.
- Consider a second persona, or a lapsed-customer version of Brenda, to show the sad path.

**What's good**

The stage structure is standard and easy to follow, the persona is a sensible target, and the format is clean. You have a workable template. It just needs the real, messy content in it.
