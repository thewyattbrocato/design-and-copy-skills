**Short answer:** it's a clean, readable skeleton, but it isn't ready for a VP. It describes the journey you hope customers have, not one anyone has observed. A VP will ask "how do we know?" and nothing on the page answers that.

**What works**
- It covers the whole span from ad to referral, so nobody can say you skipped a stage.
- The persona is a single person, not a committee.
- It was clearly made in good faith and is easy to read.

**What's holding it back, by consequence**

1. **No framing and no status line.** The map doesn't say what decision it serves or what it's built from. If it comes from interviews, survey data or support tickets, say so. If it's a team brainstorm, say that instead. Presenting it as the latter is fine. Presenting it as the former is not.

2. **The emotion row is the biggest problem.** Seven phases, seven smiley faces, and the feeling only rises: curious, interested, excited, happy, satisfied, loyal, delighted. Real customers don't feel like that. A meal-kit customer might feel anxious at checkout (is this a subscription I can cancel?), overwhelmed by a box full of small bags, or frustrated when a 30-minute recipe takes 50. The VP will notice that nothing ever goes wrong, and that undermines the whole map.

3. **The phases are your funnel, not Brenda's steps.** Awareness, Consideration, Purchase, Retention and Advocacy are how the company sees her. Her own steps would be things like "Decide whether this is worth the money," "Work out what's in the box," "Cook on a weeknight," "Skip or keep a week." The skip-a-week and cancel decisions are probably where you lose customers, and they're missing entirely.

4. **The persona is thin and unsourced.** "34, marketing manager, loves cooking, short on time" is a stereotype unless it came from your customer data. Also, "loves cooking" and "short on time" pull against each other, and that tension is where the interesting insights are. The map never uses it.

5. **There's no company side.** No touchpoints, no pain points, and nothing about what your team does at each step: fulfillment, delivery windows, customer service. For a meal-kit business, most failures happen there: late boxes, missing ingredients, wasted food. A map with only the customer's feelings can't show those.

6. **The opportunities are generic.** "Improve personalization, enhance the app, increase engagement" could be pasted onto any subscription business. None of them points to a specific moment, and there's no owner, measure or date.

**How to fix it before next week**
- Add a framing line and a status line. For example: "Brenda, from first ad to her fourth box, to decide where to invest next quarter. Draft based on [your actual sources]."
- Rename the phases from her side, and add the weekly skip/keep/cancel decision.
- Replace the smileys with honest, mixed feelings. If you have no data, label them "hypothesis" and don't draw a line.
- Add rows for what goes wrong and where she touches your company.
- Mark one or two moments that matter, with a reason. My guesses are the first checkout and the first skip-or-cancel decision, but check those against your churn data.
- End with two or three hypotheses, each tied to a moment, with an owner, a measure and a date.
