**During the story, put up nothing: a black slide.** For a two-minute story to 400 people, the best visual is you. A screen with content on it makes people split their attention, and a blank one sends it back to you. In PowerPoint or Keynote, press B or insert a plain black slide so there's no glow or logo behind you. The dark screen also tells the room that something different is starting.

The one exception is a real photo from that night, like the flooded floor or the crew mid-move. If you have one and it's good, put it up full-bleed with no caption or title. Don't use stock flood imagery. The room will notice, and it undercuts a story that's true.

**The slide right after is the point of the talk, as a full-sentence claim.** For example:

> **The plans that work are the ones the front line can change without asking.**

Show it as large text on a plain background, with no bullets and no logo. Bring it up as you finish the last line of the story, then pause for a beat before you speak. The room has just felt the example, and this is where they get the claim it proves.

The next slide, or the next two, should answer what made that night possible. I don't know your details, so that part is yours. It might be what the crew was allowed to decide, or what you had or hadn't told them beforehand. Build it from what actually happened in Columbus so the claim keeps resting on the story.

**Timing:** minute 12 of 30 is a good spot for this. It's roughly where you've earned attention but still have time to build on the claim.
