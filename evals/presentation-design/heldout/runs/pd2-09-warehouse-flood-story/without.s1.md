**While you tell the story: almost nothing.** Use one full-bleed photo, or a plain dark slide, and keep it up for the whole two minutes.

- **Best option:** a real photo from that night, like the flooded floor, the night crew, or pallets and carts being moved. A real, slightly imperfect phone photo will land harder with 400 logistics people than stock imagery, because many of them have lived a night like it. No caption and no text.
- **If you have no photo:** go to black or a solid dark slide. A blank screen pulls the room toward you, and a two-minute story is the one place in a 30-minute talk where that's what you want.
- **Avoid:** bullets, a timeline, or "3,000 orders" in giant type while you speak. The audience will read it instead of listening, and you'll lose the beat when you say the number.

If you want one visual device, hold the number for the moment you say it. Cut to a slide with just **3,000** and then back to the photo or black. Don't leave it up the whole time.

**The slide right after: your thesis, in one sentence.** Something like:

> **The plans that work are the ones the front line can change without asking.**

Put it on screen right after the last line of the story, while the room is still feeling it. Say it aloud first, pause, then let the slide confirm it. Don't pre-load the point before the story. The story should earn the sentence.

Two things to check:

1. **Don't explain the story after it ends.** Go straight from "...and by morning, every order was at the other site" to the thesis slide. If you recap, you dilute it.
2. **Make the next slide after that a contrast.** For example, what the written plan said versus what the crew actually did. That turns the story into your framework (what to write down, what to leave open), and it gives the thesis slide something to introduce.
