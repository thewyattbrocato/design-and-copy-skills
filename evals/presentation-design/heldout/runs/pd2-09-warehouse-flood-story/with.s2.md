**During the story, put nothing on screen.** Use a plain black slide. Don't use a photo, a timeline, or "3,000 orders" in big type. For a story, the speaker is the show. A slide gives 400 people something to read instead of listening to you, and it competes with the part that carries the talk. A black slide also gives the room a visible change of mode at minute 12.

- Build it as an actual black slide in the deck, not the B key. The key is easy to hit twice or miss, and a slide advances with your clicker like any other.
- If the keynote is streamed or recorded, don't leave it black. Use one quiet slide tied to the moment, such as the warehouse name or a single plain photo of the building, so remote viewers aren't looking at nothing.
- If you have a real photo from that night and want to use it, make it a full-bleed single image with no text. Black is still the stronger default.

**The slide right after the story states the point as a claim, as its title:**

> **The plans that work are the ones the front line can change without asking.**

Put one visual under it, and make it your own fact from the night, not a stock graphic. The best candidate is the single decision the crew made without approval, for example "Moved to the other site by hand, no sign-off" if that's what happened. I don't know the details, so confirm the wording. Keep the slide under about 20 words. The audience just felt the story, and this slide names what they felt.

It also works as your takeaway slide. If you stated the thesis in your opening, this is the first time the room sees it written, with proof behind it. The next supporting point (what makes a plan changeable by the front line) then builds on it.

I assumed the story sits in the middle of the talk and that your opening already previewed the thesis. If the thesis only appears after the story, the slide above is still right, but your minute-12 setup line has to do more work.
