**During the story:** put up one full-bleed image with no text and no logo, and leave it there for the whole two minutes. The best image is a real photo from that night: the flooded dock, or the night crew with carts and pallets. A phone photo is fine, and it's better than a polished one. If you have no photo, use a plain black slide. Don't build the story in steps or put bullets on screen. Four hundred people can either read or listen, and you want them listening.

If you want one beat of emphasis, you could fade in a single "3,000" late in the story, at the moment you say the number. Add it only if you'd say it anyway, so the screen reinforces your line instead of competing with it.

**The slide right after:** state the lesson as a short sentence, in large type, on a plain background. For example:

> **Nobody approved the plan that saved those orders.**

Or, if you'd rather make the claim directly:

> **The plans that work are the ones the front line can change without asking.**

I'd lean toward the first. It's concrete and still tied to the story, and it makes the audience reach the principle themselves. You can then say the thesis out loud while it's on screen. If the thesis is the title of your whole talk, it may land harder as a spoken line, so keep the screen to the first version.

**Why this order works:**
- The photo keeps the story human and physical, and keeps people from reading ahead.
- The sentence slide comes while the audience is still in the story. That's when the principle hits hardest.
- The sentence is short enough to take in at a glance, which lets you pause for a beat instead of talking over a dense slide.

One thing to decide: the slide after that should move from the story to what the audience can do about it. Something like "What did the crew have that most front lines don't?" gives you a natural bridge into the rest of the talk. I can help draft that slide or tighten the two-minute story if you'd like.
