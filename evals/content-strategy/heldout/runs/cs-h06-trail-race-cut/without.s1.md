Runners are your main audience, but I'd check that against your own data before relying on it.

## Who the site is for

- **Runners:** They pay entry fees and make up the mailing list. The site should be built for them, and the homepage should answer "what's the next race and how do I sign up?"
- **Volunteers:** They're your scarcest resource, since the races can't happen without them. They need one clear page that's easy to find from every race.
- **Sponsors and landowners/permit people:** You probably reach both through personal contact, not by browsing. For them the site is proof you're legitimate and well run, so it can be small and polished.

To verify this, look at your analytics (or just ask yourselves) which pages get traffic and where registrations come from. That will tell you whether the blog is bringing in runners or just sitting there.

## What to have

1. **Homepage:** The next race, its date, a Register button, a mailing list signup, and a two-line description of who you are.
2. **One page per race:** Registration, course and elevation, schedule, parking and logistics, cutoffs, FAQ, past results and photos. Keep the same URL every year and update it, instead of making new pages.
3. **Volunteer:** One page with the signup, what the roles are, and what volunteers get. Link it from every race page.
4. **Sponsors:** One page covering who you are, your audience size (mailing list, entrants), what sponsors get, past sponsor logos, and a contact. Also make it a one-page PDF you can send in emails.
5. **About, including the land:** Your story, who you are, and a short section thanking landowners and explaining trail rules and closures. Keep it short and respectful.
6. **Mailing list signup:** In the header or footer of every page.

## Fold together or drop

- **Land and permits page:** Fold the public-facing parts (thanks, trail etiquette, Leave No Trace) into About. Keep actual permit paperwork out of the site and handle it by email or a shared folder.
- **Past races and results:** Fold into each race's page.
- **The blog:** This is likely your biggest time sink. Generic training tips compete with huge sites and probably do little for you.
  - Keep the 5–10 posts that get real traffic, or that are specific to your courses.
  - Group them under a "Training" section, and move it out of the main navigation.
  - Redirect or archive the rest, and don't just delete them. Check traffic first.
  - Stop writing new generic tips. If you write anything, make it course previews, race recaps, and volunteer stories. Those can double as newsletter content.

## Navigation

Races · Volunteer · Sponsors · About · Newsletter signup. Training goes in the footer or as a sub-page.

## What to do first (about 5 hours a week)

- **Week 1:** Look at the analytics and write down which pages matter. Rebuild the homepage around the next race.
- **Week 2:** Make one race page the template, then copy it for the other two. Add the mailing list signup site-wide.
- **Week 3:** Consolidate volunteer signup into one page and link it from the race pages.
- **Week 4:** Fold the land page into About, then write the sponsor page and its PDF.
- **Later:** Prune the blog. Do it last because it's the most tedious and the least urgent.

It's October, which is probably the off-season. That makes this a good time to finish before registration opens for next year.
