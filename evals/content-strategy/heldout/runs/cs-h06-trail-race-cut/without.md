Your guess is probably right: runners matter most, because entry fees pay for everything else. But the audiences don't need equal prominence, and they don't need equal amounts of your time.

## What each audience needs

| Audience | What the site must do | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Runners | Get them to a race page and register with no questions left | Most of the site |
| Mailing list | Turns one-time visitors into repeat entrants | Prominent signup on every page |
| Volunteers | Show the dates, roles and a signup link | One page, linked from each race page |
| Sponsors | Show who you are, your reach and how to contact you | One page, in the footer or nav |
| Landowners and permit people | Show you're responsible, careful and legitimate | Low traffic, high stakes |

Landowners and permit officials rarely browse the site. They will Google you, though, so the land and permits page works as a credibility page. Rename it something like "Land, trails & stewardship" and move it under About. Runners will also read it as a sign you're well run. Keep it short and current: who you work with, leave-no-trace practices, trail work days, and a contact for land managers. Don't let it grow.

## Proposed structure

1. **Home page:** the next race, with date, a register button and the mailing list signup. Other races sit below it.
2. **Races:** one page per race, all built from the same template (see below).
3. **Volunteer:** one page with a general signup. Each race page links to it with that race's roles.
4. **Sponsors:** one page, plus a downloadable one-pager.
5. **About:** your story, the land and stewardship section, and contact.
6. **Training blog:** much smaller (see below).

## Race page template

Each race page should have these, in this order:
- Date, location, distances, price and a register button
- Course map and elevation
- Cutoffs and aid stations
- Parking and logistics
- Refund and transfer policy
- Last year's results
- FAQ
- Volunteer link

Most of your support emails probably come from missing items on this list.

## The blog

A big training-tips blog is the most likely place to be wasting time. Check your analytics before deciding anything:
- Keep and refresh the 10–15 posts that get real search traffic. Put a mailing list signup and a "see our races" link on each.
- Merge overlapping posts, and delete or redirect the ones nobody reads.
- Stop publishing generic training tips. Generic advice competes with huge running sites. Write about things only you can: course previews, race recaps, volunteer stories, trail conditions. Those sell entries.

## What to do first (about 5 hours a week)

1. **Week 1:** Look at your analytics and registration data. See which pages get traffic, where entrants come from, and whether the blog brings in any registrations or list signups. This is also how you'll find out whether runners are really your top audience.
2. **Week 2:** Rebuild the home page and navigation around the next race. Add the list signup.
3. **Weeks 3–4:** Bring all three race pages up to the template.
4. **Weeks 5–6:** Do the blog audit. Cut, merge and redirect.
5. **Later:** Tighten the sponsor and volunteer pages and trim the land page.

It's October, so you're likely in the off-season. That's the best time to do this, before registration opens for next year.
