# Website plan: three races, about five hours a week

**The decision:** everything is equally prominent, so nothing stands out. Make the site mostly about the race pages and the mailing list. Fold, shrink or stop the rest.

## Who the site is for

1. **Runners deciding whether to enter** come first. I'm assuming entries are what pay for the races, and that's why they rank first.
2. **Volunteers** come second. You need them for each race, so they're a repeat audience.
3. **Sponsors** come third. They're a small number of people who mostly reach you by email.
4. **Landowners and permit people** come last. They probably don't browse the site. They want to see that you're a legitimate, careful operation, so the page needs to be correct and easy to find, not prominent. It stays regardless of traffic.

## What the site should have

| Content | Decision | Owner | Stale when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Home page**: next race, entry button, list signup | Rebuild around the next race | [Staff A] | A race opens, closes or sells out |
| **Race pages** (3, one shared template) | Keep, and make them the core | [Staff A] | Registration opens or closes, and the day after each race |
| **Mailing list signup** | Put it on every page | [Staff A] | Never, but test the form each season |
| **Volunteer page** | Keep as one hub. Put each race's open roles on that race's page. | [Staff B] | After each race, and when roles change |
| **Sponsor page** | Shrink to current sponsors, what you offer and a contact | [Staff B] | A sponsor joins or leaves, and before each race |
| **Land and permits** | Keep it as a section of an About page, linked from the footer | [Staff B] | A permit renews or a landowner changes, and the landowner reads the wording |
| **Blog** | Freeze, triage, then fold | whoever writes posts today | See below |

Each race page uses the same template. That way you only have to work out what a race page needs once.

- **Top of page:** date, location, distances, price, and a clear Enter button or status banner (open, closed or sold out)
- **Course and logistics:** course and elevation, start times, cutoffs, parking, packet pickup
- **Policies and FAQ:** refund and weather policy, FAQ, past results
- **Get involved:** a short volunteer box and the sponsor logos

## The blog

Don't delete posts by traffic alone. Low traffic can mean nobody can find a post.

1. **Stop publishing** new training posts now. Race news and recaps go in the mailing list.
2. **Pull a list** with these columns: URL, publish date, visits, which race it relates to, and whether other sites link to it.
3. **Keep** the few posts that help someone prepare for one of your specific races. Link them from that race page as "Prepare for this race."
4. **Merge** overlapping posts into one short Training page.
5. **Retire** the rest. Redirect any post that has outside links, and unpublish the others.

The blog owner decides which posts stay. Nobody else is asked to review them.

## What to do first

All of this fits in one or two weeks of your five hours.

1. **Hour 1:** Make the next race page correct and complete. If registration is open or about to open, this matters more than anything else on the list.
2. **Hours 2–3:** Reorder the navigation to Races, Volunteer, Sponsors, About. Put the signup form on the home page and in the footer.
3. **Hour 4:** Freeze the blog and pull the list above.
4. **Hour 5:** Name one owner per row of the table. Put a "last checked" date on the land and permits page.

After that, a rough split of your hours would be:

- **Most hours:** the race page for whichever race is next
- **Occasionally:** the volunteer and sponsor pages
- **Rarely:** the About page
- **Never:** new blog posts

**Two things to check:**
- **Whether runners really come first.** Look at where last race's entries came from (direct, mailing list, search or social). If most came through the list, it deserves even more of your hours.
- **The owner names.** I used [Staff A] and [Staff B] as placeholders because I don't know who does what. Swap in the real people.
