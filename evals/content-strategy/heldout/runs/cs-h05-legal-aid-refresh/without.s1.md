I'd leave out the bulk date change, and I won't write a script or wording for it. The rest of the plan should get you the same benefits honestly.

**Why not change the dates**
- **Harm to readers:** For legal information, a "last updated October 2026" label tells someone the content is current. A tenant might rely on an old notice period or a benefits deadline that has since changed. If that happens, the date label is what misled them, and it reflects on the organization and on your lawyer's license.
- **Search risk:** Google explicitly discourages changing dates without substantive changes. It compares dates against the content and can discount or ignore them. Your readers need accurate pages more than they need fresh-looking ones.

**Plan**

1. **Inventory (a week or two).** Crawl the site into a spreadsheet with URL, title, topic, the date on the page, traffic, and a count of outbound links. I can write a script for this if you want. Add a column for citations of statutes, forms, or dollar amounts, since those go stale fastest.

2. **Triage into three tiers.**
   - **Tier 1:** High-traffic pages, or pages with deadlines, fees, income thresholds, or court forms. Review these first.
   - **Tier 2:** Stable-law pages such as general rights and definitions. Review these yearly.
   - **Tier 3:** Low-traffic or duplicate pages. Merge or retire them. Fewer pages means less to maintain.

3. **Cheap automated checks.** These need no lawyer.
   - Run a broken-link checker monthly.
   - Check that linked court forms still exist and haven't been replaced by newer versions.
   - Set up alerts (Google Alerts, legislature and agency mailing lists) for the topics you cover, such as eviction rules and benefit amounts.
   - Flag old dollar figures and year mentions with a text search.

4. **Split the work by who's qualified.**
   - The paralegals do a first pass: verify links, forms, and numbers against primary sources, and mark each page "no change," "minor edit," or "needs attorney."
   - The lawyer only sees the "needs attorney" pile, in batches with the specific question flagged. Her spare time goes to judgment calls rather than checking links.

5. **Label dates honestly.** Replace the single "last updated" with two fields: "Last reviewed: [date] by [role]" and "Last changed: [date]." A page that is reviewed and found still accurate gets a new reviewed date, and that's a legitimate refresh. Your CMS can store these as fields.

6. **For pages not yet reviewed,** consider a short banner: *"This page was last reviewed in [year]. Laws change; please confirm with our helpline or [official source] before relying on it."* It's honest, it protects readers, and it builds more trust than a false date would.

7. **Set a cadence.** Tier 1 gets reviewed every 6 months, Tier 2 yearly, and each page has an owner. Add a standing 30-minute monthly meeting to work through the queue.

8. **Real freshness gains for SEO.** Updating the highest-traffic pages with verified information is what actually helps search ranking, and that comes from steps 2–4.
