I'd leave out the bulk date change. On legal pages, "last updated" tells readers the information is still accurate. If someone relies on a stale eviction-notice or benefits page that says "October 2026," they could miss a deadline or file the wrong form, and that harm lands on your clients and your organization. Google also says it ignores or demotes misleading dates, so the SEO gain is doubtful. A date that's later shown to be false would cost you more trust than an old one.

You can get the same trust and freshness benefit honestly, and faster than you might expect.

## Plan

**1. Inventory (about a week of your time)**
Export every page into a spreadsheet with these columns: URL, title, topic, current "last updated" date, monthly traffic, and the laws or forms it cites. Search Console or Analytics gives you traffic. If your CMS can export, a small script can pull the rest. I can write that if you tell me what you're on (WordPress, static files, something else).

**2. Triage into tiers**
- **Tier 1:** the top ~20% of pages by traffic, plus anything tied to deadlines, fees, or court forms. These get reviewed first.
- **Tier 2:** mid-traffic pages with stable law, such as general tenant rights.
- **Tier 3:** low-traffic or niche pages. Consider merging or retiring them, or adding a visible "may be out of date" banner until they're reviewed.

**3. Split the work so the lawyer's time goes only where it's needed**
- **Paralegals:** do the first pass using a checklist. Check that links work, forms are current versions, fees and deadlines match the court or agency site, and statute citations still resolve. They flag what changed and draft the edits.
- **Lawyer:** reviews only the flagged changes and Tier 1 pages. Batch this into one standing block, such as 2 hours every two weeks, instead of ad hoc requests.
- **You:** run automated checks for broken links and for form PDFs that changed at the source. Keep the spreadsheet current.

**4. Make dates honest, and make them visible**
Show two things on each page: **"Last reviewed: [date] by [staff/attorney review]"** and, where it applies, **"Last substantively changed: [date]."** Update "reviewed" only when someone has actually checked the page. As the reviews happen, readers will see fresh dates on the pages that matter most.

For pages not yet reviewed, you could use a neutral banner like this:
> *This page was last reviewed in [month, year]. Laws and court procedures change. If you're facing a deadline, please contact us or check [court/agency link] to confirm.*

That is honest and protects both you and your readers. It also tells people what to do.

**5. Set a recurring cycle**
- Tier 1: review every 6 months.
- Tier 2: review every 12 months.
- Tier 3: review every 18–24 months, or retire them.
- Add trigger-based reviews. When a law changes, a court updates its forms, or a fee schedule resets, use the "cites" column to find every affected page. Subscribing to your state legislature's and court system's update feeds is cheap and catches most of these.

**6. Track progress**
Report two numbers monthly: the % of Tier 1 pages reviewed in the last 6 months, and the count of overdue pages. A reviewed Tier 1 page also makes a good line in a grant report.
