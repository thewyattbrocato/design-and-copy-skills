# Content audit plan: six weeks, part-time

You don't need to read every page. Build a complete inventory with data, then spend human attention only where it changes a decision.

## Week 1: Set scope and build the inventory

- **Ask your director one question:** what is the audit for? A redesign, a cleanup, a CMS migration, and an accessibility push all lead to different priorities. Get the answer in writing, even as a two-line email.
- **Build one spreadsheet with a row per URL.** Export from your CMS if you can. Otherwise crawl the site with Screaming Frog (free up to 500 URLs) or a similar tool. Capture URL, title, page type, last-modified date, and the author or owner if the CMS records one.
- **Add traffic data.** Pull 12 months of pageviews per URL from Google Analytics, plus search impressions from Search Console. Join them to the inventory.
- **Sort pages into types:** event, branch, blog, service or program, policy, and other. The types will drive most of your decisions.

## Week 2: Triage with rules, not reading

Apply rules to the spreadsheet to sort the pages into groups.

- **Past events.** Events with dates in the past and little traffic are archive or delete candidates. This is probably your biggest bucket, and it needs almost no reading.
- **Blog.** Sort by date and traffic. Keep anything still getting search traffic, and flag the rest.
- **Branch pages.** There should be exactly 11, plus whatever sub-pages you intentionally keep. Anything else is a duplicate or a stray.
- **Zero-traffic pages** with no inbound links and an old modified date are likely removal candidates.
- **Duplicates.** Look for near-identical titles and use your crawler's duplicate detection.

At the end of this week you'll have a few hundred pages that need human judgment, and the rest will already have a provisional decision.

## Weeks 3–4: Review the judgment pages

With your helper, review the remaining pages against a short rubric. Write the rubric down so you both score consistently, and have each person score the same 15–20 pages first to compare. Score each page on:

1. **Accuracy:** hours, addresses, phone numbers, staff, and policies.
2. **Usefulness:** does anyone need this page, and is it findable?
3. **Quality:** broken links, outdated language, accessibility basics such as alt text and heading structure.
4. **Ownership:** who would maintain it if you kept it?

Assign each page one outcome: **Keep, Update, Merge, Archive, or Delete.** Give your helper the clearer buckets, like branch pages and the blog, and keep the ambiguous ones for yourself.

Branch pages are the most important check. Compare each one against the real hours and services, ideally by confirming with the branch manager. Wrong hours are the failure patrons actually feel.

## Week 5: Check the decisions and plan the work

- **Spot-check** about 10% of the decisions on each side, especially deletes. Before you delete anything, check what links to it, including newsletters, printed materials, and partner sites. Use redirects where there's a sensible replacement.
- **Show draft decisions to branch managers** for their pages. This catches mistakes and builds buy-in for the changes.
- **Estimate effort** for the Update and Merge groups (quick fix, an hour, or needs a subject expert).
- **Draft a governance proposal.** The site got messy because nobody owned it. Propose a short list: who can publish, who owns each page type, and a yearly review date. Without this, the site will be messy again in three years.

## Week 6: Write up and hand off

**Give your director:**

1. **A one-page summary:** total pages, how many fall into each outcome, the top findings (for example, "about 40% of pages are past events"), and the risks you found, such as wrong hours or broken links.
2. **A prioritized action plan:** what to do first, who does it, and the time needed. Separate quick wins (bulk-archiving old events) from larger projects.
3. **The governance proposal,** with a clear decision requested of the director.
4. **The spreadsheet** as an appendix. It's your evidence, and it gives you a starting point for ongoing maintenance.

Be explicit about what you did not check, such as PDFs or content on third-party platforms. A report that states its limits is more credible.

## Pitfalls to watch

- **Don't start deleting during the audit.** Decide first, then act in batches from the spreadsheet.
- **Low traffic doesn't always mean low value.** A page for a small branch or a niche service may matter to the few who use it.
- **Protect the helper's time.** Give them tasks with clear rules, not open-ended judgment calls.
