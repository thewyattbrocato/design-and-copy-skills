She's mostly wrong. For a line chart like this, you don't need to start the axis at zero.

**Why zero isn't required here**
- The zero-baseline rule applies to **bar charts**. Bars encode value as length, so a truncated axis exaggerates differences.
- A line chart encodes value as position and slope. The point is to show how the values change over time, not to compare each one to zero.
- Zero Celsius is an arbitrary point (the freezing point of water), not a natural "no temperature" baseline. A zero-based axis would squash a 21.4 to 24.9 range into a flat line and hide the day-to-day pattern.
- Your 20 to 26 range fits the data tightly without being extreme. It's a reasonable choice.

**Where she has a point**
A non-zero axis can make a small change look dramatic. A 3.5 °C swing in a lake over a month is real, but it's modest. To keep readers from overreading it:
- Label the axis clearly, with units (°C) and tick marks, so the range is obvious.
- Avoid a range much tighter than yours. 20 to 26 is fine, but 21 to 25 would make the swings look bigger.
- Consider a note or subtitle such as "Axis begins at 20 °C" if the audience is non-technical.
- If the chart is meant to convey that temperatures stayed stable, a wider range, such as 15 to 30, would support that message better.

So keep your chart, make sure the axis is clearly labeled, and you can tell your coworker that the zero-baseline rule is for bar charts.
