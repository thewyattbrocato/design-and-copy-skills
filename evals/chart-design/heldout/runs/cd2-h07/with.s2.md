**Recommendation: a 4×3 grid of small multiples, one panel per store, with the same weeks on every x-axis.**

Twelve lines in one frame would be unreadable. The 9,000 vs 400 range would also flatten your small stores into the floor of a shared axis. Small multiples let each store's shape show.

**Layout**
- **Grid:** 4 columns × 3 rows, wider than tall. Each panel is a plain line over weeks 1–52.
- **Y-axis scale:** Give each panel its own y-axis, because the question is about each store's shape and not about who is biggest. Put each store's peak value in its panel so the scale is still readable. Without that, a 400-visitor store would look as busy as a 9,000-visitor one.
- **Order:** Sort panels by annual total, largest first. This restores the size comparison that the independent axes hide. Put the annual total or peak in each panel label, for example "Downtown, peak 9,000".
- **X-axis:** Label only the bottom row, with ticks at weeks 1, 13, 26, 39 and 52, or with month initials. This keeps the slide clean.
- **Gridlines:** Use none, or a very faint line at the peak.

**Making the exceptions stand out**
- Draw all lines in muted gray or dark neutral. Use one accent color for the two stories.
- **Airport:** Accent line, with a small note at the July peak ("Peaks in July, not December").
- **Harbor Mall:** Accent line, with a note at week 30 ("Renovation closure from wk 30"). Shade or dash the post-week-30 stretch so it reads as a closure and not as a collapse in demand.
- **Christmas pattern:** Add a faint vertical marker at the pre-Christmas week (about week 51) in every panel. The ten typical stores then visibly share a peak, and the Airport's gap from the marker stands out.
- **Peak dot:** Optionally mark each panel's peak with a small dot.

**Title**
Use a title that states the finding, for example: *"10 of 12 stores peak the week before Christmas; Airport peaks in July and Harbor Mall dropped after its week-30 renovation."*

**Two decisions to make**
1. **Independent vs shared y-axes.** Independent axes are better for shape, which is what you've described. If someone on Monday cares about absolute size, the sort order and the labeled peaks cover most of that. A shared axis would make the small stores nearly flat. If you want one compromise, use a shared axis only within the top row.
2. **Harbor Mall.** Check that its weeks after 30 are real low counts and not missing data. If they are zeros from the closure, say so in the note. If they are partial, mark them as partial.

**Alternative:** If the slide has to answer "which stores are seasonal?" at a glance, a heatmap (stores as rows, weeks as columns, color for visitors indexed to each store's own peak) fits all 12 stores in one compact image. It's harder for a general audience to read than lines. I'd use it only as a backup.
