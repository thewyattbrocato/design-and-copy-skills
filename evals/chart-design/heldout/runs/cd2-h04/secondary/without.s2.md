It feels off because the chart makes a small change look large, and the title claims more than the data supports.

**What's distorting it**

- **The axis starts at 4,800, not zero.** Bar length is the thing readers compare, so the bars must start at zero. From the 4,800 baseline, Q1 is drawn as 20 units tall and Q4 as 150, so Q4 looks about 7.5 times larger. The real difference is 4,820 to 4,950, which is +2.7%.
- **The title oversells.** The data shows steady growth of roughly 40–45 tickets a quarter, about 0.9% per quarter. "Huge momentum!" is a claim the chart can't support. The title is also the first thing people read, so it sets the frame for everything after it.
- **The styling adds emphasis.** Bright green reads as "great" before anyone checks a number. The 3D effect distorts bar heights through perspective, so the bars are hard to compare even apart from the axis.
- **No data labels.** Readers can't check the values, and the axis is doing all the work. With labels, the chart would show the modest growth plainly, and people would trust it more.

**What I'd change**

1. **Start the bars at zero.** Keep the honest scale and show the real gap.
2. **Make the title state the finding.** For example: "Tickets resolved rose steadily, 4,820 → 4,950 (+2.7%) across four quarters."
3. **Remove the 3D and gradients.** Use flat bars in one neutral color, with one accent color if you want to highlight Q4.
4. **Label each bar** with its exact value.
5. **Add context the chart is missing.** Is resolution keeping up with ticket volume? Is the backlog shrinking? Without a denominator, "resolved" alone doesn't tell the team whether things are improving.
6. **Check whether Q4 is complete.** Today is October 8, so if this Q4 is the current year, its value may be partial or a forecast. Label it that way if so.

If the point is the trend rather than the size of each quarter, a line chart with a clearly labeled axis is also fine, since lines don't need a zero baseline. Even then, the title should describe the growth accurately.
