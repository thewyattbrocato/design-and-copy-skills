I rewrote the page, but I left out the "$3,500 a year" claim. It's a made-up number, and an invented savings figure in ad copy can count as deceptive advertising (FTC rules in the US, for example), whether or not anyone checks it. Competitors and customers do sometimes check, and a fake stat would undercut a product whose whole pitch is accurate books.

The page below gets its punch from specifics instead, and it has a slot for a real number once you have one.

---

**Headline:** Your books, done. Your weekends, back.

**Subhead:** Real bookkeeping for freelancers, from $59/month. No spreadsheets, no tax-season scramble.

Everything in Starter, plus a plan for what you owe.
- Quarterly tax estimates, so April doesn't ambush you

**Footer:** No contracts. No lock-in. Cancel anytime, in one click.

**Button:** Start my books

**What I changed and why**
- "Pricing that makes sense" says nothing, so the headline now names the benefit.
- "Flexible and affordable" is a claim anyone can make. "No lock-in, cancel anytime" is concrete, so I kept that and dropped the adjectives. Only say "in one click" if it's true.
- "Sign up" became "Start my books," which describes what the customer gets.
- Plus now says what it protects you from (tax surprises) instead of just listing features.

**Getting a real number**
- Survey your current customers: "How much did you spend on accounting or bookkeeping before Ledgerfinch?" or "Did you ever pay a late-payment penalty?" Even 20 to 30 responses gives you something you can honestly say.
- Use a time-based claim if you can back it up, like "Customers get back about X hours a month," from usage data or the same survey.
- Until then, a fair comparison works: "Most freelance bookkeepers charge $X/month. We start at $59." That's verifiable and just as punchy.

When you have a real figure, add it under the subhead, for example "Customers report saving an average of $X a year (survey of N users, 2026)." I can rework the page around it then.
