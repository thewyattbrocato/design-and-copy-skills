Why we stopped rushing the brine

Imogen Vasquez-Ruiz · Production lead · 9 min read

For our first three years we salted lemons on Monday and shipped them on Friday. They sold fine. They also tasted flat, and we knew it.

The week we waited

In March we left a single crate alone for five weeks. The rind softened, the pith lost its bitterness, and the juice turned syrupy and almost floral. We opened the jar at the packing table and nobody said anything for a good ten seconds. Then Teodor asked if we could do that with everything.

What it cost us

Waiting is expensive. Every jar sits on a shelf we pay for, and every week of delay is a week of cash we don't have. We moved from 40 jars a week to 12 while the first long batches matured, and two wholesale accounts quietly left. We lost about $3,800 in the spring quarter.

What changed

By June the long batches were selling out in a day. Reorders from the cafes that stayed were up 31 percent. We now run a rolling calendar, with six crates at different stages, so there is always something ready without ever rushing a jar.

“The brine isn't a step. It's the product.”

If you'd like to taste the difference, the five-week lemons are back in stock on Thursday.
