Nothing on the page is tied to dog groomers or bookkeeping. Each choice is the default one, and together they say "template." You could swap in a tax tool or a meditation app and not change a pixel.

## What's making it generic

1. **The signals are borrowed.** The "New: AI-powered" pill, the gradient headline, the glowing button, and the blobs all signal "tech startup" and nothing else. None of them tell a groomer anything about their day.
2. **"AI-powered" is a feature, not a benefit.** Every competitor says it. A groomer doesn't want AI. They want to stop doing receipts at 10pm.
3. **The cards are interchangeable.** Three identical cards with an icon tile, a bold title and two grey lines have no hierarchy. Nothing is the main point, so nothing gets read. The gradient icons are decoration and carry no information.
4. **Pricing repeats the feature layout.** Pricing is a different job (compare, pick, trust), but it gets the same visual treatment as features.
5. **Gradients and blobs add noise without meaning.** Purple-to-pink is the most common startup palette, which makes it the least distinctive. The blobs hold no content and don't relate to anything on the page.
6. **There's no evidence of the real world.** There are no dogs, groomers, appointments, tips, or product screenshots. The page has no point of view.

## What would make it yours

**Start with their vocabulary and problems.** Groomers deal with:
- Cash and Venmo tips
- Appointment no-shows and deposits
- Supply costs (shampoo, blades)
- Mileage, if they're mobile
- Quarterly estimated taxes as a sole proprietor
- Booth rent, if they rent a chair

Name these on the page. A headline like "Know what you actually made after the shampoo, the blades, and the no-shows" does more than any gradient.

**Show the product instead of describing it.** Use a real screenshot or a realistic mock of a day's ledger with entries like "Biscuit, full groom, $85 + $15 tip" and "Blade sharpening, −$22." Concrete details like that are what make a page feel specific.

**Pick a palette and type from the trade.** Look at groomer culture for ideas: salon-style warmth, clipper-guard colors, or a hand-painted shop sign. Choose one or two confident colors, drop the gradients, and use a typeface with some character for headlines. A flat, solid button is fine.

**Break the grid where content differs.**
- Lead with the one benefit that matters most, large, and make the rest smaller.
- Try a "a day in the life" section: morning bookings, midday tips, evening totals.
- Make pricing a plain comparison, or even one plan with a clear explanation, rather than three cards.

**Use real proof.** Quote a named groomer, ideally with their shop and a photo of them with a dog. One specific quote ("I found out I was losing $300 a month to no-shows") outperforms a row of logos.

**Cut the AI pill.** If AI does something useful, describe the result, such as "Snap a receipt, and it's categorized," in the feature copy. Don't badge it.

**Make one memorable move.** Pick a single distinctive element and commit to it, such as a paw-print-stamped receipt motif, an illustrated mascot, or a ledger that looks like a groomer's appointment book. Everything else can stay quiet.

A quick test: cover your logo and ask whether a groomer could tell this page was for them in five seconds. If not, add specificity until they can.
