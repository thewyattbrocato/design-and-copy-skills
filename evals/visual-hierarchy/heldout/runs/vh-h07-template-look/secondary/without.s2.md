The page looks generic because every choice is a default that a template would make, and the copy and layout say nothing about dog grooming. A visitor can't tell whether it's a bookkeeping tool, a project manager, or an AI note-taker. The decoration is the most visible symptom, but the bigger problem is that the page has no specific claim.

**What's making it feel generic**

- **The decorations are the SaaS starter kit.** The "New: AI-powered" pill, gradient headline, glowing button, and blobs signal "template" before anyone reads a word. Glow shadows and gradient text are the most recognizable markers.
- **Three identical cards, twice.** Icon tile, bold title, two grey lines is a layout that works for any feature list, so it tells the reader nothing about your product.
- **The copy is interchangeable.** If "AI-powered" is the headline hook, the page is selling the category, not the people you serve.
- **No real product or people.** There's no screenshot of a groomer's day, no real customer, no real number.

**What would make it ours**

1. **Start with the groomer's actual problem.** Bookkeeping for groomers is specific: tips paid in cash, deposits and no-show fees, multiple groomers splitting a chair rental, supplies bought at three different stores, sales tax on retail products but not services, and for mobile groomers, van mileage and fuel. Put one of these in the headline, e.g. "Tips, deposits, and van mileage, sorted before tax season." Concrete beats clever.
2. **Show the product.** Replace the abstract hero with a real screenshot of a booked day or a month-end summary with your actual categories. A groomer should recognize their own week in it.
3. **Let the sections have different shapes.** Instead of three equal cards, use one large feature for the thing you do best (say, the tip split or the mileage log), a short numbered list for "how it works," and a plain paragraph for the promise. Vary the rhythm down the page.
4. **Rethink pricing around how groomers charge.** Price per chair, per groomer, or per location, not three tiers with identical checkmarks. If it's a flat fee for solo groomers, say so plainly: "$X a month for one groomer, no per-transaction fees." Pricing that matches their business reads as "made for us."
5. **Use plain, specific language.** Write like a person who has been in a salon: "Your Saturday tips, split by who groomed the dog." Skip "seamless," "powerful," "effortlessly."
6. **Pick one visual idea from the trade and commit to it.** Think salon-appointment-book styling, a receipt or ledger motif, or the warm, practical look of a grooming shop's signage. Avoid paw-print icons and purple gradients, which are the first things a reader will associate with a generic tool.
7. **Cut decoration until something earns it.** Remove the blobs, the glow, and the gradient text first. If a shadow or color doesn't help someone find the button or read the page, drop it.

**Order I'd do it in:** rewrite the headline and subhead with a specific pain point, replace the hero visual with a real screenshot, then cut the decoration. Restructure the features and pricing last, since those depend on what you decide the product's core promise is.
