The page reads as generic because almost every choice is a default. The visual tokens (gradient pill, gradient headline, glowing button, blobs, identical icon cards) are the stock vocabulary of AI-startup templates, and the copy is probably generic too. Your product is specific, but the page doesn't show that anywhere. The fix is mostly about replacing the template with things only a dog-grooming bookkeeping tool would have.

**What's making it generic**

- **The decoration carries no meaning.** The gradients, glow, and blobs are there because they're common, not because they say anything about your customer. Remove them and you lose nothing.
- **The "New: AI-powered" pill leads with your tech, not their problem.** Most groomers care whether the tool saves them a Sunday night, not whether it's AI.
- **The sections are interchangeable.** Three cards, then three more cards. Each section could sit on any SaaS site.
- **The content could be about any vertical.** If you swapped "dog groomers" for "dentists," nothing would break. That's the core problem.

**What would make it feel like yours**

1. **Write the hero around a specific situation.** For example: "Tips, deposits, and shampoo receipts, reconciled before you've dried the last poodle." Use their words: mobile van, walk-in, no-show fee, tap-to-pay, tip split, cash drawer. Test every headline by asking whether a competitor could use it unchanged. If yes, rewrite it.
2. **Show the product, in their world.** Replace the abstract hero visual with a real screenshot or a ledger row that looks like their day: "Bella, full groom, $65, tip $12, Venmo." If you have no product shots yet, a hand-drawn or photographed grooming table with a clipboard will read as more human than a gradient.
3. **Drop the card grid for a sequence that matches their workflow.** Something like "Book → Groom → Get paid → Tax time," written as a short narrative with a different layout for each step. Or a plain table comparing "your shoebox" to "your books." Varied layouts signal a real product; uniform cards signal a template.
4. **Make pricing concrete.** Skip the three-tier "Most popular" pattern. Try one price framed as something they recognize: "Less than two baths a month." Or a simple per-chair rate. Specific numbers feel more trustworthy than tiers.
5. **Use proof from their world.** One named salon, a city, a number ("saved 4 hours at tax time"), and a photo if you can get one. A groomer quote beats a stock testimonial.
6. **Use a voice with some personality.** Write like someone who knows the trade. Short sentences, a little dry humor, and no "unlock," "supercharge," or "seamless."

**What I'd do first:** Rewrite the hero headline and subhead and replace the hero visual with a real screenshot. Then delete the gradients, glow, and blobs, and use one solid accent color. Those two changes alone will do more than any amount of spacing tweaks.
