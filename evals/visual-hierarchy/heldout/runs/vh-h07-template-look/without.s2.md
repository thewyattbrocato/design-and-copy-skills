# Why it feels generic

Nothing on the page is a bad choice. The problem is that every element is the default choice, and none of them says anything about dog groomers or bookkeeping.

- **The palette and effects are a template.** Purple-to-pink gradients, gradient text, a glowing button shadow, and blob backgrounds are the stock look of SaaS starter kits and AI-tool pages. They don't tie to your product or your customer.
- **"New: AI-powered" is the most interchangeable claim you could make.** Any product could put it in that pill. It also talks about your technology when your customer cares about their Tuesday.
- **The three-card row says "I filled in a template."** Identical cards with an icon tile, a bold title, and two grey lines give every point the same weight. Pricing repeats the pattern, so the whole page has one rhythm.
- **Gradient icon tiles are decoration.** They don't show the product, so they're a placeholder for the thing you should be showing.
- **The blobs are the same on every section.** They add no information and no personality, and they flatten the page.
- **Nothing on the page shows a groomer's world.** There are no dogs, appointments, tips, or supply receipts.

# What would make it yours

**1. Lead with the customer's real problem, in their words.**
Groomers deal with cash and Venmo tips, per-dog pricing, no-shows, deposits, and a shoebox of receipts for shampoo and blade sharpening. Your headline could be something like "Know what you actually made this week" or "Tips, deposits, and blade sharpening, sorted." Specific beats clever here.

**2. Show the product instead of describing it.**
Replace the hero's abstract centered layout with a real screenshot, or a mocked-up week of a groomer's books. Use real-looking entries like "Biscuit, full groom, $85 + $15 tip" and "Clipper blades, $42." One concrete UI beats three feature cards.

**3. Drop the AI pill, or make it concrete.**
If the AI is useful, say what it does: "Snap a receipt, we file it." Put that in the body next to a demo. Don't use it as a badge.

**4. Pick a palette from the trade, not the template.**
Choose one or two colors with a reason. For example, a warm cream background with a deep teal or tub-blue, and a single bright accent. Use flat color and drop the gradients and glow. A button needs contrast, not a halo.

**5. Give the type some character.**
Swap the default sans for a headline face with personality, such as a friendly serif or a chunky grotesque, paired with a plain body font. Type does more than any other element to make a page feel like someone made it.

**6. Break the card grid.**
- Turn the three features into three alternating rows, each with a screenshot or a small illustration of that feature in use.
- Or use one big "a day in the life" walkthrough from first appointment to end-of-week totals.
- Make pricing two or three plans that differ in layout. Highlight the one most solo groomers need, and write the plan names in your own voice, like "Solo," "Salon," and "Mobile van."

**7. Replace the blobs with something real.**
Use real photos of groomers and dogs if you can get them, or a simple custom illustration style. A paw print or comb motif is fine if you use it sparingly. Otherwise leave the whitespace empty.

**8. Add proof from actual groomers.**
A quote like "I found out I was losing money on doodles" from a named salon owner does more than any feature list. Include a number, such as hours saved at tax time.

**9. Write like a person.**
Short sentences, trade vocabulary (matting fees, add-ons, no-show deposits), and a bit of humor. The copy matters as much as the visuals.

# If you only do three things

1. Rewrite the headline around a real groomer problem.
2. Put a believable product screenshot in the hero.
3. Remove all gradients and blobs, and commit to one warm palette and one distinctive headline font.
