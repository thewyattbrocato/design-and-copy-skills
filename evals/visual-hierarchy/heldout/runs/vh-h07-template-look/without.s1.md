# Why it feels generic

**Nothing in it is about dog groomers.** You could swap in "AI-powered bookkeeping for plumbers" or "AI-powered notes for lawyers" and change only the nouns. A template feels generic when its content is interchangeable, and the visuals follow the same logic. Each element is a default someone picked so they wouldn't have to decide:

- **Purple-to-pink gradient, pill, gradient headline:** This is the standard "modern SaaS" palette. It says "software startup" and nothing about who it's for.
- **"New: AI-powered" pill:** It leads with the technology, not with what the groomer gets. Groomers don't want AI. They want to stop doing receipts at 10pm.
- **Glowing button shadow and blobs:** These are decoration with no meaning. They fill space without carrying any information.
- **Three identical cards with icon tile, bold title and two grey lines:** Every feature gets equal weight, so nothing is emphasized. The copy is probably abstract ("Smart invoicing"), so the structure and the words are both generic.
- **Three-card pricing:** This is the same pattern again. Repeating one layout makes the page read as a template.

# What would make it yours

**1. Start from the groomer's actual life.** Write down what's specific to their bookkeeping:
- Tips, often in cash, sometimes split between groomers
- Booth rent or commission splits
- Per-dog pricing that varies by breed and coat condition
- Supplies like shampoo, blades and the dryer
- Mileage, if they're mobile
- No-shows and deposits
- Quarterly estimated taxes for a self-employed person

Your headline and features should name these. "Know what you actually made after the Saturday poodle rush" beats any gradient.

**2. Make the hero show the product with real content.** Replace the centered pill, headline and button with a real screenshot or mock-up using believable data: "Biscuit, full groom, $85 + $15 tip" and "Blade sharpening, $18". Specific details make a page feel real.

**3. Pick a palette and type from the world of the customer.** Groomers' world is warm, tactile and a bit playful: towels, brushes, kennel tags, appointment books. Choose one or two colors you can defend (a deep teal with a warm cream background, say, and one accent) and drop the gradients. Use a typeface with some personality for headlines and a plain one for body text.

**4. Cut the AI pill.** If AI is a real benefit, say what it does ("Snap a receipt, it's categorized") in the feature copy. Use a plain statement in place of the pill.

**5. Break the three-card rhythm.** Give the one or two features that matter most real space, with a screenshot and a short story. Put minor ones in a simple list. Vary the layout from section to section.

**6. Replace blobs and glows with real imagery.** Use photos of actual grooming tables, or simple illustrations (a dog silhouette, a clipper). Even a few hand-drawn marks would carry more identity than all the blobs.

**7. Make pricing specific.** Two tiers named for the customer ("Solo groomer", "Shop with staff") with a short line on who each is for will work better than three generic cards. If there's a real recommended plan, make it visibly different instead of identical with a "Popular" badge.

**8. Write like a person who knows the trade.** A testimonial from a named groomer, a line like "Built with help from groomers in [city]", or a sample tax-time report will do more for trust than visual polish.
