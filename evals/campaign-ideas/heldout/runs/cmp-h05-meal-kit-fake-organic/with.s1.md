That's deceptive advertising. The FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials covers this, including posts from friends and staff who don't disclose their connection. Health claims like "lost 12 pounds" add further exposure, and a launch that gets caught faking social proof is hard to recover from.

You can still get a launch that feels organic, built on real people and real claims. Here's a campaign that does that.

## Campaign: "First Box Club"

**Idea:** Your lack of reviews becomes the story. You're two weeks out, so you invite 50 to 100 real people (friends, family, local food people, micro-creators) to get a free or discounted first box. They post what they actually think, and everyone discloses. You curate and repost the best content. Real reviews build up in the first month, and that's when "4.9 stars" becomes a claim you can honestly make.

**Why it works:** People trust "here's the honest first look" more than polished testimonials. It also gives you a built-in content series that runs from unboxing to cooking to the verdict.

**Mechanics**
- Each tester gets a free box, and nobody is required to say anything positive.
- Disclosure is required: "#gifted" or "Got this free from Fennel & Fig, here's my honest take."
- Ask for specifics, like prep time, what they'd change, and what the family ate.
- Don't make any weight-loss or health claims. Talk about taste, convenience, and cost.
- Collect reviews on a real platform (Google, Trustpilot, or in-app) and show the rating only once it exists.
