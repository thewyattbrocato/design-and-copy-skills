# Series concept: "What your books know that you don't"

**Angle:** Every post takes one number from a dental practice's financials and explains what it says about the practice. It's about reading the business, not saving tax. Your eight years of looking at dental books is the credential, and you don't need client stories because each post is built on how the numbers work.

**Format:** Each part opens with a question a dentist can ask about their own practice and ends with one thing to check this week.

1. **Production is not income.** The gap between production, adjusted production, collections, and the money that lands in the bank.
2. **"What should my overhead be?"** Why benchmark percentages mislead, and which categories to compare (lab, supplies, staff, occupancy).
3. **The hygiene department.** How to tell from the books whether hygiene is an engine or a quiet drag.
4. **Two jobs, one paycheck.** Separating what you earn as a clinician from what you earn as an owner, and why a practice that "feels profitable" may just be paying you well.
5. **The equipment purchase.** What a big purchase does to cash flow versus the P&L, and why the monthly payment is the number that matters.
6. **What your practice is worth.** Clean books as the document a buyer, lender, or associate reads first, and what makes them trust or discount it.

# Part 1: Production Isn't Income

*(Written to work as the monthly email. Cut it at the line marked ✂️ for a LinkedIn post.)*

**Subject: The number on your schedule isn't the number in your bank**

Ask a dentist how the practice did last month, and you'll usually hear a production number.

"We did 140."

That's a real number and a useful one. It's also not what the practice earned.

Production is the value of the work you did, at your full fee schedule. Collections are the money that came in. Between the two sits a gap, and in a dental practice that gap is bigger than in most businesses.

Here's an illustration with round numbers:

- Gross production: **$140,000**
- Insurance write-offs (the difference between your fee and what the PPO agreed to pay): **–$28,000**
- Adjusted production: **$112,000**
- Collections: **$108,000**

Same month, same patients, same work. The practice "did 140," and it collected 108. Rent, payroll, and the lab bill are paid from the 108.

None of this is a problem in itself. Write-offs are the cost of being in-network. What matters is whether you can see them.

**Why it matters**

1. **Write-offs aren't a fixed cost.** They move with your payer mix. If a patient base shifts toward one plan with a deep discount, production can rise while collections stay flat. The schedule looks busy, and the bank account doesn't agree.
2. **The gap between adjusted production and collections is a signal.** If you did $112,000 and collected $108,000, most of that $4,000 is probably timing. If the gap is there every month and keeps growing, money is sitting in receivables, or claims are being denied or aging out.
3. **Every ratio you use depends on which number you divide by.** Overhead at 60% of production and overhead at 60% of collections are very different practices.

✂️

**One thing to check this week**

Pull your last three months of reports from your practice management software and write down three numbers for each month: gross production, adjusted production, and collections.

Then calculate two things:

- Write-offs as a percentage of gross production
- Collections as a percentage of adjusted production

If the first is climbing, look at your payer mix. If the second is below roughly 98%, look at your receivables. Your software's name for the second number is probably "collection ratio," but the reports differ, so confirm which production figure it uses.
