**Yes, this is a dark pattern, and it's likely a legal problem too.** The sign-up is two taps, but cancelling takes four screens and a phone call during business hours. Regulators call that asymmetry "roach motel" design, and it's the main thing that gets subscription businesses in trouble.

## What's fine

- **A pause option.** Offering a free freeze is a legitimate retention offer, as long as it's one choice among equals and not a gate.
- **"Keep my membership" as a button.** Fine on its own.
- **Showing the member-retention stat**, if it's accurate and sourced. Some teams would still avoid it on this screen.
- **Asking why they're leaving**, if the question is optional.

## What isn't

1. **Cancel is hidden.** It's the last item under "Billing FAQs," in small grey text. Users shouldn't have to hunt for the cancel option.
2. **Visual interference.** A big green "Keep" button next to a small grey "Continue" is a textbook asymmetric choice. The designer is steering people, not presenting options.
3. **The reason dropdown is required.** A mandatory field before you can leave is friction with no benefit to the user.
4. **Cancelling requires a phone call, and only Mon–Fri 9–5.** This is the biggest problem. Many jurisdictions require online cancellation for online sign-ups. Phone-only with limited hours fails that test.
5. **No confirmation until the call.** The user has no record that anything happened, and the cancellation isn't real until a phone call. That's the kind of thing that turns into a chargeback or a regulator complaint.
6. **Guilt-tinged copy.** "Before you go... 3x more likely to hit their goals" plus "Sorry to see you go" is mild confirmshaming. It's not the worst offence, but it adds to the pattern.

## Legal exposure (verify with counsel)

- **California's Automatic Renewal Law** requires cancellation to be available online, and at least as easily as sign-up. Phone-only cancellation for an online signup likely violates it.
- **Other states** (e.g., New York, Minnesota) have similar automatic-renewal rules.
- **The EU** requires a cancellation button for online subscriptions under the amended Consumer Rights Directive, applying from June 2026.
- **The FTC's federal "click-to-cancel" rule** was vacated in 2025, but FTC Act Section 5 still applies to deceptive and unfair practices, and the FTC has pursued this kind of case under it.

## What to change

**Make cancelling as easy as signing up:**

- Put **"Cancel membership"** in the main Membership screen, not under FAQs.
- Make the cancel flow **one screen, online**:
  > Cancel your membership?
  > You'll keep access until [date]. [Cancel membership] [Keep membership]
  Both buttons the same size and style.

**Keep one retention offer, but make the exit equal:**

- "Pause instead? Free for up to 2 months." Buttons: **[Pause membership] [No thanks, continue cancelling]**. Same size, same style.

**Make the reason optional:**

- Dropdown with a "Prefer not to say" or skip option. Don't block cancellation on it.

**Confirm immediately:**

- Show a confirmation screen with the end date and what happens to billing and refunds.
- Send the confirmation email right away.
- If you want a phone line, offer it as an alternative ("Prefer to talk to us? Call…"), not as the only route.

**Clean up the copy:**

- Change "Continue" to "Continue to cancel" so the user knows where the button leads.
- Drop the "3x more likely" line, or make it factual, sourced, and neutral.
- Make "Sorry to see you go" plain: "We're sorry to see you go."

**Other things to check:**

- Whether cancellation takes effect immediately or at period end, and say which one.
- That billing actually stops, and that the user isn't charged again after the confirmation.

One practical test: count the taps to cancel and compare them to sign-up. The gap should be small, and the cancel path should need no phone call. If you'd like, I can draft the revised screen copy or a short note for the designer.
