Yes, this is a dark pattern. The core problem is that sign-up takes two taps online, but cancelling requires a phone call during business hours. That's the classic "roach motel", and it's also the part most likely to cause legal trouble.

## What's fine
- **A pause offer.** It's a real alternative, it's free, and "Pause" and "No thanks" are presented as equal buttons.
- **A retention message.** One is acceptable if it's honest and easy to skip.
- **Asking why they're leaving.** This is fine if it's optional.
- **Putting cancellation under Settings > Membership > Manage.** The location is reasonable. The problem is how it's presented.

## What isn't fine
1. **Step 1: the link is buried.** It's a small grey link, last in the list, under "Billing FAQs". Other actions on that screen are presumably more prominent.
2. **Step 2: the buttons are lopsided.** A big green "Keep" button sits next to a small grey "Continue" link. "Continue" is also vague, so it doesn't say that it leads to cancelling.
3. **Step 2: the statistic is misleading.** "3x more likely to hit their goals" is a correlation. People who were already motivated stay longer. If you can't substantiate it, it's a deceptive claim. Even if you can, it works as emotional pressure at the moment of decision.
4. **Steps 2 and 3: two interstitials in a row.** One retention screen is normal. Two stacked ones, before the real flow even starts, add friction on purpose.
5. **Step 4: the required dropdown.** Forcing an answer before someone can cancel is a gate.
6. **Step 4: the phone call.** This is the biggest problem. Phone-only cancellation after online sign-up, limited to Mon–Fri 9–5, means many working people can't cancel until their next billing cycle. It's the most clear-cut pattern here.
7. **Step 5: no confirmation until after the call.** Users have no proof of cancellation, and the app never says whether they're still enrolled. This leaves them exposed to disputes and extra charges.

## Legal exposure
This isn't legal advice, so have counsel check it. The FTC's "Click-to-Cancel" rule was vacated by a federal appeals court in 2025, but that didn't remove the underlying risk. ROSCA, the FTC's enforcement under Section 5, and state auto-renewal laws still apply. California, New York, and several other states require online cancellation when sign-up was online. The EU and UK have similar rules. Cancel flows like this one are a common target of FTC actions and class actions.

## What to change
1. **Make cancellation fully self-serve in the app.** Remove the phone step. Phone can stay as an optional alternative.
2. **Make the entry point visible.** Use a normal-weight, clearly labelled "Cancel membership" row, not a small grey link under the FAQs.
3. **Use one retention screen at most.** Combine the pause offer with any other offer. Give "Cancel anyway" the same size and weight as "Keep", and label it clearly.
4. **Cut or substantiate the statistic.** Replace it with something factual, like what they'll lose or what the pause option does.
5. **Make the reason question optional.** Add a "Skip" option.
6. **Send a confirmation immediately.** Include the effective date, the last billing date, and what access remains through the paid period.
7. **Test it.** Have someone who has never seen the flow try to cancel. If it takes more than about as many taps as sign-up, simplify it. A good target is roughly three to four taps to cancel, including one confirmation.

You can keep the retention goal. A single honest pause offer will probably save more members than the maze does, and it won't generate chargebacks or complaints.
