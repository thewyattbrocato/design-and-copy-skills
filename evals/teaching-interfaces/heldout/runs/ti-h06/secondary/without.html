One Feeding, One Cycle: How a Starter Moves Through the Day

After you feed a starter, it goes through a predictable arc. Knowing where it sits on that arc tells you when to mix your dough.

1. Feed

Fresh flour and water go in. The starter is at its baseline volume, and the yeast and bacteria start eating the new sugars.

2. Rise

Gas builds up and the starter grows, often doubling. Bubbles form throughout and the surface begins to dome.

3. Peak

Volume is at its highest. The surface is domed and full of bubbles, and the gluten network is strong and elastic.

4. Collapse

The bubbles pop and the starter sinks, often with a flat or slightly wet top. It is now more acidic and the gluten has weakened.

Why the timing matters for mixing.
  • Mix near the peak. A starter at peak has the most active yeast and the strongest rise, so the dough lifts well and holds its shape.
  • Mix a little before the peak if you want a gentler dough. It will still be lively, with a milder sour flavor.
  • Mixing after collapse weakens the dough. Acid has broken down the gluten, so the loaf may spread, feel slack, and taste sharper.
  • Warmer kitchens move faster. Cooler kitchens stretch the whole cycle, so check the starter rather than the clock.

Times are approximate. A 1:1:1 feed (starter, flour, water) peaks more slowly than a larger feed such as 1:5:5. Watch the starter itself: a domed top with plenty of bubbles means it is ready.
