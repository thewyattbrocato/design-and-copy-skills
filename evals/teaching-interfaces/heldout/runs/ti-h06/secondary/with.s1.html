How a sourdough starter cycles after you feed it

A starter is a live culture of wild yeast and bacteria. After a feeding, it grows, peaks, and falls back. Knowing where it is in that cycle tells you when to mix your dough.

1. Feed

You add fresh flour and water (and often discard part of the old starter first). The culture is diluted, so volume is at its lowest and activity restarts.

2. Rise

Yeast and bacteria wake up and multiply. They produce gas and acid, bubbles form, and the starter climbs steadily in the jar.

3. Peak

Gas production is at its highest and the starter is at its tallest, often domed with large bubbles on top. This is the best moment to mix dough for maximum lift.

4. Collapse

Gas escapes faster than it is made, so the dome sinks and the surface may look flat or grey. Acid builds up, flavor gets sharper, and rising power drops.

Why the timing matters when you mix dough

Mix your dough when the starter is at its peak, or just before. At that point it has the most leavening power and the gluten is still strong enough to hold gas.

Mixing early gives a slow, dense rise. Mixing after collapse gives a flat, gummy loaf with a sour taste that overpowers the bread.

Timing shifts with temperature. In a warm kitchen (about 75°F / 24°C) a fed starter often peaks in roughly 4 to 8 hours, and a cool kitchen can take much longer. Watch the jar, not the clock.

Tip: put a rubber band around the jar at feeding time. When the starter has doubled and the top is domed, you are at peak.
