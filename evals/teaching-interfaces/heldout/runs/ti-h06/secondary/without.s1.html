What your starter does after a feed

Feed your starter and it follows a predictable arc: it wakes up, rises, peaks, then falls. Where it is on that arc when you mix your dough decides how much lift the bread gets and how sour it tastes.

1. Feed 0 h

Fresh flour and water go in, usually 1:1:1 by weight with starter. The culture is diluted and hungry, and the yeast and bacteria start eating the new sugars.

For dough: too early to use. Little gas, little lift.

2. Rise about 1 to 5 h

Yeast makes CO₂ faster than bacteria make acid. Bubbles form throughout the jar and the top climbs the sides.

For dough: building strength. Usable for bakes that want lift, but not yet at full power.

3. Peak about 5 to 7 h

The starter is at its highest and domed on top, full of bubbles, and often floats when dropped in water. Gas production is strongest here.

For dough: the best window to mix. Maximum lift with balanced flavor.

4. Collapse about 7 h and on

Gas escapes faster than it forms. The top sinks, sometimes with liquid on it, and the smell turns sharp as acid builds up.

For dough: still usable and more sour, but the gluten weakens. The dough can slacken and proof flat.

When to mix

Mix when the starter has domed or is right at the top of its rise. Go by the jar, not the clock:

  • Domed top, with bubbles through the whole jar and a slight wobble when you tap it.
  • Optional float test: a small spoonful floats in water.
  • Mixing early gives a dense loaf. Mixing late gives a sourer, looser dough.

Times assume about 24 °C / 75 °F. A cool kitchen can take two to three times as long, and a warm one can finish in half the time. Your starter's timing will differ from this chart, so learn its rhythm by watching it.
