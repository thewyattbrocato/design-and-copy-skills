A day in the life of a sourdough starter

A starter is a jar of flour, water, and wild yeast and bacteria. After you feed it, it goes through a predictable cycle: it wakes up, swells, peaks, then runs out of food and sinks. Knowing where it is in that cycle tells you the best moment to mix your dough.

1 Feed

Hour 0

You stir in fresh flour and water. The jar looks flat and smooth, and the microbes have plenty to eat.

2 Rising

About hours 1–6

The yeast eat and release gas. Bubbles appear and the starter swells, slowly at first and then faster.

3 Peak

About hours 6–8

Tallest and bubbliest, with a domed top and a mild, sweet-tangy smell. This is the best time to use it.

4 Collapse

After the peak

The food runs low and the dome sinks. The smell turns sharp, and you may see a grayish liquid on top. It is still alive, just hungry.

Why timing matters for your dough

The starter is what makes your loaf rise, so you want it at full strength.

  • Too early (still rising): there isn't enough active yeast yet. The dough ferments slowly and the loaf can bake up dense.
  • At or just before the peak: yeast and bacteria are balanced and at their strongest. The dough rises reliably and tastes pleasantly tangy.
  • Too late (collapsed): the yeast are worn out and the acids have built up. You get a sluggish rise, a sour taste, and sometimes a gummy crumb. Feed it again and wait for the next peak.

How to watch it: after feeding, put a rubber band on the jar at the starter's level. When it has at least doubled and the top is domed and bubbly, it's ready. Warm kitchens speed everything up and cool ones slow it down, so a peak might come at 4 hours in summer or 10 in winter. Watch the jar, not the clock.
