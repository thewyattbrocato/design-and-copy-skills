Rye & Ember

A starter's day, from feeding to collapse

After you feed a starter, the yeast and bacteria in it wake up, make gas, and push the mixture up the jar, then run out of food and sink back. Afterwards you'll be able to tell when it's ready to mix into dough, and why waiting too long costs you.

  1. 1 Fed

    You stir in fresh flour and water. Look: flat, smooth top. Mark the level with a rubber band.

  2. 2 Rising

    Yeast eats the new flour and makes gas. Look: bubbles appear and the level climbs past your mark.

  3. 3 Peak

    Gas production is at its fullest. Look: highest level, often a domed top with bubbles throughout.

  4. 4 Collapse

    The food runs low and the structure can't hold the gas. Look: the dome flattens or dips, and the smell turns sharper.

Why timing matters: a starter near its peak is full of active yeast, so it lifts dough steadily and tastes mild and sweet.

After the collapse it is hungry and weaker. Dough made with it tends to rise slowly and can taste sourer. It isn't ruined: feed it again and wait for the next peak.

So: feed the starter ahead of baking day, watch it against your rubber-band mark, and mix your dough as it nears or reaches the peak.

Your own jar is the real clock. Note when yours peaks after a feeding and you'll know your timing for next time.
