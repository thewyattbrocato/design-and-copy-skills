What your starter does after you feed it

A starter is flour and water full of living yeast and bacteria. After a feeding it rises, peaks, then falls. Once you can spot the peak, you'll know when to mix your dough.

  1. 1 · Feed

    Minute zero

    You stir in fresh flour and water. The microbes have plenty to eat again. Mark the jar's level with a rubber band.

  2. 2 · Rising

    Hours, not minutes

    The microbes eat and release gas. Bubbles appear and the starter creeps up past your mark.

  3. 3 · Peak

    The brief high point

    Tallest and bubbliest, often with a domed top. The microbes are at their strongest.

  4. 4 · Collapse

    After the peak

    The food runs low and the dome sinks. It isn't ruined. It's hungry and needs another feeding.

Why timing matters when you mix dough

Your starter is what makes the loaf rise, so use it when it's strongest.

  • Too early (still rising): the starter is still building strength, so the dough may rise slowly and come out dense.
  • At the peak: the starter is at full strength, which gives the most reliable rise.
  • Too late (collapsing): the starter is tired and the dough can be sluggish. You can feed it again and wait for the next peak.

How long does each stage take? It depends on your starter, your flour and how warm your kitchen is. A warm room speeds everything up and a cool one slows it down. Don't rely on the clock. Watch the jar against your rubber band, and note how many hours your own starter takes to peak.
