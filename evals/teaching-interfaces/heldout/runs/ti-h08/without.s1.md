Your instinct is right. Teaching forty shortcuts on day one fails for everyone, and lawyers are an especially bad audience for it. They're time-pressured and billing in increments. A mandatory tour reads as an obstacle between them and the matter they opened the app to work on. Retention of that much unprompted, context-free material is close to zero, and people who skip it learn to skip your onboarding in general.

**Principle: teach shortcuts at the moment they'd save time, not before.**

**1. Day one: teach 3 to 5 shortcuts, and make one of them the safety net.**
- Pick the ones that match the first-session workflow: new note, jump between matters, quick-cite, search.
- Teach the command palette or shortcut cheat sheet (`?` or `Cmd+/`) as the one thing to remember. If they know where to look things up, they don't need to memorize anything.
- Make it skippable and short, under a minute.

**2. Contextual nudges after the user does something the slow way.**
- If someone opens the matter switcher by mouse five times, show a small, dismissible hint: "Jump to any matter with `Cmd+J`."
- Show each hint at most once or twice, and stop if they dismiss it.
- This is the biggest lever. Behavior-triggered hints land because the user has the problem right then.

**3. Show the shortcut everywhere the action lives.**
- Put shortcuts in menu items, tooltips, and the command palette results. Every mouse path then teaches the keyboard path passively.

**4. Group shortcuts by workflow, not alphabetically.**
- Organize the cheat sheet as "Working across matters," "Citing and sourcing," and "Split view." Users learn a cluster when they adopt a workflow, so release them in clusters.

**5. Progressive disclosure over weeks.**
- A "Shortcut of the week" card, or a low-key "You've used 8 of 40 shortcuts" progress view for people who like that. Keep it opt-in.
- Offer an optional "Power user track" they can start whenever they want. This gives the PM a full walkthrough without forcing it.

**6. Make them forgiving.**
- Provide undo, avoid destructive single-key shortcuts, and let users rebind keys. Lawyers coming from Word, Outlook, or Westlaw will have muscle memory that conflicts with yours.

**For the PM conversation**
Frame it around the metric they care about. If the goal is shortcut adoption, the thing to measure is the share of users who use each shortcut by day 30, not the tour completion rate. A tour can hit 90% completion while teaching nothing. I'd propose an A/B test of the full tour against the lean day-one plus contextual nudges, measuring day-7 and day-30 shortcut usage and day-30 retention. Then you're settling it with data instead of opinion, and the PM keeps a path to the full walkthrough as the opt-in track.

One caveat: I'm inferring from general onboarding patterns, not from your user research. If you have any interviews or usage data on how your lawyers work, check which five shortcuts to feature first against that.
