You're right. A forty-shortcut tour on day one is the wrong move for this audience. Teach a few shortcuts in the middle of real work, and make the rest easy to find later.

**Why the tour fails.** New users don't need forty shortcuts on day one. They need to get their first matter's notes in, and nobody retains forty keystrokes they haven't used yet. Lawyers and paralegals also bill their time, so a mandatory tutorial reads as a tax, and they'll click through it. The shortcuts they do remember are the ones tied to something they just did by mouse.

**What to do instead**

1. **Make the shortcuts visible where the action already is.**
   - Show the keystroke in menus, buttons, tooltips, and the command palette, next to the mouse action.
   - Every mouse use then doubles as a lesson, and this teaches more than any onboarding.
   - If you don't have a command palette, a searchable one that lists each action with its shortcut is the best single investment.

2. **Teach two or three shortcuts in the first session, inside the real task.**
   - Pick the ones tied to the first thing a new user does. Quick-cite and jumping between matters are likely candidates, but check your usage data.
   - Introduce each one right after the user does the same thing with the mouse: "Next time: ⌘[key]." That's a one-line hint, not a gate. Don't use a "Got it" modal.
   - Let them try it right then.

3. **Nudge by behavior, not by calendar.**
   - If someone opens split view by mouse five times, show the shortcut once, dismissibly.
   - Don't use drip emails or streaks. Reminders tied to something the person just did are fine.

4. **Keep a reference for people who want the whole set.**
   - Add a searchable shortcut sheet, opened with `?` or from the Help menu, grouped by task (matters, citing, layout) rather than alphabetically.
   - Make it printable. Many power users will want it taped next to the monitor.
   - Link to it once from onboarding as "All shortcuts, whenever you want them."

5. **Offer the full walkthrough as an opt-in.**
   - If the PM needs a tour to exist, make it a skippable "Learn the shortcuts" option in Help, and let people pick a track such as Matters or Citing.
   - Don't make it the default path.

6. **Fix the shortcut design first.**
   - If some of the forty need explaining, make them guessable. Use mnemonic keys, group related ones under a shared modifier, and follow conventions from tools lawyers already use, like Word and Outlook.
   - Every shortcut you don't have to teach is one less thing to remember.

**How to pitch it to the PM.** The goal is that users are using shortcuts a few weeks in, not that they finished a tour. You can measure that without a tour. Track the share of eligible actions done by keyboard at weeks 1, 4, and 8, and compare a cohort that gets contextual hints against one that gets the full tour. If the tour wins, you'll have the data to switch. I'd expect hints to win, but that's a hypothesis, and I don't have your numbers.

I assumed you have a command palette or can build one, and that your usage data is available. If either is wrong, tell me and I'll adjust the plan.
