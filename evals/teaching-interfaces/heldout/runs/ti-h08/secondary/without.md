I'd agree with you. A forty-item tour on day one mostly gets dismissed, and what sticks is whatever a user needs in the first hour. Shortcuts are learned at the moment of friction, not by reading a list. Here's what I'd do instead.

**1. Make the command palette the main discovery tool.** If you don't have one, build it first. Cmd/Ctrl+K (or `/`) opens a searchable list of every action, and each row shows its shortcut. Lawyers who don't know a shortcut exists will usually search for what they want, and the palette teaches the key while they do it. This covers most of the forty for almost no onboarding cost.

**2. Show the shortcut where the action already is.** Put the key next to the item in menus, buttons, and right-click menus, and show it in tooltips. When someone clicks "Split view" for the tenth time, the shortcut is right there.

**3. Nudge in context, based on what they actually do.** When a user does something manually that has a shortcut, show a small, dismissible hint once: "You've inserted citations by hand 6 times today. Quick-cite is Cmd+Shift+C." Limit it to one hint per session and stop after a shortcut has been used. Track usage per user so you don't teach shortcuts they already know.

**4. Onboarding: teach three, tied to their first real task.** Ask one question at setup, like "Are you mostly litigation, transactional, or paralegal work?" and surface the two or three shortcuts that fit that work. Then add a single "Keyboard shortcuts" pane reachable with `?`, which opens a one-page reference they can print and keep by the monitor. Many people in this line of work like having a physical card.

**5. Let them rebind.** Lawyers already have muscle memory from Word, DMS tools, and Westlaw/Lexis. Check that your keys don't collide with those, and let people remap. A shortcut that conflicts with their existing habit will get switched off, not learned.

**6. Measure adoption, not completion.** Track whether users who see hints adopt the shortcuts, and how many weeks later they still use them. "Finished the tour" tells you nothing about whether anyone is faster.

**For the PM:** the goal is valid. Power features should get used, and the forty shortcuts are part of the product's value. The disagreement is about the delivery. A forced tour on day one costs billable-minded users time they don't have, and what they retain is probably the first three to five items anyway. The palette, contextual hints, and a role-based starting set reach the same forty shortcuts without the tour. If the PM still wants a guided intro, make it short (three shortcuts, under two minutes, skippable) and A/B test it against no tour. Then the decision rests on adoption data, not opinion.

If you want to check your instinct before pitching this, watch five paralegals work for an hour and count how often they do something manually that a shortcut already covers. That number will make the case better than either of our opinions.
