I'd agree with you. Forty shortcuts in one walkthrough on day one will mostly be forgotten by the next session. People learn shortcuts when they feel the cost of not knowing one, so teach them at that moment.

**What I'd build instead:**

1. **Teach at the moment of use.** When someone does a shortcut-able action with the mouse (splits a view, switches matters, inserts a cite), show a small non-blocking hint with the key combo. Let them dismiss it, and stop showing it for that action once they've used the shortcut a few times. This is the highest-leverage piece.

2. **Make the command palette the main discovery tool.** Use a single keystroke to open a searchable list of every action, with its shortcut displayed next to it. Lawyers already expect this from Westlaw, Outlook, and most modern editors. Power users will learn the palette early, and it teaches the rest as a side effect.

3. **Start with a small default set.** Pick the three to five shortcuts that get used most, based on usage data, and surface only those. Put the rest in the palette and a printable reference card.

4. **Let people bring their own keys.** Lawyers and paralegals already have muscle memory from Word, Westlaw, Lexis, and their DMS. Check which of the forty conflict with those conventions. Offer a keymap preset or a rebinding screen before you train anyone on defaults they'll resist.

5. **Make onboarding role-aware, but short.** A paralegal filing cites all day and an associate jumping between matters need different things. Ask one question at signup ("what do you do most?") and surface the shortcuts for that workflow. Keep the first-run flow to a few screens, and make it skippable.

6. **Let the data decide.** Measure how often people use each shortcut against the mouse path, and track shortcut adoption at 2 and 6 weeks. If the mouse-to-keyboard ratio barely moves, the hints are the thing to fix, not the number of tours.

**How to bring this to the PM:** a full tour doesn't fail on its own merits. It fails because it's built around the wrong moment. Propose a two-week test: the contextual hints and palette for one cohort against the full day-one tour for another, with shortcut adoption as the metric. That gives them a number to argue about, rather than a debate about which approach is better.
