Your instinct is right. A forty-step tour on day one won't stick. People dismiss it, forget it by the next matter, and never see most of the shortcuts again. Lawyers also arrive with strong muscle memory from Word, Westlaw, and Outlook, and they're billing while they learn. Teach shortcuts where they're useful, at the moment someone reaches for the mouse.

What I'd propose, roughly in order of value:

1. **Make a command palette the front door.** Cmd+K (or whatever fits your platform) lists every action, searchable, with its shortcut shown beside it. Someone who types "cite" sees quick-cite and its key. This is the highest-leverage item because it teaches every shortcut at the point of need, and power users will live in it.

2. **Contextual hints after repeated mouse actions.** If someone opens split view by clicking three times in a session, show a small, dismissible hint with the shortcut. Cap it at one hint per session, and stop permanently for any shortcut the user has used. This gives you spaced exposure, which a one-time tour can't.

3. **A "?" or Cmd+/ overlay and a printable one-page reference.** Firms often post these at desks. Cheap to build, and it serves people who prefer to look things up.

4. **Let people remap keys.** Many lawyers and paralegals already have habits from other tools, and conflicts with Word or the research platforms they keep open will kill adoption. Check your forty against the shortcuts in those apps before you ship.

5. **Peer and firm-level reinforcement.** Admins or practice-group champions can share a "shortcut of the week." This works better than any in-app flow for a group that talks to each other.

For the PM, I'd frame the goal as "people use the shortcuts that save them the most time" and suggest a compromise: a short onboarding that covers the three to five actions your telemetry shows are most frequent. If you don't have that data yet, pick the candidates from the beta users' workflows. Quick-cite and matter-jumping are likely on that list.

Measure adoption, meaning the share of shortcut-eligible actions done by keyboard, rather than onboarding completion. Completion rates tell you the tour was clicked through, not that anyone changed how they work.
