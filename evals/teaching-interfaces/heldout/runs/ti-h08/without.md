Your instinct is right. A forced walkthrough of all forty on day one fails for three reasons:

- **Retention.** People forget nearly everything from a tour of that size. They also haven't yet felt the pain each shortcut solves, so nothing sticks.
- **Interruption.** Lawyers and paralegals are billing time. A mandatory tour on day one reads as friction, and they'll skip it with muscle memory.
- **Wrong unit.** Forty shortcuts is a reference, not a lesson. Users need a handful at first and the rest when the work calls for them.

## What I'd do instead

**1. Day one: teach 5 shortcuts, not 40.**
Pick the ones that map to the highest-frequency actions, probably quick-cite, jump between matters, and new note, plus the command palette. Make it skippable and under a minute, ideally as a checklist the user completes by doing the actions, not a modal slideshow.

**2. Add a command palette (Cmd/Ctrl+K) if you don't have one.**
This does most of the work. Every action is searchable, and each result shows its shortcut next to it. Users who reach for the palette repeatedly see the shortcut each time, so they learn passively at the moment of use. It also gives you a safety net for the other 35.

**3. Teach in context, based on behavior.**
When someone does something by mouse that has a shortcut, show a small, dismissible hint, such as "Next time: ⌘J jumps between matters." Rules that keep it from becoming nagging:
- Show it only after they've done the action a few times.
- Show at most one hint per session.
- Stop hinting for a shortcut once they've used it.
- Let them turn hints off entirely.

**4. Show shortcuts everywhere the action lives.**
Put them in menu items, button tooltips, and context menus. This costs almost nothing and is the most reliable way to teach.

**5. Provide a reference, not a tutorial, for the rest.**
Add a searchable cheat sheet (`?` or Cmd+/) grouped by workflow (matters, citing, layout) instead of alphabetically. Also offer a printable PDF, since some of this audience will want to tape it next to their monitor.

**6. Pace the rest over the first weeks.**
A "shortcut of the week" tip, or a short email or in-app card tied to features they've actually used (for example, split view after they've opened two notes in a row). Treat it as optional.

## How to pitch it to the PM

The PM's goal is probably that people adopt the shortcuts and see the value quickly. Frame your approach as a better route to that goal, and propose measuring it:

- Track the share of users who've used at least 5 shortcuts by day 7 and by day 30.
- Track tour completion and skip rates for the day-one flow.
- Compare retention for an A/B test of the forced full tour against the staged approach.

If the staged approach wins, you have data. If the PM still wants a full walkthrough, offer it as an opt-in "Learn all shortcuts" page, not a gate.

One caveat: I'm reasoning from general onboarding patterns, not from your users. A few quick interviews with actual paralegals about what they do most would sharpen which five to lead with.
