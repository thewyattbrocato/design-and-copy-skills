Your instinct is right. Teaching all forty shortcuts on day one fails for three reasons:

- **Nobody retains it.** People forget most of a forty-item walkthrough within a day, because they have no context for why they'd need any of it yet.
- **It front-loads friction.** Lawyers and paralegals are time-pressured and skeptical of tools that slow them down. A long tour tells them the app is complicated before they've gotten any value from it.
- **It teaches the wrong thing.** Shortcuts are learned when the need arises, such as when someone is about to do a task by mouse that they'll repeat a hundred times.

**What I'd propose instead: teach each shortcut at the moment it's useful, and let people pull more when they want it.**

1. **Day-one onboarding covers 3–5 shortcuts, not forty.** Pick the ones that matter in every session, like new note, search or jump-to-matter, and quick-cite. Make it a short, skippable, interactive exercise where they press the keys, not a slideshow.

2. **Add contextual hints.** When someone does something by mouse that has a shortcut, show a small, dismissible hint, such as "Next time: ⌘⇧S to split view." Show each hint at most once or twice. Show it after the action succeeds, so you never interrupt the work.

3. **Show shortcuts where people already look.** Put them in menu items, tooltips, and the command palette. A ⌘K-style palette is the biggest lever for a forty-shortcut app. It's searchable, it displays the shortcut next to each command, and every use teaches the key.

4. **Add a searchable cheat sheet** on `?` or ⌘/, grouped by task (matters, citing, layout) instead of one flat list of forty. Link it from the Help menu.

5. **Nudge based on behavior.** If someone has used split view by mouse ten times, surface the shortcut once. Optionally add a weekly "you might like" tip for the shortcuts they haven't touched, and let them turn it off.

6. **Make the tips unobtrusive.** Lawyers will tolerate a few good tips but will switch them off if they feel naggy. Give them a single "stop showing tips" control.

**How to pitch it to the PM.** The PM's real goal is probably that people discover the shortcuts and the product feels powerful. Progressive teaching serves that goal better, and you can measure it:

- **Adoption:** the share of users who use each shortcut by day 7 and day 30.
- **Retention:** week-1 and week-4 retention compared with the long-tour flow.
- **Onboarding completion:** how many people finish or skip the flow.

If the PM still wants something on day one, offer the compromise: a 3–5 shortcut starter, plus a visible "Explore all shortcuts" link. You could also A/B test the long tour against the progressive approach, since you'll have the instrumentation anyway.
