**The one thing to teach:** "A heat pump water heater doesn't make heat. It pulls heat out of the air around it and moves it into your water."

Everything on the page should serve that sentence. Here is how I'd lay it out.

**1. Heading and one-line intro (top)**
- Heading: "A heat pump water heater moves heat instead of making it."
- One plain sentence under it, such as "Think of a refrigerator running in reverse: it pulls heat from the room and puts it in your water."
- Skip the page-opening list of benefits. Put any savings claims lower down, and only ones you can back up.

**2. The diagram, built in three steps**

Don't show the whole machine and then explain it. A reader sees all the parts at once and doesn't know where to start. Use three still frames, or one diagram that reveals in stages with a visible "Next" button. Each frame has the sentence directly beside or above the part it explains.

- **Frame 1, "Warm room air comes in."** Show the fan and evaporator coil with the "air in" and "cool air out" arrows. Caption: the coil soaks up heat from the air, and cooler air leaves.
- **Frame 2, "The compressor concentrates that heat."** Add the compressor. Caption in plain words, for example "makes the captured heat hot enough to heat water."
- **Frame 3, "The heat goes into the water."** Add the condenser coil around the tank and the "heat into water" arrow. Now the cold-water inlet and hot-water outlet become the focus.

This keeps it to about three new ideas before the reader reaches the full picture. The final frame is the complete diagram, and it should make sense on its own.

**3. Labeling rules for your diagram**
- Put each label on or touching its part ("Fan + evaporator coil," "Compressor," "Condenser coil," "Cold water in," "Hot water out"). Don't use a lettered legend or a key.
- Introduce the part names in frame 1 or 2, before the process uses them.
- Color the heat path warm and the cooled air cool. Add a second cue as well, such as a solid line for heat flow and a dashed line for air, so it still reads in grayscale.
- Keep label text at body size or larger on phones. If it won't fit, stack the frames vertically instead of shrinking the type.
- Write alt text that names the parts in order: air enters at the top, the coil takes heat, the compressor boosts it, the condenser coil passes it into the tank, and cold water enters at the bottom while hot water leaves at the top.

**4. Where the 600 words go**
- About 100 to 150 words become the three frame captions, so the core explanation lives next to the picture.
- Use the rest below the diagram, under questions homeowners actually ask, for example:
  - Why does it use less electricity than a regular electric heater?
  - What does it need in terms of space and airflow?
  - What happens when it's cold, or when I use a lot of hot water?
  - What does it sound like, and what maintenance does it need?
- Cut or demote anything that doesn't help someone answer "would this work in my home?" Only include numbers (efficiency, savings, temperatures) that you can source. I'd rather you leave a placeholder than guess.

**5. End of the page**
- One clear action for Tidewater, such as "Not sure if it fits your home? [Call / request a quote]."
- Place it after the explanation, not before. By then the reader understands the product, so the call to action will land better.

**Things to avoid:** a lettered legend below the diagram, an animation with no step controls, decorative photos larger than the diagram, and jargon like "refrigerant cycle" without a plain-language gloss.

**How to check it works:** show it to a few homeowners who haven't seen it. Ask them to explain, in their own words, where the heat comes from. Do this a day or two later if you can. If they say "it heats the water with electricity," frame 1 isn't landing.
