**Recommended layout: build the diagram up one part at a time. Don't show it whole.**

Your finished diagram has about eight parts plus three arrows. A newcomer can't take that in at a glance, and a legend would make them hunt for each name. Reveal it in four stills. Each still is the same drawing with one or two parts added, and each part is labeled on the part itself.

**The one thing to teach:** afterwards a homeowner can say, "It doesn't make heat, it moves heat from the air around it into the water." Everything on the page serves that sentence.

### Page order

1. **Headline and answer, above the fold.**
   - Use a plain heading like "How a heat pump water heater works."
   - Follow it with one sentence: "Instead of burning fuel or heating a coil, it pulls heat out of the air and moves it into your water."
   - One optional line can help: "Think of a refrigerator running in reverse." Most people already have that model.

2. **Four build-up steps.** Put each step's sentence directly above or beside its picture, not in a caption far below. Keep each to 2–4 sentences, which is roughly 60–80 words.
   - **Step 1, the tank.** Show the tank, the cold inlet at the bottom, and the hot outlet at the top. The message is that water goes in cold and comes out hot, and the question is where the heat comes from.
   - **Step 2, the air side.** Add the fan and evaporator coil, plus the "air in" and "cool air out" arrows. The message is that the fan pulls room air across the coil, the coil absorbs its heat, and cooler air leaves.
   - **Step 3, the compressor.** Add the compressor. The message is that it squeezes that gathered heat into a hotter, more useful form.
   - **Step 4, the condenser.** Add the condenser coil wrapped around the tank and the "heat into water" arrows. This is the complete diagram, with the heat's whole path highlighted.

   Previous steps stay visible and dimmed, and the newest part is highlighted. Use stills with "Next/Back" controls, not an auto-playing video, so readers set their own pace and can print the page. If you want motion, make it a short, pausable loop of the arrows only, with a stills fallback for reduced-motion settings.

3. **"What this means in your home."** Move the rest of your 600 words here as short items, since they are consequences rather than mechanism. Examples are the cooler air it blows out, the space and airflow it needs, and noise. Use your own verified figures, such as `[efficiency / savings claim]`, and don't borrow generic numbers.

4. **One call to action.** For example, "Wondering if it fits your basement or utility room? [Book a quote / call Tidewater]." Don't add a second call to action.

### Details that matter
- **Labels on parts.** Write names directly on the diagram, using SVG text beside each shape or a short leader line. Don't use a lettered key.
- **Words match the labels.** Use the same words on the picture and in the text, such as "evaporator coil" in both. Add a half-clause the first time, like "the coil that absorbs heat."
- **Arrows carry the story.** Use one color for heat and another for water, and keep both consistent across all four stills.
- **Mobile.** Stack each step as a picture with its sentence right above it. Don't put the text in a column that falls far below the figure.
- **Accessibility.** Give each still alt text that states its message ("Fan pulls room air across the coil, which absorbs its heat"). Nothing essential should live only in an animation or on hover.
- **Cut.** Skip stock photos of smiling homeowners and any decorative water-drop graphics.

### How to check it works
Show the page to 3–5 people who haven't seen it. Ask them, "Where does the heat in the hot water come from?" and "Why does the air coming out feel cooler?" If they answer in their own words, it works. Later, see whether customers' basic "how does it work?" calls drop.
