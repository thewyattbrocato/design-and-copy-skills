**Goal sentence:** After reading, a homeowner can say, "A heat pump water heater moves heat from the air into the water instead of making heat, so it uses much less electricity."

Don't invent savings figures. Use `[X]%` placeholders unless Tidewater has a source for the number.

## Layout, top to bottom

1. **Headline and one-sentence takeaway.** "A heat pump water heater moves heat instead of making it." Put a one-line analogy right under it: it works like a refrigerator running in reverse, pulling heat out of the room air and putting it into your water. Most homeowners already have a model of a fridge, so this is the anchor.

2. **The diagram, built in 3 stills.** Don't show all of it at once. You have about seven parts (tank, fan, evaporator, compressor, condenser, inlet, outlet) plus three sets of arrows. Build the same drawing up in steps, each with a visible "Next" and "Back":
   - **Step 1, the tank alone.** Cold water in at the bottom, hot water out at the top. This is the part they already know.
   - **Step 2, heat from the air.** Add the fan and evaporator coil, with the air-in and cool-air-out arrows. Caption: "The fan pulls in room air. The evaporator coil absorbs its heat. The air leaves cooler."
   - **Step 3, heat into the water.** Add the compressor and the condenser coil around the tank, with the heat-into-water arrows. Caption: "The compressor concentrates that heat and sends it to the coil around the tank, which warms the water."

   Highlight whatever is new in each step and dim the rest.

3. **Labels go on the parts.** Write "Fan," "Evaporator coil," and so on directly on or touching each part, with a short leader line if needed. Don't use a lettered legend or a key. Put each step's caption directly above or beside its drawing, including on phones. Don't park it far below the figure.

4. **Make every step readable without motion.** Stills work better than an animation for this, and they print. If you later add a short looping animation of the heat flow, make it pausable and give it a still fallback. Nothing essential should live only in the animation or in hover text.

5. **Your 600 words, split up:**
   - **Under each diagram step:** 1–2 sentences, taken from your text.
   - **"Why it saves energy":** one short paragraph after the diagram. Moving heat takes less electricity than creating it with a resistance element.
   - **"What this means for your home":** a short block with the practical points. Examples are that the unit cools and dehumidifies the room around it, needs some open air space, and recovers more slowly than a standard electric heater unless it has a backup element. Only include what your own text and Tidewater can stand behind.
   - **Jargon:** define "evaporator," "condenser," and "compressor" once, on the labeled part, in a few words each. Add a refrigerant explanation only if your text needs it.

6. **Call to action at the end.** "Wondering if one fits your home? [Call/Request a quote]" with Tidewater's actual contact details. Keep it to one action.

## Cut or demote
- Stock photos of smiling families, and any decorative art bigger than the diagram.
- A "benefits" carousel above the diagram.
- Any paragraph that restates what the diagram step already says.

## Check it works
Show it to 3–5 homeowners, with no coaching. A few days later, ask them to explain in their own words where the heat comes from. That test tells you more than time on page or scroll depth.

**Assumptions:** I haven't seen your text or diagram, so the step split assumes the parts you listed. If your 600 words cover other topics, such as sizing, cost, or maintenance, move them to separate sections below the explainer or to their own pages.
