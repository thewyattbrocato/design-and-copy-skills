**Takeaway sentence:** "A heat pump water heater moves heat from the air around it into your water, instead of making heat from scratch, so it uses far less electricity."

Everything on the page should serve that sentence. You can check it by asking whether a homeowner could repeat it to a neighbor afterward.

## Layout, top to bottom

**1. Headline and one-line answer (above the fold)**
- Heading: "How a heat pump water heater works."
- Directly under it, the takeaway sentence in plain type. Don't open with a hero photo or a benefits strip.

**2. The diagram, built up in 3 stills**
Your diagram has about eight parts (tank, fan, evaporator, compressor, condenser, inlet, outlet, and the arrows). That is too many to show at once to a newcomer. Draw the same picture three times, adding one idea each time, and put a short sentence directly above each still:

- **Still 1, the tank:** Tank, cold inlet at the bottom, hot outlet at the top. *"Cold water goes in at the bottom. Hot water leaves from the top."* Homeowners already know this part.
- **Still 2, heat from the air:** Add the fan, the evaporator coil and the "air in / cool air out" arrows. *"A fan pulls room air across a coil. The coil soaks up the heat in that air, and the air leaves cooler."*
- **Still 3, heat into the water:** Add the compressor, the condenser coil around the tank and the "heat into water" arrows. *"The compressor concentrates that heat and sends it to a coil wrapped around the tank, which warms the water."*

Each still adds only one or two new things. Mark what's new with a color or an outline, and show earlier parts in gray.

**3. Labels go on the parts**
- Put each name on or touching its part, with a short leader line if needed. Don't use a lettered key or a legend.
- Put the plain-language job in the label, for example "Evaporator coil: picks up heat from air."
- Use one color for heat and one for water throughout, so the arrows read at a glance.
- On a phone, put each still's sentence directly above its drawing, not in a caption far below. Don't rely on hover for anything.

**4. Use stills, not an animation**
Stills work at the reader's own pace and print well. If you want motion later, an optional short loop of the heat arrows is the only place it earns its keep. It should pause and respect reduced-motion settings. Give the diagram real alt text, such as a sentence per still, because nothing essential should live only in the image.

**5. Your 600 words: split them by job**
- **Beside the stills (about 60–90 words total):** the three sentences above, trimmed to one or two each.
- **Under the diagram, as short sections with plain headings (about 300 words):**
  - "Why it uses less electricity." This is the payoff, so lead with it. Compare moving heat with making heat.
  - "What to expect." Cover the cooler, quieter air around the unit, the fan noise, and the space it needs. Only include claims you can back up for your installs.
  - "Is it right for your home?" Cover location, space and climate, and keep it honest.
- **Move to the bottom or a collapsible section:** the technical detail on refrigerant and compression. Most homeowners won't read it, and the ones who want it can open it.
- **Cut:** anything that repeats what the stills already say.

**6. One clear next step**
End with a single action, such as "Get a quote" or "Ask us whether it fits your home," with a phone number. Skip pop-ups or carousels in between.

## Things to avoid
- Showing the full diagram in one go with a legend.
- Pasting the 600 words as one block above or below the picture.
- Stock photos of smiling families, which would be the thing remembered instead of the idea.

## Checking whether it works
Show it to 3–5 homeowners and ask them to explain it back in their own words. A day later, ask them again. If they say "it pulls heat out of the air," the page works. If you have analytics, track whether people reach the quote step after the diagram, but don't assume a good number means they understood.
