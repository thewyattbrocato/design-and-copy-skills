# Page layout: how a heat pump water heater works

**The one thing:** after reading, a homeowner can say, "It doesn't make heat. It moves heat from the air around it into the water."

## Top to bottom

1. **Heading that states the takeaway.** For example: *"A heat pump water heater moves heat into your water instead of making it."* Avoid a topic label like "How It Works".

2. **One sentence of context, then the diagram.** Say it works like a refrigerator running in reverse, pulling heat out of the air and putting it into water. Skip the intro and the company history.

3. **The diagram, as a block diagram with words on the parts.** Details are below.

4. **The explanation as 3–4 short segments, each beside the part it describes.** The sequence is:
   - Warm room air is pulled in.
   - The evaporator coil picks up its heat.
   - The compressor concentrates that heat.
   - The condenser coil passes the heat to the tank water, and cooler air leaves.

   Cut your 600 words down to a sentence or two per segment.

5. **A separate "What this means in your home" section.** Put the practical material here, such as space, noise, cooler air, and backup heating. Use only what your 600 words already say. This keeps the mechanism section short and gives skimmers a clear exit.

6. **A call to action** for Tidewater Plumbing, such as a quote or a question line.

## The diagram

- **Labels go on the parts, with no legend.** Use plain wording with the jargon second:
  - "Fan and evaporator coil: picks up heat from the air"
  - "Compressor: concentrates the heat"
  - "Condenser coil: gives heat to the water"
  - "Cold water in" and "Hot water out" sit right at the inlet and outlet.
  - Keep each label to about 25 characters and put the rest in the segment text.
- **That's three new terms.** It's the most to introduce before the reader has a picture, so name the parts first and show the process second.
- **Label the three arrows in place:** "Warm room air in," "Cooler air out," "Heat moves into water." Differ them by line style as well as color, so the diagram reads in grayscale.
- **Consider adding a refrigerant loop,** if your diagram doesn't have one. A single line labeled "refrigerant" running evaporator → compressor → condenser makes the compressor look connected rather than floating. Use a different line style from the air and heat arrows.
- **Build it in stills.** Show the parts first, then highlight one stage at a time with the matching sentence directly above or beside it. A short "Next" step beats a long animation, and the reader controls the pace. Use motion only if it's short, pausable, and falls back to stills for reduced-motion users.
- **Mobile:** run it top to bottom. Each sentence sits directly above its stage, not in a caption far below.
- **Accessibility:** give the SVG a short text alternative naming the parts and flow in order, and keep label text at about 12px or larger. Check contrast in both light and dark themes.
- **Stand-alone test:** hide the surrounding text. If someone can't state the takeaway from the heading and labels alone, a label or arrow is missing.

## What to cut

- Lettered or numbered legends.
- Stock photos of water heaters or happy families, which would become the memory instead of the idea.
- Any refrigeration-cycle detail beyond the three parts above.

## How to know it worked

Show the page to a few customers and ask them to explain it back in their own words a day later. Fewer "how does this work?" calls is a second signal. I'd avoid any completion or retention number you haven't measured.
