**The one thing:** after reading, a homeowner can say, "A heat pump water heater doesn't make heat. It moves heat from the air around it into the water, so it uses much less electricity."

Everything below supports that sentence. I'm assuming your readers are homeowners who may be comparing it with a standard electric tank, and that they have no refrigeration background.

## Layout, top to bottom

**1. Headline and one-line takeaway (above the diagram)**
"How a heat pump water heater works: it moves heat instead of making it." Add one plain sentence under it, such as "Think of a refrigerator running in reverse: it pulls heat out of the air and puts it into your water." The fridge comparison is the only analogy you need. Keep it, and don't add others.

**2. Diagram built up in three steps, not shown all at once**
Your diagram has about eight parts and three arrow types. That's too much to take in at once. Use the same drawing three times, adding one idea each time, with a "Next" control or three stacked images:

- **Step 1: Warm room air goes in.** Show the tank, the fan and evaporator coil on top, and the air-in and cool-air-out arrows. Sentence: "A fan pulls in room air. The coil absorbs its heat, so the air leaving is cooler."
- **Step 2: The compressor boosts the heat.** Add the compressor. Sentence: "The compressor concentrates that heat, making it hot enough to heat water."
- **Step 3: The heat goes into the water.** Add the condenser coil around the tank, the heat-into-water arrows, and the cold inlet and hot outlet. Sentence: "The hot coil warms the tank. Cold water comes in at the bottom, and hot water leaves from the top."

On each step, highlight the newly added parts and dim the rest. That limits the new ideas to two or three per step.

**3. Labels on the parts**
Put each name directly on or touching its part, with a short leader line where needed. Don't use a lettered key or a legend below the image. Use plain names first, with the technical term second where you need it, for example "Fan", "Evaporator coil (absorbs heat from air)", "Compressor", and "Condenser coil (releases heat into water)". Put each step's sentence directly above or beside its image. On phones, don't let the text drop far below the figure.

**4. Your 600 words, cut into short segments**
Don't run them as one block. Give each of the three steps its own sentence or two, as above. Move the rest under plain question headings that a homeowner would actually ask:

- Why does it use less electricity?
- What happens in a cold garage or basement?
- Does it cool the room it's in?
- What does it need (space, airflow, a drain)?
- When might a standard tank be the better choice?

Keep each answer to a short paragraph. People can skim to the question they care about. Only include claims about savings, temperature limits and installation requirements that you can source. I haven't invented any numbers here, so mark `[your figure + source]` where you want one.

**5. Call to action (last, once)**
"Not sure if it fits your home? Tidewater Plumbing can check your space and your current setup." Add a phone number or a button. Don't add a pop-up or any content between the diagram and the explanation.

## Things to avoid
- A single full diagram with a numbered legend underneath.
- Animation as the only explanation. If you add motion to the air and heat arrows, make it short and pausable, show stills when the visitor prefers reduced motion, and keep the same meaning in the text.
- Stock photos or a mascot above the diagram. The diagram should be the most memorable thing on the page.
- Alt text that says only "diagram". Describe the sequence in a sentence or two: air in, heat absorbed, compressed, released into the tank.

## How to check it works
Show the page to three or four people who aren't in the trade and ask them, without the page in front of them, "Where does the heat come from?" If they say "the electric element" or "I'm not sure", the step 1 sentence needs work. That's a better measure than time on page.
