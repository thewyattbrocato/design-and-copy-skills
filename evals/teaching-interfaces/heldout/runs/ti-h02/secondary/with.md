**Why people skip**

Step 1 is the first thing they see, and it gives them nothing to do. It's a welcome plus a "Next" button, shown before they've decided why they're here. Skip is the only exit that doesn't cost them anything, so 80% take it.

Steps 2–4 are inventories: nine sidebar labels, five top-bar items, five dashboard metrics. The sidebar labels are self-explanatory and need no tour. The dashboard metrics mean nothing on a new account with no data, so the tour is pointing at numbers that are all zero or blank. The tour also shows at once, as seven modal steps over an empty screen. Nothing happens for the person until step 7, so the whole thing is an unpaid wait.

The real first success (making an invoice) is step 5, and it's described rather than done. The bank step is the one that matters most for payments, and it comes last, after they've already started skipping.

Analytics show where people leave, not why. Before changing copy, check whether people who skip ever create an invoice in their first week. That's the measure that counts, not tour completion.

**What I'd change**

1. **Replace the tour with a guided first task.** Start the new account on an empty Invoices screen with one primary button: "Create your first invoice." Ask only for what the first invoice needs: client, one line item, due date. Put the tax, discount and payment-link options behind "More options," since they aren't needed for a first send. The step 5 text becomes the hint on the form, not a slide.

2. **Cut steps 2–4 entirely.** Explain the dashboard metrics on the dashboard once there's data, with a one-line caption on each. On an empty dashboard, show what the area is for and the one action that fills it.

3. **Teach the shortcut where it's used.** Put "N" in the tooltip on the + button. Don't present it as a tour step.

4. **Move bank connection to the moment it pays off.** Show a prompt after the first invoice is sent, or on the first payment that can't be matched: "Connect your bank to match payments automatically." Step 6 as written is a setup chore with no reason attached.

5. **Make skip safe and return possible.** Keep a visible "Skip for now" that doesn't dead-end. Add a "Show me around" link in the help center for later.

6. **Rewrite the ending as a confirmation with a next step.** Replace "You're all set!" with what now works and what's next, e.g. "Invoice [number] is ready. Send it to [client] when you're ready." No ceremony between them and the task.

**Sketch of a replacement sequence**

- Empty Invoices screen: "Send your first invoice in about two minutes." [Create invoice]
- Form with the three required fields, a "More options" disclosure, and a hint on the + button: "Shortcut: press N."
- After save: "Invoice [number] is ready to send." [Send] · "Connect your bank later to match payments automatically." [Connect bank]
- Help center: "Show me around" replays the three most useful tips, if you still want a tour.

I haven't checked the actual button labels or the Banking menu path in the product, so the copy above uses placeholders where those matter.

**Notes**

- Measure first-week "invoice created" and "invoice sent" for skippers versus non-skippers, and run the new flow against the current one, rather than judging by the skip rate alone.
- If the product has several main reasons people sign up (for example, invoicing and expense tracking), the first question could pick a short path for each. With one dominant reason, I wouldn't branch.
