**Tell him: "They asked for the outcome, not for unlimited authority. We can give them the outcome and cap the downside."**

## Why "they asked for it" doesn't settle it

1. **"Handle everything" is a statement about effort, not risk.** In an interview, nobody is pricing in the bad case. Customers are saying "I don't want to do this work." They aren't saying "I accept that you may burn a month of my budget in a weekend." Interviews tell you what pain to remove. They don't tell you what risk to take on.

2. **The payoffs are lopsided, and that's his language.** Suppose the assistant is right most of the time. The upside is a happy customer who would have been fairly happy anyway. The downside is an unrecoverable loss for a small shop, where ad spend is often a large share of discretionary cash. Small shops can't absorb a bad week the way an enterprise can. One visible overspend costs you that customer, their word of mouth, and possibly a refund or dispute. Avoiding the tail matters more than the average.

3. **You have no track record yet.** On day one you don't know your real error rate or how often it misreads a business. Full control means taking the maximum exposure when your uncertainty is highest. Starting limited produces the data to justify widening later.

4. **Limited control still delivers "handle everything."** What the customer experiences is that they don't have to touch it. A hard spend cap, a daily budget ceiling, and a pause button don't change that. They only bound the worst case.

## What to propose instead

- **Full autonomy inside a cap.** The customer sets a monthly max at signup. The assistant picks channels, copy, bids and timing freely within it, and it can't exceed the cap.
- **A one-line promise:** "While you're away, it will run and adjust your ads within $X/month, and it will never spend past that or change the cap itself."
- **Hold anything outside the cap for approval.** That covers a budget increase, a new channel, or a sharp change in strategy.
- **Early warning, not after-the-fact.** Alert at a threshold before the cap, not once it's crossed. Use a weekly digest for routine activity and interrupt only for problems.
- **Visible activity and easy pause.** One status screen shows running or paused, last action, and spend against the cap. Pause is one tap, and every change can be reviewed.
- **Widen trust by proposal.** After a run of clean weeks, the assistant offers "Want to raise your cap or let me add a channel?" and the customer accepts. Nothing widens silently.

## How to put it to him quantitatively

Don't argue with the interview data. Reframe the decision:

- **Cost of being too cautious:** some customers raise the cap in week 3 instead of week 1. That's a small, recoverable delay in spend.
- **Cost of being too aggressive:** a bad-case overspend, then churn, a refund and a bad review. That's large and hard to reverse.
- **Test it.** Run a cohort on capped autonomy and track cap-raise rate, pause rate, support tickets about spend, and retention. If customers keep hitting the cap and asking for more, you have evidence to loosen it. I won't invent figures for this. You'd fill those in from your own pilot.

One caveat: this assumes the failure modes are real for your product. If your assistant already has hard platform-side limits that make a runaway impossible, tell him that. It changes the argument, and the cap is then partly built already.
