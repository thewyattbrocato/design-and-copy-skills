**Reframe it for him: you're not disagreeing with the customer, you're disagreeing about what they asked for.**

"Handle everything" is a request about their effort: they don't want to do the work. It isn't a request to remove every limit on how much money the agent can spend. Nobody in those interviews said "and I'm fine if it overspends my budget." You can test this by asking the same customers, "Would you want a hard cap you set yourself?" Nearly all of them will say yes. That's the strongest evidence you can get cheaply.

**The numbers argument is about tails, not averages.**

- **The payoff is asymmetric.** Unlimited autonomy adds a little convenience for the many users who would have approved the agent's actions anyway. It adds catastrophic downside for the few where the agent misfires, such as a bad bid loop, a misread budget, or a campaign that spends all day on junk clicks. A small shop with a $2k/month budget can't absorb one $1k bad day. A big advertiser shrugs that off.
- **Cost of an incident versus the benefit.** Compare the expected cost (probability of an incident × refunds, churn, support, reputation, and possibly liability for spending money you were never authorized to spend) with the expected gain (the marginal conversion from skipping an approval step). Even at a low incident rate, the first term likely wins. Small shops also talk to each other, so one bad story costs you more than one customer.
- **You don't have the data yet.** You don't know the agent's real failure rate on live accounts. Shipping full control means finding it out with your customers' money.

**Don't make this "control versus no control." Propose a ladder that gets him most of what he wants.**

1. **Day one:** the agent acts automatically within hard limits, such as a daily and monthly cap the user sets, a maximum change per day (for example ±20% on any budget or bid), and a one-click pause.
2. **Notifications, not approvals:** it tells the user what it did instead of asking first, so it still feels like it "handles everything."
3. **Larger moves** (raising the budget, launching new campaigns) need approval until the account has a track record.
4. **Widen the limits per account** as the data supports it.

This still delivers the "just handles it" experience for the 90% of actions that are routine. It also generates the numbers he'll want: override rate, cap-hit rate, and spend variance versus plan. Those tell you when to loosen the limits, and you can show him the criteria in advance (for example, "if the override rate is under X% over 30 days, we raise the default cap").

**Close with the cost of being wrong.** If the cautious version is too restrictive, you loosen a setting in a day. If the unrestricted version burns a customer's budget, you can't undo it. When one option is cheap to reverse and the other isn't, and you lack data, a numbers person should pick the reversible one.

If you can, run a few quick tests before the meeting. Ask five or six of the interviewed customers about spend caps, and pull any historical data on how often ad changes get reversed. A concrete number from either will land better than any argument.
