Don't argue that customers are wrong. Argue that "handle everything" and "unlimited spend authority from day one" are different things, and that you can get the first without the second.

**1. Customers asked for an outcome, not a permission level.**
"Handle everything" means they don't want to do the work. It doesn't mean they want to hand over an uncapped card. Few small-shop owners, asked directly, would say "yes, spend whatever you decide with no limit, starting before you've proven yourself." If you haven't asked that question, you don't have data on it. Interview answers about wants are weak evidence of what people will accept when real money is at stake.

**2. The payoff is asymmetric, and the downside is bigger for this segment.**
- Upside of full control on day one over a capped version: a modest efficiency gain, since most of the value comes from the work being done at all.
- Downside: a bad bid, a bugged campaign, or a misread of seasonality burns real cash. For a small shop, a $1–2k bad week can be a meaningful share of monthly revenue. They can't absorb the variance the way a larger advertiser can.
- A single serious overspend is likely to cost you the customer, a refund or credit, and word of mouth in a segment that runs on it. Those costs are far larger than the gain from skipping a ramp.

**3. You don't have the error rate yet.**
A numbers person will respond to this one. You're setting the autonomy level before measuring how often the system makes costly mistakes. Day-one caps aren't pessimism. They're how you collect the data you'd need to justify removing them.

**4. Propose a ladder with exit criteria, not a "no."**
- Start with a hard spend cap per customer, set by them, with the assistant free to act fully inside it.
- Raise the cap automatically as measurable gates are met, for example a few weeks of ROAS at or above baseline, no anomalies, and a high share of actions the customer didn't reverse.
- Let customers opt up to "full control" themselves. That gives you the real revealed-preference number: what share choose it, and how fast.

This is a better test of the CTO's own thesis. If customers really want full control, the opt-up rate will show it within weeks and you'll have the evidence to make it the default. If they don't, you've avoided a costly mistake.

**What to bring to the meeting:** a rough expected-cost estimate (assumed error rate × average overspend × customers affected, plus churn from affected accounts) next to the estimated incremental benefit of skipping the ramp. Even with rough inputs, the comparison will likely favor the ladder. Ask him what error rate he'd need to believe for full control to win. That turns it into a discussion about numbers instead of about opinions.
