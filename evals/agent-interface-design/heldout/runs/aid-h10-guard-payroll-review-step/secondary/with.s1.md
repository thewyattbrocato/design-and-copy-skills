**I'd push back on this. Don't remove the review based on a streak of approvals.**

- **A streak measures how often the review was wrong, not whether it's needed.** Three clean runs show that the timesheets and autopilot were right three times. The review exists for the run where they're wrong: a missed clock-out, a rate change, a new hire, a terminated employee still on the sheet. That run is also most likely to come after a streak, since the owner has stopped looking.
- **"They click through" is a finding about the review's design.** If nobody reads the screen, it's probably showing too much to read or too little to act on. The fix is a better review, not no review.
- **The downside is asymmetric.** Support says the money lands the next day and is hard to recall. A wrong pay run at an 8-person company means real people paid wrong, and the owner may not notice until the next payday.
- **Small-business owners rely on this step.** They may have no finance staff to catch an error after the fact, so the approval may be their only control.

**What I'd do instead:**

1. **Check the premise first.** Pull data on approvals: time on screen, whether anyone ever edited a run after seeing it, how often a run was changed or reversed after send. If customers really never look, that's a UX problem to solve.
2. **Make review fast when nothing changed.** Show only exceptions: changes from the last run, new or departed employees, overtime, pay above a threshold, anything the autopilot flagged. A clean run should be a quick, specific confirmation, not a blank button.
3. **Add a send window for the hard-to-reverse part.** Hold approved runs for a few hours before they reach the bank, with a cancel option. This addresses the irreversibility, which is the real risk, and it's cheaper than removing review.
4. **If product still wants delegation, make it an explicit opt-in.** The customer turns on auto-approve with stated limits (e.g., pay within ±X% of the last run, no new or removed employees, no exceptions flagged). Show what it did after each run, make it easy to turn off, and never let a streak enable it silently. A streak can suggest the option; the owner has to accept it.

If the goal is to cut friction for the people who really do rubber-stamp, options 2 and 4 get most of the benefit without letting the first bad run go out unseen.
