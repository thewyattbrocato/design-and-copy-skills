**Don't do it as proposed.** Three approvals in a row show that the screen is easy to click through. They don't show that the pay runs were correct. Payroll is also the kind of task where the cost of a wrong run is far larger than the cost of one extra click.

**Why the proposal fails**

- **Money can't be pulled back.** Funds land the next day and are hard to recover. The review screen is the last point where a mistake is cheap, so removing it leaves no safety net behind it.
- **Clicking through is what a safety check looks like when it works.** Most runs are fine, so people approve quickly. The review matters in the rare run that isn't fine: a missed timesheet, a duplicated entry, a changed rate, a new hire or terminated employee. Three clean runs say little about the fourth.
- **With about 8 employees, an error is a big share of the payroll.** The owner may be thinly staffed, and one wrong paycheck is a real problem for them and for the employee.
- **"They'll notice afterward" isn't a safety plan.** Once the money has moved, noticing is too late.
- **Trust builds up silently and is lost suddenly.** If the autopilot misreads a timesheet once with no review in place, the customer's trust in the product is gone, not just for that run.

**What to do instead**

Product's real goal is less friction for people who always approve. Meet it without removing the approval on money movement:

1. **Make the review fast and focused.** For a customer on a clean streak, replace the full screen with a short summary: total, headcount, and what changed since last run. Put the doubtful items first (new hire, rate change, hours far from the usual, a missing timesheet). Keep "Approve and send" as one tap.
2. **Offer auto-send only as an opt-in, with caps.** Let the customer propose it and accept it themselves. Don't switch it on silently for them. Set explicit limits, such as total within a stated range of recent runs, no changes to employees or rates, and no unusual hours. Any run outside those limits falls back to manual review.
3. **Add a hold before the money moves.** Even under auto-send, delay submission by a stated window (for example, until a cutoff the bank allows) and send a heads-up summary beforehand. Include a clear "Stop this run" control. The hold window is a starting value to confirm with your bank and processing timelines, not a recommendation I can verify.
4. **Make the exceptions loud.** Anything that breaks the pattern should interrupt the customer. Routine runs can be batched into a digest.
5. **Keep a status view and a pause.** Show running or paused, the last run, the next run, and the caps. Pausing should be one step away.
6. **Be honest about undo.** Say plainly what can't be reversed once funds are released, and give a human path to support for corrections.

**Before deciding, check the data**

Product's claim that customers "obviously just click through" is untested. Look at how long customers spend on the review screen, how often they edit or reject a prepared run, and how often an approved run later needs a correction or reversal. If corrections after approval are not zero, that settles it. If the autopilot is wrong rarely but expensively, the opt-in design above gets most of the convenience at far lower risk.

**Recommendation:** ship the faster summary review first, then offer capped, opt-in auto-send with a hold window to customers with a clean history. Don't remove the review by default.
