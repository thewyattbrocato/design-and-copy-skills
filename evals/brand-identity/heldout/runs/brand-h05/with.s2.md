**Refine it. Keep the dog.**

Your partner wants a clean, professional logo for three locations. You want to keep what the neighborhood already recognizes. Redrawing the dog gets you both.

**Why refine.** The trigger is expansion, and expansion is a reason to make the logo work at more sizes. It isn't a reason to change what people recognize. Twelve years of neighborhood recognition is worth keeping. The open locations will also be part of the same community. Your existing customers will see the new signs and know it's still you. People in the new neighborhoods will see a friendly, hand-made dog, and few vet logos look like that. A polished generic dog or paw print is much easier to confuse with another clinic.

**What I'd do:**

1. **List what people recognize.** I'd guess it's the dog with one ear up, the wobbly hand-drawn feel, and the rounded lettering. I'd keep those and fix everything else.
2. **Redraw the dog as one confident, even-weight line.** Keep the pose, the tilted ear, and the character of the original. Remove the pencil texture, the hairline strokes, and the accidental wobble. Leave a little of the hand-drawn quality in the curves so it still looks like the same dog. It should read as that dog drawn carefully, not as a different dog.
3. **Make it work solid black on white and reversed on dark.** Pencil sketches fail here, so this is the main technical job. The dog needs to be a flat shape or a bold line, not shading.
4. **Draw a simpler small version** for favicons, social avatars, and tags. It would probably be the head and the up ear only, with no fine detail. Don't just shrink the full logo.
5. **Rework the name lettering.** Keep the rounded feel. Tune the spacing by eye and change one letter or detail so it's custom and not a stock font. Match its weight to the dog's line.
6. **Set up three lockups:** dog beside the name, dog stacked over the name, and dog alone. Add a minimum size and clear space. Each new location gets its own line of text, such as "Eastside", under the logo and never inside it.
7. **Pick one signature color and keep it.** If the old logo has a color people know, use it. If it was only pencil gray, choose one warm color now and commit to it.

**How I'd handle your partner.** Present this as the clean professional logo they asked for, with the dog as the part that sets it apart. Show both of you the redrawn version on a sign, a scrub top, a website header, and a 32-pixel icon. Seeing it there tends to settle the argument faster than discussing it.

**One way to check.** Show 10 or 20 clients the old logo with the name covered and ask what they recognize. I'd expect most to say the dog. I haven't seen your logo or any survey data, so that's an assumption. If recognition turned out to be low, I'd revisit the call, but refining is the safer default either way.

**Trade-off.** A redraw keeps the logo's personality, but it will look less corporate than a fully new mark. If your partner wants a "serious" look, I'd make the type and layout cleaner and leave the dog alone.

I can't promise the mark is unique. Have a trademark attorney check it before you print it on three storefronts.
