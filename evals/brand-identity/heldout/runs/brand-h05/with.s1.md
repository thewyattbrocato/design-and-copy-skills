**Refine it. Keep the dog.**

Twelve years of neighborhood recognition is the most valuable thing in your logo, and a new clinic in a new neighborhood will borrow trust from it. Your partner is right about one thing, though. The current file probably doesn't hold up on three signs, a favicon and a tablet-sized app icon. The fix is to redraw the dog properly, not to replace it.

**What I'd do**

1. **Redraw the dog as a clean, solid, single-weight version.**
   - Trace the original and keep what people recognize: the pose, the one raised ear, and the slight lopsided charm.
   - Drop the pencil texture and smooth the wobble into confident, even lines.
   - Don't straighten it into a generic vector dog. If it looks like stock clip art, you've lost what you were trying to keep.

2. **Make a heavier small version.** Pencil-thin lines vanish at 16–32 px. Draw a simplified dog with thicker strokes for favicons, social avatars and stamps. Don't just shrink the full logo.

3. **Check it in black on white, then reversed on dark.** If it reads in both, color is a choice you make last. Keep whatever color people already associate with you.

4. **Fix the wordmark.** The rounded font suits a friendly clinic, so keep that temperament. Choose a cleaner rounded face, tune the spacing by eye, and make one small custom touch, such as a letter that echoes the raised ear. Match its weight to the dog's.

5. **Build a set of lockups.** You need horizontal, stacked and dog-only versions, plus a rule for minimum size and clear space. For three locations, put the location name on a separate line under the logo. Don't build it into the mark.

6. **Test recognition before you commit.** Ask 15–20 clients at the front desk to pick the "same" logo from the old version and the redrawn one. If they match the redrawn version to the old one, you're done. If they hesitate, adjust the drawing until they don't.

**What to tell your partner**

The result will look clean and professional, and it will still be recognizably yours. Opening two more locations is when a known mark pays off most, because it's a head start you'd otherwise have to rebuild from nothing.

I'd only replace the dog if the survey shows people don't actually connect it with you, or if the redrawn version can't survive the small-size test. Neither seems likely from what you've described.

**Assumptions:** I haven't seen the current file, so this is based on your description. A real designer should work from the original sketch. Also, no logo can be guaranteed unique. If you want to protect the new version, a trademark search by a lawyer is the way to do it.
