**Partly true, but it's not an excuse.** Embroidery and tiny icons do have hard physical limits, but the fix is a purpose-built version of the logo for each use. Your designer should have made those versions before the files went out.

## Why it happens

Your logo is probably a single design drawn for large, high-resolution use. Both of these uses break that design for different reasons.

**Favicon (32px):**
- At 32×32 pixels (16×16 on many tabs), you have about 1,000 pixels total. Thin strokes, small text, and fine gaps get averaged into gray blur.
- Browsers shrink the full logo down, so nothing is *designed* for that size.

**Embroidery:**
- Thread has physical width, roughly 0.3–0.4 mm per stitch line, and fabric puckers and shifts.
- Lines thinner than about 1 mm tend to fill in. Gaps between elements smaller than about 1 mm close up.
- Text needs to be about 5–6 mm tall (roughly ¼ inch) to be legible, and even then only in a simple, sturdy typeface. A small tagline under that size will turn to mush.
- Gradients, shadows, and subtle color shading can't be reproduced. Each color has to be a solid thread.
- Someone has to convert the artwork into stitch instructions (called *digitizing*). If the embroiderer auto-converted your PDF or PNG, the results are often poor.

So your designer is right that a detailed logo can't be embroidered exactly as it appears on screen. They're wrong if they mean you're stuck with the result. This is a routine, solvable problem.

## What to do

You need three versions of the logo, not one:

1. **Favicon version:** Just the symbol or a single initial, with no tagline and no thin lines. Use bold shapes and high contrast. Design it at 32×32 and 16×16 directly, then check how it looks in an actual browser tab. Export it as `.ico` or PNG/SVG.
2. **Embroidery version:**
   - Simplify the logo: thicker lines, wider gaps, fewer colors (ideally 1–4 solid colors).
   - Either drop the tagline or enlarge it and set it in a bold typeface. Dropping it is usually best on a polo.
   - Have a **digitizer** produce a stitch file (usually `.DST` or `.PES`) from the simplified art. Many embroidery shops do this for a one-time fee, typically $25–$75. Ask for a **sample stitch-out** on a polo before ordering the full batch.
3. **Full logo:** Keep it for the website, deck, and print.

## Practical next steps

- Ask your designer for a simplified mark, a small-use version, and a one-color version. This is a normal "logo system" deliverable and shouldn't be a big project.
- Send the simplified art to your embroiderer and ask for a digitized file and a test sample.
- Keep the digitized file. Reuse it for hats, bags, and any future orders so you don't pay again.
- If your designer pushes back on creating these variants, a freelancer can do it in an hour or two.
