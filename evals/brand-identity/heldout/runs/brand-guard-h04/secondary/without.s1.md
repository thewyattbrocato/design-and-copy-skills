**I'd mostly keep it.** A 41-year-old name in a heavy slab serif, in safety orange, is a rare asset for a local hardware store. People recognize it from across the street, and it signals "we've been here a long time," which a chain or a new brand can't buy. A full rebrand would give up that recognition, and you'd pay for it in new signage, bags, and receipt stock, plus the goodwill spent explaining the change.

"Looks dated" may still be pointing at something real, though. Before deciding, I'd check:

- **Where the brand actually shows up.** If the sign is faded, the bags are flimsy, or the website is a relic, that's a condition problem, and it's fixable without changing the identity.
- **Who "dated" is for.** Your regulars may find the look reassuring. Your marketing hire may be picturing customers you don't have yet, such as younger DIY and renovation buyers. Ask whether you're losing those customers, and whether anyone has told you the look is the reason.
- **Whether the gradient icon adds anything.** I'd skip it. Gradients often reproduce poorly on bags, receipts, and vinyl, and they'd be the first thing to date.

If you want to modernize without losing equity, consider:

1. **Refresh the existing wordmark:** tighten the spacing, clean up the letterforms, and keep the slab, the caps, and the orange. Regulars will see the same store.
2. **Use a sans-serif for digital only:** a website, social profiles, or an app icon can use a cleaner face while the sign and bags stay as they are.
3. **Test before committing:** ask 20 to 30 customers, both regulars and newer faces, which version they'd trust with a repair job. A few minutes of this will tell you more than either of our opinions.

Two questions would sharpen the advice: is the hire's concern based on customer feedback or sales data, or is it a general sense? And is the current sign and bag stock in good shape?
