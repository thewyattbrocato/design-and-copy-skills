I'd lean toward not rebranding. The identity you describe is doing real work: a heavy, all-caps, orange-on-white sign says "sturdy, established, we know hardware," and 41 years of recognition is hard to buy back once it's gone. "Looks dated" is a taste claim, not a business problem. Before you spend anything, ask the hire what problem they're solving.

Some questions that would settle it:

- **Are customers confused or not coming in?** If people can find you and know what you sell, the logo isn't the bottleneck.
- **Is the weak spot the digital side?** If the website, social, or delivery apps look generic next to competitors, that's a real gap, and you can fix it there without touching the sign, bags, or receipts.
- **What does a rebrand cost?** Count the sign, awning, vehicles, bags, receipt printers, signage inside the store, and the loss of recognition during the changeover. Those costs are real, and the benefit is hard to measure.

If you want to take the hire's concern seriously without a full rebrand, I'd try:

1. **Keep the wordmark and colors.** Have a designer clean up the logo: fix spacing, make a crisp vector version, and make sure it holds up at small sizes and on a phone screen.
2. **Pair it with a modern sans for the secondary layer.** Use it for the website, emails, and social posts, where a lighter look can help without touching the sign.
3. **Skip the gradient icon.** It would fight with the heavy, flat, honest look that makes the current identity work for a hardware store.
4. **Test before committing.** Try the refreshed look on one thing, like a new tote bag or a web header, and see how regulars and new customers react.

My recommendation is to keep the core identity and let the hire refresh the digital presence. If they can name a specific problem the current look causes, that's the point to revisit the question.
