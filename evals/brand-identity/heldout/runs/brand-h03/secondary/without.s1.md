**What's going on:** Every element you listed is a common bakery signal (wheat, script, cream and pink, gold, "since" taglines), and the combination is what makes it generic. Each piece says "artisan bakery," so together they say nothing specific to Crumb & Co. The one thing that could be yours, the name, is the most understated part. It's also a playful name, and a script wordmark with a heritage palette makes it read as a traditional bakery instead of a witty one. I'm going from your description, not the actual file, so some of this may be off.

**What I'd change:**

1. **Make the name the logo.** Set "Crumb & Co." in a confident, slightly quirky typeface, bigger and bolder than the script. Drop the banner and the gold outline. Both are decoration that doesn't add identity and are the first things to break at small sizes.

2. **Own one visual idea.** Pick one thing that's specific to you and build the mark around it. Options:
   - The ampersand drawn as something edible, like a twisted loaf or a crumb.
   - A crumb or torn-bread shape as the mark, which ties directly to the name.
   - A simple monogram ("C&") for Instagram and stamps.
   
   The wheat stalk can go. It's the most generic element you have.

3. **Change the color.** Brown, gold, cream and pink is the default bakery palette. One strong color that's yours (a tomato red, a deep green, a cobalt) will stand out on a box or a shop front more than a fourth shade of tan.

4. **Cut the tagline from the logo.** Tiny caps won't read on a sign or a phone screen. Keep "baked fresh with love since our first loaf" for the box or the back of a card, where there's room. It's also generic. Replace it with something only you could say, such as a signature product, your hours, or a real detail about how you started.

5. **Build a small system.** Make a one-color version, a reversed version for dark backgrounds, and a square monogram for avatars. If the mark falls apart at favicon size, it's too detailed.

**A quick test:** Show a few people the logo with the name covered, then ask what the bakery is called. If they can't tell which bakery it is, the mark isn't doing its job yet.
