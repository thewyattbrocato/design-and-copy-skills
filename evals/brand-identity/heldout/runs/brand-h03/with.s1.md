**Why it looks like every other bakery**

Your logo is a stack of category clichés, and each one is common among bakeries:

- **Script wordmark in brown.** This is the default bakery signal. Script is also the first thing to fail small. On a 16–32px Instagram avatar or a favicon, the hairline connectors fill in and the name stops being readable.
- **Wheat stalk.** It's the stock bakery icon, and it illustrates the category rather than saying anything about Crumb & Co. It's also a second idea. The mark is "a name and a wheat drawing," not one thing.
- **Cream-and-pink banner.** The banner is decoration behind the name. It adds a third element and adds nothing the name doesn't already say.
- **Gold outline around everything.** The outline is a fourth layer. Gold lines are fine detail, so they disappear small and turn muddy in one color. They also won't survive stamping, embossing or a sign at a distance.
- **Tagline in tiny caps.** At small sizes the tagline is unreadable, so it works as texture. It also locks the line into the mark, so you can't drop it when space is tight. "Baked fresh with love" is the most-used bakery phrase there is, and "since our first loaf" is a claim without a date.

Together this is five ideas, with no single one anyone would remember. "Looks like every other bakery" is probably accurate.

**What I'd change**

I'm assuming you're a small independent shop, warm and approachable, and that you'd rather be remembered than look like a brand-name chain. I haven't seen the actual artwork, so this is based on your description. If the execution is better than the description suggests, some of this may be gentler.

1. **Pick one idea and say it in one sentence.** Your name has a built-in one: the *crumb*, and the "&". Something like "a bold, hand-cut wordmark with one small crumb detail" is a mark. Drop the wheat. A single quiet nod (a crumb-shaped counter, a torn edge on the ampersand, a bite out of a letter) will be more ownable than a wheat drawing.
2. **Customize the wordmark.** Choose a sturdy, friendly typeface with some weight, and make one custom change: the ampersand, a cut, or a ligature. A heavier form holds up on a box, a sign and a 32px avatar. Check that "Crumb & Co." reads clearly aloud and cropped.
3. **Remove the banner and the gold outline from the core mark.** Make the core mark flat and solid. The pink and cream can live in your packaging, as background colors and not as a shape behind the logo.
4. **Make the tagline a separate, optional line.** Use it where there's room, such as boxes and the sign's lower edge. Never lock it into the mark. I'd also rewrite it with something specific to you, because "with love" is what everyone says.
5. **Keep brown and cream.** If customers already know you by them, keep them as your recognizable color base. Make the color the system and not the outline. Choose one lead (the brown), one accent (the pink, used boldly and sparingly), and gold only as a foil or print finish, not as a drawn line.
6. **Build three versions:** horizontal, stacked, and a symbol-only version for avatars and favicons. The symbol-only version should be a heavier, simplified drawing, not the full logo shrunk. Test each in solid black, reversed on dark, and at the smallest size.
7. **Add a system around the mark.** The identity should survive covering the logo. A recurring graphic device drawn from the mark (a crumb pattern, a torn-edge crop), consistent photography, and fixed box and layout rules would do it.

**Refine or replace?** I'd redraw it, not start over: keep the name and the brown/cream/pink palette, and replace everything else. I can't judge recognition without evidence. The test is to show 10–20 regulars the logo with the name covered and ask who it belongs to. If most can't tell, the wordmark is carrying all the recognition and the rest is free to change.
