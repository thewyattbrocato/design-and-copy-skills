**Why it looks like every other bakery**

The logo is a stack of category clichés, so it can't say anything specific about Crumb & Co.

1. **It has too many ideas.** It has a script wordmark, a wheat stalk, a banner, a gold outline and a tagline. Each one is a standard bakery signal, and together they read as a template. Nothing in it could only belong to you.
2. **The wheat stalk is the trade's stock icon.** It shows what every bakery sells and tells people nothing about you.
3. **The cream, pink, brown and gold palette is the default bakery palette.** It's pleasant, but it's the first thing any bakery reaches for.
4. **The detail won't survive small sizes.** The gold outline, the tiny-caps tagline and the thin script strokes will blur at Instagram avatar size. Gold also goes muddy on a printed box unless it's foil. A thin outline around everything makes the logo fussy on a sign.
5. **The tagline is locked inside the mark.** It will be unreadable on most uses. "Baked fresh with love" is also a phrase every bakery uses.
6. **There is probably only one version.** I'm assuming this is a single full-color horizontal logo. It can't work as a one-color stamp on a box, a reversed sign at night, or a square avatar.

**What I'd change**

The name is your best asset. "Crumb & Co." is warm, a bit cheeky and specific. I'd build the logo around it.

1. **Choose one idea, and say it in one sentence without "and".** For example: "Crumb & Co. is a wordmark where the *C* of Crumb is a bite taken out of a loaf." Or use the ampersand as the single custom detail. Either is something a visitor could remember. Drop the wheat, the banner and the outline.
2. **Make the wordmark do the work.** Script is the most common bakery choice. Try a sturdy, slightly soft serif or a chunky display face with one custom change: a cut, a ligature or a reshaped letter. Check how it reads aloud, stacked, and cropped to a square.
3. **Test it solid black on white first, then reversed, then at 32 px.** If it works there, add color. Choose the color for a reason, such as one dark brown plus one accent. Keep the cream as a background, and keep gold for foil on boxes only.
4. **Make three lockups.** Make a horizontal one for the sign, a stacked one for boxes, and a symbol-only one for the avatar and favicon. The symbol-only version could be a monogram or the bite mark, drawn heavier than the full logo, not just the full logo shrunk down.
5. **Take the tagline out of the logo.** Keep it as an optional line on boxes and the website, and consider rewriting it in your own voice.
6. **Build a small system so the brand is recognizable without the logo.** Pick one color role, one typeface pair, one graphic device (a stamp, a pattern or a box-sealing sticker), and one photo style. On a pastry box, those matter more than the logo.

**Refine or replace?** I'd replace it. It is generic and it fails at small sizes, which are both reasons to replace. The one thing worth keeping is the dark brown, if customers already associate it with you.

**Next test:** Show 5 to 10 regular customers the logo without the name and ask which bakery it is. Then show them a new direction beside it. That tells you far more than my opinion or anyone's "it looks generic."
