Case study · E-commerce UX

Making returns something customers could actually start

I redesigned the returns flow for Kestrel Outfitters, a mid-size online camping gear shop. Return-request form completion went from 37% to 68%.

Role
Sole product designer
Team
PM (Daria), two engineers
Timeline
About 10 weeks, 2025
Client
Kestrel Outfitters

The problem

To start a return at Kestrel, customers had to email support. The way in was a “Contact us” form buried in the site footer, so before a customer could return anything, they first had to find that form.

I read through support tickets to understand where people got stuck. The most common complaint was not knowing whether an item was eligible for return. Customers were asking support a question the site could have answered, and the old flow only revealed the answer after they had already committed to the process.

Design goal: let customers start a return from where they already look for their purchases, tell them early whether an item qualifies, and cut every step that didn’t need a human.

The flow, before and after

The redesign moved the starting point into the account page and brought the eligibility answer and the shipping label into the flow itself.

Before

  1. Hunt for “Contact us” in the footer
  2. Fill out a general contact form
  3. Pick a reason from a 9-option dropdown
  4. Wait for support to reply by email
  5. Learn about eligibility and label later

After

  1. Open an order in your account
  2. Tap “Return an item” on that order
  3. See eligibility for each item up front
  4. Choose a reason in one tap, add a note if you like
  5. Get your prepaid label and drop-off map right away

Three design decisions

Decision 1

Start returns from each order, and check eligibility per item up front

I added a “Return an item” action to every order in the account page. Once a customer picks an item, the flow checks whether it’s eligible right away, instead of at the end.

Why: uncertainty about eligibility was the most common complaint in the tickets I read. Putting the answer at the start means people don’t invest effort in a return that can’t go through. Starting from the order also means the customer doesn’t have to describe what they bought.

Decision 2

Replace the 9-option reason dropdown with one tap and an optional note

The reason step became a single tap, with a free-text note for anyone who wants to add detail.

Why: in the old dropdown, most answers were “Other.” Nine options were adding effort without producing useful data, so I cut the step down to what customers would actually use.

Decision 3

Show the prepaid label and drop-off map on the confirmation screen

When a customer finishes the request, the confirmation screen shows their prepaid label and a map of drop-off locations immediately, rather than sending them by email later.

Why: the moment a return is confirmed is when customers want to know what to do next. Showing the label and the map there removes a wait and a dependency on finding an email.

Results

37% → 68%return-request form completion

In the six weeks after launch, the completion rate of the return-request form rose from 37% to 68%, a gain of 31 percentage points. Figures come from the product manager’s dashboard.

This is the one metric I have. I changed three things together, so I can’t say how much each decision contributed on its own.

Working together

I was the only designer on the project. I worked day to day with Daria, the PM, and two engineers over about ten weeks, and I owned the research read-through, the flow design, and the interface decisions above.
