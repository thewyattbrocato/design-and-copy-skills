Case study · E-commerce · Product design

Making returns something customers can actually start

I redesigned the returns flow for Kestrel Outfitters, an online camping gear shop, replacing a footer contact form with a self-serve flow that answers "can I return this?" before the customer has to ask.

Client
Kestrel Outfitters, mid-size online camping gear retailer
My role
Sole product designer
Team
Daria (PM), two engineers
Timeline
About 10 weeks, 2025

The problem

Starting a return meant writing to support

At Kestrel, a customer who wanted to return something had to email support to begin. The entry point was a "Contact us" form buried in the footer, so the first step of a return was finding that form, and the second was waiting for a reply.

I read through support tickets to understand where people got stuck. The most common complaint wasn't about shipping or refunds. It was that customers didn't know whether their item was eligible to be returned at all.

The core insight: the old flow made people commit effort before telling them whether the effort would pay off. Eligibility was the question on everyone's mind, and it was answered last, if at all.

Before

  • "Contact us" form in the footer
  • Customer emails support to start a return
  • Eligibility unclear until a human replies
  • Nine-option reason dropdown, mostly "Other"
  • Return label sent later by email

After

  • "Return an item" on each order in the account page
  • Customer starts the return themselves
  • Eligibility checked per item, up front
  • One-tap reason plus optional note
  • Prepaid label and drop-off map on the confirmation screen

Key decisions

Three changes, each aimed at a specific point of friction

1

Put the entry point on the order, and check eligibility per item up front

"Return an item" now lives on each order in the account page, right where a customer looks when they're thinking about a purchase. Eligibility is checked for each item at the start of the flow instead of at the end.

Why: eligibility uncertainty was the top complaint in the tickets I read. Answering it first means customers don't invest effort in a return that can't happen, and the ones who can return get a confident yes immediately.

2

Reduce the reason dropdown to one tap plus an optional note

The old form asked customers to pick from nine reasons. I replaced it with a single tap and an optional free-text note for anyone who wants to say more.

Why: the nine-option dropdown was mostly answered with "Other," which meant the extra choices were adding effort without adding useful signal. A lighter question is faster to answer and keeps the door open for detail when someone has it.

3

Show the prepaid label and drop-off map on the confirmation screen

Once a return is submitted, the confirmation screen shows the prepaid label and a drop-off map right away, instead of sending the label by email later.

Why: the end of the form is the moment the customer is most ready to act. Giving them what they need to finish the job right then removes a wait and a second place to look.

Result

Form completion nearly doubled

37%→68%

Return-request form completion rate in the six weeks after launch, up 31 percentage points.

Before
37%
After
68%

Source: product manager's analytics dashboard.

This is the one number I have, and I'd rather be clear about its limits than stretch it. It measures whether people who start the return-request form finish it. It doesn't tell me about support ticket volume, return rates, or customer satisfaction, so I'm not claiming effects on those.

Reflection

What I took from it

The biggest lever wasn't a visual change. It was reordering the flow so the question customers cared about most, "is this eligible?", came first. The other two decisions followed the same principle: remove the steps that cost effort without paying it back.

Working as the only designer with Daria and two engineers kept the loop short. Decisions could be made, built and checked quickly, which mattered in a 10-week window.

If I did this again, I'd agree on secondary measures with Daria before launch, such as support contacts about returns, so the story wasn't resting on a single metric.
