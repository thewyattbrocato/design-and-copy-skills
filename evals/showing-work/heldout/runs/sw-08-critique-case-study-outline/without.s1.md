The outline is thorough, but it's thorough in the way a junior portfolio is. For a senior role, hiring managers want to see judgment, impact, and tradeoffs, and this structure mostly shows that you followed a process.

## Biggest problems

**1. It's a process walkthrough, not a story.**
Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test is the generic template every bootcamp case study uses. Senior reviewers skim past it. Cut the diagram, or replace it with the process you actually ran, including the messy parts: where you backtracked, what you skipped, and why.

**2. There's no problem statement or business context.**
"Elevating the Wine Club Experience" is a vague title. What was broken? Churn? Skipped shipments? Support tickets? Senior designers frame work around a problem and a measurable goal. Add a short summary at the top covering the problem, your role, the team, the timeline, and the outcome.

**3. There are no outcomes.**
Nothing in the outline says whether the redesign worked. Even imperfect evidence helps: retention, engagement, task success, support volume, usability test results, stakeholder feedback. If you don't have hard metrics, say what you measured instead and be honest about it.

**4. "Users were frustrated" is the weakest line in the outline.**
It's the research section, and it says nothing. Replace it with specifics: how many people you interviewed, who they were, and the 2–3 insights that changed your design. For example: "Members skipped shipments because they couldn't tell what was coming next, not because they'd lost interest." Then show which design decision each insight drove.

**5. Your role and the decisions you made are missing.**
You never say what you owned versus what the team did. You also don't mention constraints, tradeoffs, disagreements with PMs or engineers, or alternatives you rejected. That's where seniority shows.

## Section-by-section

- **About me (#2):** Move it to the end or your portfolio's About page. The case study should open with the project. Eight years of experience is better shown through the work. Wine enthusiasm is fine as a one-liner if it gave you domain insight.
- **Background (#3):** Three paragraphs on company history from 2011 is too much. Two or three sentences on the business and the problem is enough.
- **Wireframes (#6):** "Lots of them" is a red flag. Pick 2–3 that show a real decision, annotated with why you chose that direction over the alternatives.
- **Final design (#7):** A six-screenshot gallery with no context is the most common weakness in case studies. Walk through the key flows and tie each screen back to a research insight. Show the before state too, so the improvement is visible.
- **Learnings (#8):** "I learned the importance of user-centered design" is a throwaway line, and for a senior candidate it can read as naive. Say something specific: what you'd do differently, what surprised you, what you'd test next.
- **Thank you (#9):** Cut it. End with outcomes and reflection, or a clear next step.

## Suggested structure

1. **Title and summary:** problem, your role, team, timeline, result
2. **Context and challenge:** what was at stake for the business and for members
3. **Research and key insights:** a few specific, evidence-backed findings
4. **Strategy and key decisions:** options considered, tradeoffs, constraints, and how you aligned stakeholders
5. **Solution:** before and after, with annotated key flows linked to insights
6. **Validation and impact:** testing results, metrics, and what shipped
7. **Reflection:** what you'd change and what you learned
8. **Optional:** a short bio or contact line
