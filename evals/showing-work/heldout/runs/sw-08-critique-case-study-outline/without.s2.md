**Short version:** the outline is thorough about what you did, but a senior role is hiring you for how you think. As written, it's a generic process walkthrough. Nothing in it shows your judgment, your impact, or the tradeoffs you made.

## Biggest problems

**1. There's no story or stakes.**
"Elevating the Wine Club Experience" could be the title of any project. Senior reviewers skim, so lead with a one-paragraph summary: the problem, your role, what you did, and the result. For example: "Members were churning after month 3. I led the redesign of the dashboard, which raised skip-management completion from X to Y."

**2. There are no outcomes.**
Nothing in the outline says whether the project worked. Include retention, support tickets, task success, NPS, or conversion. If you don't have hard numbers, use directional evidence like usability test results or stakeholder feedback, and say plainly what you couldn't measure. Without outcomes, the case study reads as an exercise.

**3. "Users were frustrated" is the weakest line in the outline.**
Frustrated by what? Name the specific insights, such as "Members couldn't tell when their next shipment would charge, so they called support to skip." Include how many people you interviewed, who they were, and one or two quotes. Most important, show how a finding led to a design decision.

**4. The process diagram is filler.**
Empathize/Define/Ideate/Prototype/Test is the generic design-thinking diagram, and every junior portfolio has it. Cut it. Describe your actual process, including where it was messy, where you went back, and what you skipped and why.

**5. The wireframes section will bury you.**
"Lots of them" shows effort, not thinking. Pick two or three key decisions and show the alternatives you considered. For each one, say why you chose what you did and what you gave up. That's the senior signal.

**6. The final design is a gallery with no explanation.**
Six screenshots with no annotation make the reader guess what matters. Tie each screen back to a problem you identified earlier.

**7. "Learnings" is too generic.**
"I learned the importance of user-centered design" is a line a junior would write. A senior would write something like "I'd push for instrumenting the skip flow before launch, because we couldn't attribute the retention change cleanly." Reflect on a specific tradeoff, mistake, or what you'd do differently.

## Smaller fixes

- **About me:** Cut it to two or three lines, or move it to the end or to your portfolio homepage. Your 8 years matter, but your love of wine is a nice touch rather than a qualification. Spend that space on your role instead.
- **Company history:** Three paragraphs on the company's 2011 founding is too much. Keep only the context that explains the business problem, in a few sentences.
- **Missing: your role and team.** State what you owned, who you worked with (PM, engineers, researchers), and how big the team was. For a senior role, include how you influenced stakeholders, handled disagreement, or made tradeoffs with engineering.
- **Missing: constraints.** Timeline, tech limits, legacy systems, and business pressure are what make a project feel real.
- **Cut "Thank you."** Close with outcomes and reflection, or a next-steps note.

## Suggested structure

1. **Title and TL;DR:** problem, your role, team, timeline, and headline result
2. **Context and the problem:** short, tied to a business metric
3. **Your role and constraints**
4. **Research and key insights:** 3 or 4 specific findings with evidence
5. **Key design decisions:** 2 or 3 decisions, each with the options considered, the rationale, and the tradeoffs
6. **Final design:** annotated, tied back to the insights
7. **Impact:** metrics, or an honest account of what you could and couldn't measure
8. **Reflection:** what you'd do differently, and what you'd do next
