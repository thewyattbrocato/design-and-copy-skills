# Critique of your Orchard & Vine case study outline

## What works
You have the raw material: real interviews, wireframes, final screens, and a reflection. Nothing is missing that you'd need to invent. The problem is the order and the framing. The outline follows your project's calendar, so a reviewer has to get through seven sections before they reach the part that shows how you think.

## Findings, in order of cost to the reader

1. **The work is buried.** The final design is section 7. Before it come a title, your bio, and three paragraphs of company history (founded 2011). A reviewer skimming for 60 seconds sees none of your design work. **Fix:** The first screen should have the name, one plain sentence about what the dashboard is, two to four concrete facts, and your strongest screenshot. Put a short paragraph on why the redesign happened right after that.

2. **The sections are process phases.** "Research," "Wireframes," and "Final design" tell the reader what you did, not what you decided. Senior roles are hired for judgment. **Fix:** Title each section with a decision and how you made it, for example "Renewal moved to the top of the dashboard, because [finding]."

3. **"Users were frustrated" carries no information.** This is your weakest section. It names no finding, no number of interviews, and no change that resulted. **Fix:** Fold research into the decisions it explains. Use the form "Interviews with [N] members showed [specific behavior]. So I [changed X]." Add a real quote only if you have one.

4. **The Empathize→Test diagram is generic.** Every candidate has this diagram, and it says nothing about your project. **Fix:** Cut it. If your process was unusual, describe that one thing inside the relevant decision.

5. **"Lots of wireframes" is a run of images with no claims.** Volume reads as padding. **Fix:** Show only wireframes that prove a point. The best use is an alternative you rejected, next to the one you chose, with a sentence on why.

6. **The six-screenshot gallery proves nothing in particular.** A gallery asks the reader to find the good parts themselves. **Fix:** Put each screen next to the claim it supports. Where possible, show before and after at the same width, with the old version's faults pinned to the spots where they occur.

7. **The "Learnings" line is the most generic sentence in the outline.** "The importance of user-centered design" could close any case study. **Fix:** Be specific and candid. Say what didn't work, what you'd do differently, and what's still limited or unshipped.

8. **Senior signals are missing.** There's no scope, team, constraints, tradeoffs, stakeholder pushback, or outcome. Those are what separate senior from mid-level. **Fix:** Add a short line near the top covering your role and who you worked with. Show at least one tradeoff you negotiated, and include a result.

9. **"Thank you" is a dead end.** **Fix:** End with limits, credits, and one action, such as "Let's talk" with your contact link.

10. **The "About me" paragraph is in the wrong place.** Eight years of experience and a love of wine are fine, but they belong in one line, or on your portfolio's About page. The wine detail is a nice touch if it's short.

## Rebuilt spine

1. **Orchard & Vine wine club dashboard**
   - One sentence, e.g. "A redesigned member dashboard for [platform], used by [N] club members."
   - Facts: your role, team, timeline, and constraint.
   - The strongest final screen.
   - One short paragraph on why the redesign happened.
2. **[Decision 1, with its means]**
   - Claim: what you changed and why.
   - Proof: the research finding behind it, then a before/after.
   - Caption: one sentence of fact the paragraph doesn't already say.
3. **[Decision 2]**
   - Include the rejected wireframe alongside the chosen one.
4. **[Decision 3]**
   - Make this the tradeoff you negotiated, such as with engineering, product, or the business.
5. **Result**
   - `[result: what you measured, how, and the number]`
6. **What didn't work and what's still limited**
7. **Credits and one action**
   - `[credits: names and roles]`, then "get in touch."

Keep the company history to one sentence, and the bio to one line.
