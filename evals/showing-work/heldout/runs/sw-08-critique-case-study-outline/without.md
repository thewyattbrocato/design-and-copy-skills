The outline is thorough, but it's the structure every junior-to-mid case study uses. For a senior role, hiring managers look for judgment, impact, and influence, and this outline doesn't show them yet.

## Biggest problems

**1. It's a process walkthrough, not a story.**
The Empathize/Define/Ideate/Prototype/Test diagram is generic. Every reviewer has seen it hundreds of times, and it tells them nothing about how *you* think. Cut it, or replace it with a short account of what you actually did and why you deviated from the textbook.

**2. There's no problem, no outcome, and no numbers.**
Nothing in the outline says what was broken, what changed, or whether it worked. A senior case study should open with a summary of the problem, your role, the constraints, and the result. Put it right after the title, not buried at the end. If you have metrics (retention, churn, support tickets, task completion, NPS), lead with them. If you don't, use qualitative evidence or a clear account of what you'd measure.

**3. The research section is the weakest part.**
"Users were frustrated" is the least informative sentence you could write. Say how many people you interviewed, who they were (new members, long-time members, lapsed ones), and what specific insights changed your direction. Then show the decision that followed: "We learned X, so we did Y instead of Z."

**4. Your role and your decisions are invisible.**
Seniority shows in the following:
- Tradeoffs you made and what you gave up
- Stakeholder pushback and how you handled it
- Working with PMs, engineers, and leadership
- What you cut and why
- Where you were wrong and changed course

None of this appears anywhere in the outline.

## Section-by-section

- **Title:** "Elevating the Wine Club Experience" is vague marketing language. A better title says what changed, such as "Cutting wine club cancellations by redesigning how members manage their shipments" (adjust to your real story).
- **About me:** Move it to the end or to your portfolio's About page. Don't spend the opening of a case study on it. "Love of wine" is fine as a short aside, but it doesn't help here.
- **Project background:** Three paragraphs on the company's history and its 2011 founding is too much. Reviewers care about the business problem, not the company's origin. Give two or three sentences of context, then the problem, the goals, and your team and timeline.
- **Wireframes ("lots of them"):** Volume isn't a virtue. Show two or three that illustrate a real decision, such as an option you rejected and why. Annotate them.
- **Final design (6 screenshots in a gallery):** A gallery with no explanation wastes the best part of your work. Tie each screen to a problem it solves and the insight behind it. Before-and-after comparisons work well for a redesign.
- **Learnings:** "I learned the importance of user-centered design" is a red flag for senior roles. Be specific: what would you do differently, what surprised you, what did you learn about influencing the team?
- **Thank you:** Replace it with a next step, such as what you'd do next, open questions, or a link to contact you.

## A stronger structure

1. **Title and one-line outcome**
2. **Summary:** the problem, your role, team, timeline, and the result
3. **Context and constraints:** brief business context and what made this hard
4. **Research and key insights:** sample size and two or three insights that drove decisions
5. **Key decisions:** two or three moments where you made a real tradeoff, with the options you considered and the one you chose
6. **Solution:** annotated screens linked to the insights
7. **Impact:** metrics, feedback, or what happened after launch
8. **Reflection:** what you'd do differently
