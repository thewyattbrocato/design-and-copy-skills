# Critique of your Orchard & Vine case study outline

## What works
- You picked a real, scoped project (a club dashboard) that a hiring manager can understand quickly.
- You're planning wireframes and final screens, so the work will be visible somewhere.
- You did interviews. That's real evidence, and it's the raw material for the decisions the page should show.

## Findings, in the order they cost the reader

**1. The work doesn't appear until section 7.**
A hiring manager skims. Your first screen is a title, then a bio, then company history, so they see nothing you designed. *Fix:* put the strongest final screen at the top, with a one-sentence definition of the project. For example: "Redesigned the Orchard & Vine wine club dashboard for [who: members / admins], on [platform], to [the problem it solves]." Give it two to four concrete facts, then the image.

**2. The outline follows the project's calendar, not your decisions.**
Background, process, research, wireframes, final design, learnings is the standard template, and every junior portfolio looks like it. For a senior role, reviewers want to see what you decided and why. *Fix:* find the two to four decisions the design stands on, and title each section with the decision and how you made it. For example: "Fewer steps to skip or swap a shipment, by editing in place." That's an invented illustration, so use your real decisions.

**3. "Users were frustrated" is the weakest sentence in the outline.**
It's vague, and it's the evidence your whole case rests on. *Fix:* name specific findings. Say what they were frustrated by, how many people said it, and what you changed because of it. Put each finding inside the decision section it drove, with a real quote if you have one. Pair it with the screen that responds to it.

**4. The process diagram (Empathize → Test) proves nothing.**
Every designer has this diagram, and it doesn't distinguish you. *Fix:* cut it. Show process only where it explains a decision, such as a rejected direction, a test that changed your mind, or a tradeoff with engineering or the business.

**5. "Wireframes (lots of them)" and a six-screenshot gallery are runs of images with no claims attached.**
A gallery asks the reader to guess what to look at. *Fix:* use the "claim, then proof" pattern. Write a short paragraph making one claim, then show the one or two images that prove it. Include before-and-after pairs of the old dashboard and your version at the same width, with the faults pinned to the old screen in a few words. Wireframes belong only where they show a decision changing, and not as a pile.

**6. The "About me" and company-history sections are costly at the top.**
Three paragraphs on a company founded in 2011 is backstory the reader didn't come for. Your wine love is a nice touch but not a first-screen item. *Fix:* give the background one short paragraph after the opening image. Move your 8 years of experience to one line near the top or the end, and your portfolio's About page.

**7. Missing: your role, the constraints, and the outcome.**
At senior level, scope and judgment matter as much as the screens. The outline doesn't say what you owned, who you worked with, what constraints you had, or what changed after launch. *Fix:* state your role and team in one line near the top. Add a result next to the decision it came from, along with how it was measured.

**8. "I learned the importance of user-centered design" is a generic ending.**
It signals junior, and it's something any candidate could write. *Fix:* end with what didn't work or is still limited, one specific thing you'd do differently, credits, and one action (get in touch). "Thank you" can go.

## Rebuilt spine

1. **Orchard & Vine wine club dashboard**
   - A one-line definition with 2–4 facts, then your strongest final screen. One short paragraph on why it was needed.
2. **Decision 1: [what you decided, and how]**
   - Claim, then proof (old vs. new, pinned faults), and the research finding that drove it. Add a caption stating a condition or number.
3. **Decision 2: [what you decided, and how]**
   - Same pattern. This is the place for a rejected direction or a tradeoff.
4. **Decision 3 (optional): [what you decided, and how]**
   - Include only if it's a real decision. Don't invent one to fill the template.
5. **What happened**
   - The result, how it was measured, and what you'd change.
6. **Limits, credits, and next step**
   - What's unfinished, who did what, and how to reach you.
