# Gridwalk offline mode case study

## Lead with these (first screen or two)
1. **Before/after screenshots.** A tiny grey icon versus a persistent banner with per-item sync states. A hiring manager gets the whole project from this in five seconds.
2. **The usability result: 1/5 → 4/5 understood what had synced.** This is your only outcome evidence, so put it up top. Label it as directional (n=5) so it reads as honest, not weak.
3. **A one-sentence problem framing.** For example: "Inspectors in the field couldn't tell what had saved and what hadn't, so they couldn't trust the app without a signal." Your interviews should back this up.

## Support (the middle, where you show how you think)
4. **Interview insights.** Distill the 6 interviews into 2–3 findings, each with one short quote. Don't summarize the Notion doc. Pick the findings that directly drove a design decision, and say which decision.
5. **The sync-conflict story.** This is probably your best material for senior roles. The engineer called it the hardest part, so show how you handled it: the constraint, the options you considered, the tradeoff you made, and how you worked with engineering. Paraphrase the Slack thread instead of screenshotting it. At most, use one short attributed quote with permission.
6. **Design evolution across the 3 versions.** Pick 4–6 frames that show what changed and why, such as v1 → v2 → v3 with a line on what each round taught you. Show the key states: offline, syncing, synced, conflict, and error.
7. **The usability test method.** A short box covering the tasks, the participants, and what you measured. Mention anything that limits the result.

## Compress to a line or two
8. **Competitor teardown.** Give it 2–3 sentences, like "Both apps did X, neither showed per-item state, so I saw an opening there." Only expand it if it changed a decision.

## Drop
- **Most of the 40 frames.** Link a Figma file or appendix if you want, but don't put them in the narrative.
- **Raw interview notes and the full Slack thread.** These are source material, not the story.
- **Any invented or implied business impact.** Don't add "improved efficiency" claims you can't back up.

## Outline

1. **Title and summary.** Project, your role, team, timeline, and a one-line outcome.
2. **Hero image.** Before and after side by side.
3. **Problem.** Who the users are, what went wrong, and why it matters (data loss and trust in the field).
4. **My role and constraints.** What you owned, plus offline and sync limits and the engineering dependency.
5. **Research.** Interview findings (2–3) and the competitor takeaway (1–2 lines).
6. **Design decisions.** Three or so, each in the form problem → options → choice → why. Include the banner, per-item sync states, and conflict handling.
7. **Iteration.** The 3 versions, with a few frames and what changed between them.
8. **Validation.** Test setup and the 1/5 → 4/5 result, with caveats.
9. **Outcome and what's next.** State that business metrics aren't available yet and say what you'd measure: support tickets about lost data, re-inspection rates, and sync-error recovery.
10. **Reflection.** What you'd do differently, such as a larger test and testing real conflict scenarios in the field.

## Things to check before publishing
- **Confidentiality.** Check whether you can show Gridwalk screens, inspector names, or the Slack content. Blur or anonymize where needed.
- **Test comparability.** Make sure the before and after tests used the same tasks and comparable participants. If they didn't, say so.
- **Your own contribution.** Be specific about what you did versus what the team did. Hiring managers look for this.
