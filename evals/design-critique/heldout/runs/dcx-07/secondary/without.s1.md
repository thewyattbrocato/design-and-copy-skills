**Ship Version B, with its copy kept and its styling fixed.**

An empty state's job is to get the owner to their next action. B does that. It names a specific walker, a specific time, and a specific place, so the owner can see what booking would look like. "Book Marta for Tuesday" tells them exactly what the button does. The "vetted and local" line answers the question a new owner will have, which is why there are so few options.

A is pleasant but doesn't do that job. "Your walks will show up here once you get started" doesn't say how to start, and "Get Started" doesn't say where it leads. The illustration and the "journey" line add polish but no information. An owner on an empty Walks tab wants to book a walk, and A makes them guess how.

**What I'd change before shipping B:**

1. **Make "Marta" real data, with a fallback.** If the founder typed that line by hand, it's a mock. Show it only when the app has the owner's favorite walker or a real open slot. Otherwise show a generic version: "Browse walkers near [neighborhood]." A hardcoded name that's wrong on launch day is worse than no name.
2. **Verify the claims in the copy.** "Photo halfway through" and "all vetted" are promises. If the product doesn't yet send photos or run vetting, cut those lines. Don't ship a promise you can't keep.
3. **Restyle the buttons to the design system.** Use the filled primary style for "Book Marta for Tuesday" and an outlined secondary style for "See other walkers." Keep the same hierarchy and wording. Default web buttons make it look unfinished, which undercuts the trust copy.
4. **Replace the dog emoji with the brand's paw mark,** or drop it. One visual language across the app matters more than one cute glyph.
5. **Keep the trust link readable.** It's the right content. Make sure it's still tappable at a comfortable size, not just small.

If you have time before Friday, put both versions in front of five or six owners and see which one they tap. If that's not possible, B's copy is the stronger call on its merits.
