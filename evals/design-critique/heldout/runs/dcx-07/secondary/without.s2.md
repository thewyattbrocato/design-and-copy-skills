**Ship Version B**, but rebuild its visuals before Friday. Keep the founder's words and drop the default-looking buttons.

**Why B wins**

- **It tells the user what to do.** A says "your journey" and "get started" without saying what happens next. B names a walker, a time, and a place, so the user can act on it.
- **It sounds like the product.** "Marta's usually free Tuesday and Thursday mornings around Pell Park" gives the user a reason to trust the app. A could be any app.
- **The buttons match the state.** "Book Marta for Tuesday" is the next step, and "See other walkers" is a real alternative. A has one button that doesn't say what it leads to.
- **The photo promise is a hook.** "She'll send you a photo halfway through" makes the walk concrete for a dog owner.

**What I'd change**

1. **Make the personalization real, with fallbacks.** Marta's availability has to come from live data. Define what happens when there's no open slot this week, when the user has no nearby walkers, and when the user is new. A new user with no history should see something closer to A: "Find a walker near you," with one clear button.
2. **Restyle the buttons.** Make "Book Marta for Tuesday" the filled primary button and "See other walkers" a secondary outline button, both in the design system. Keep the copy.
3. **Keep the visual frame from A, lightly.** Use a small, consistent brand icon or illustration instead of the emoji. The emoji works for a founder's prototype but reads as unfinished next to the rest of the app. Also check the type scale and spacing against the other tabs.
4. **Fix the trust line.** "Why only a few walkers? They're all vetted and local" answers a question nobody asked, and as a small link it's easy to miss. Move it under the buttons as a plain caption, such as "Every walker is vetted and local," and drop the question. Use it only if it's true and you can point to the vetting process.
5. **Check the claims.** Confirm the photo feature exists and that "a few walkers" is accurate for the launch area before shipping. Copy that promises something the product doesn't do is worse than generic copy.

**Caveat:** I'm judging from the descriptions, not from usage data. If you can test both with a handful of users before Friday, watch whether people tap the primary button in B. If there's no time, the reasons above are enough to choose B and make the changes.
