Going only on your description, the main risk is that the hairlines and gold color won't survive the smaller applications. I can't see the letterforms, so treat this as a checklist to test rather than a verdict.

**1. The gold has a contrast problem.** #c9a227 on white is roughly 2.4:1 by my calculation. WCAG asks for 3:1 for graphics and logos, and that is the usual bar for signage. Thin hairlines make it worse, because anti-aliasing lightens them further, so they may look washed out even on a screen. A darker gold such as #8a6d1a gets about 5:1 on white and still reads as gold. You can keep the lighter gold for decorative accents on dark or tinted backgrounds, but not for the main mark on white.

**2. The favicon will not work with this design.** At 32px, hairlines of any reasonable thinness will render as faint noise or disappear, and a tiny leaf above the "i" will be a smudge. Make a separate favicon: a monogram ("P" or "PT"), or the leaf alone, with heavier strokes and a solid background or shape behind it. Test it at 16px and 32px before you commit.

**3. The leaf needs a job.** At sign and cup scale it may read as a speck, so decide whether it's essential. If it is, make it big enough to stand alone at small sizes. If not, dropping it simplifies the mark for every use except the sign.

**4. Paper cups are a print problem.** Gold ink on white or kraft stock usually comes out duller than on screen, and hairlines fill in or break up on uncoated paper. Ask the printer for a metallic or foil ink if you want a true gold, and set a minimum stroke weight for print. Also test the mark when the cup is hot, since sleeves and heat can change how thin ink looks. Print a physical proof before ordering a run.

**5. The sign depends on the backdrop.** Pale gold on white will fade in daylight. A dark background (deep green, charcoal, or espresso brown) would give the gold strong contrast and suit a tea shop. You could also use a darker gold outline or a solid backing plate.

**6. Check script legibility.** Script with hairlines can look elegant, but at distance the letters "P," "y," and "b" often blur together. Look at the wordmark at arm's length, at half size, and in grayscale. If it reads as "Pemberly" only when you already know the word, increase the stroke weight.

**Suggested order:**
- Choose a darker primary gold and a bolder weight for the main wordmark.
- Create a separate favicon and test it at 16px and 32px.
- Decide whether the leaf stays.
- Mock up the sign on a dark backdrop, and get a print proof for the cups.
