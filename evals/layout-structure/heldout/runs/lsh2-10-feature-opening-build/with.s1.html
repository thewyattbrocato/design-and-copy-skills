The Last
Salt Barge

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

Words by Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

The barge comes up the Medway on the last of the flood, low and slow, her rust-red sail furled against the mast like a folded umbrella. Dawn is still a rumour over the Hoo peninsula. At the tiller, Maren Pollard keeps one eye on the mudbanks and one on the water, reading a river she has known since before she could read anything else. Below her feet, forty tonnes of grey sea salt ride in the hold, damp and glittering. It is the same cargo her great-grandfather carried, over the same stretch of water, for a great deal more money.

Nobody planned for this to be the last one. Salt barges once worked the estuary in dozens, hauling from the evaporating pans at the river mouth to the wharves and curing sheds upstream, and every one of them had a family attached. Then came the lorries, the bypass, and the long arithmetic of fuel and insurance. One by one the hulls were sold for houseboats or left to settle into the saltmarsh, where on a low spring tide their ribs still show, black and patient, like the remains of something enormous that simply lay down.

Maren does not call it nostalgia, a word she says belongs to people who can afford to leave. The barge, the Edith Ann, was built in 1911 and has been rebuilt so often that her owner jokes only the name is original, and even that was repainted in 1974. She carries salt because the salt must go somewhere, and because the alternative is a very good boat becoming a very expensive ornament. “A barge that stops working,” she tells me, “is already halfway to being a museum. I would rather be late and useful.”

Useful is a generous word for the economics. A run from the pans to the old wharf at Rochester takes eleven hours when the wind behaves, and pays roughly what a delivery driver earns in three. The tide, which once set the rhythm of the whole trade, now sets only the schedule of a loss. Her brother Dale does the sums each winter at the kitchen table, lays the figures flat, and says nothing. Then, each spring, they scrape the hull, tar the rigging and go out again anyway.
