The Last Salt Barge

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

By Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

Before the light comes up over Upnor, Daniel Pryor is already standing on the foredeck of the Marguerite, one hand on a coil of rope gone stiff with frost, the other around a tin mug he never quite finishes. The barge is ninety-one feet of oak and iron, tarred black, her red sails furled like a sleeping animal. Below him the Medway slides toward the estuary, brown and unhurried. He checks the water against a mark on the quay wall, nods once, and says, to nobody in particular, that it will do.

For three centuries, barges like this carried salt up and down the river. It was raked from the evaporating pans on the Hoo marshes, stacked in the hold in grey, glittering drifts, and delivered to tanneries, fishmongers and the kitchens of a dozen riverside towns. The trade fed families, built wharves, even shaped the valley's accent. Then came the lorries, then cheap refined salt from Cheshire, and by the nineties the pans fell silent. Pryor's grandfather sold his last cargo in 1994 for less than it cost to haul. Daniel has kept the boat anyway. His reasons are complicated.

This morning's cargo is eleven tonnes, bound for a smokehouse in Rochester and a gin distillery that likes to print the words "barge-carried" on its labels. By any sensible arithmetic it is a loss. The tide offers four hours of useful water, twice a day, and cares nothing for deadlines. A van could make the run in twenty minutes. Pryor's sister, Nell, who keeps the books, has a spreadsheet she describes as a work of fiction with a good ending. When asked what the ending is, she looks out of the window and changes the subject.

Still, the Marguerite goes out. She slips her mooring at half past six, the mainsail climbing in slow, wet heaves, and for a moment the whole crew — Pryor, Nell, their teenage nephew Callum, and a spaniel named Admiral — stands very still, as if the river might yet change its mind. It doesn't. The ebb takes hold of her. Somewhere behind them a gull complains and a bell rings on the quay, and the old machinery of the trade, long since declared obsolete, turns over once more and finds that it still works.
