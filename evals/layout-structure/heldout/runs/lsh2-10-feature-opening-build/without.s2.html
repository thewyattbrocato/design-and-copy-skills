Feature  /  Rivers & Trades The Long Read

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

Words by Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

Before the light comes up over the Medway, Tobias Venn is already on the hard, walking the length of a barge that is older than his father. The hull is the colour of wet slate. Salt crusts the hatch coamings like frost, and the tide, which decides everything here, is still two hours from turning. He slaps the rudder post as he passes, the way another man might touch a horse’s flank. “She knows,” he says, though what she knows he does not say, and the mud ticks and settles around us.

The Venns have carried salt on this river for six generations, from the pans at the estuary to the packing sheds upstream, a run of eleven miles that once took a crew of three and filled a ledger every week. In 1962 there were forty barges working the trade. Tobias counts them off from memory, name by name, as if reciting a roll of the dead. Today there is one. The Aldous May carries perhaps a tenth of what she carried in her prime, and on most trips, he admits, she carries it at a loss.

The arithmetic is not complicated. Lorries load faster, and the road runs straight where the river does not. A barge must wait for water, and water keeps its own hours: the Medway here rises and falls nearly five metres, and the channel only floats a loaded hull for a few hours either side of high tide. Miss the window and you sit on the mud until the next one. Tobias’s daughter, Imogen, keeps the books, and she can tell you to the pound what each of those missed tides costs.

Imogen is thirty-four and did not plan to come home. She had a job in a Rotterdam logistics firm, a flat with a view of cranes, and a settled opinion that sentiment was a poor basis for a business. Then her father’s knee gave out in the spring of 2023, and someone had to take the tiller. “I came for a fortnight,” she says, pouring tea from a flask on the afterdeck. Outside, the water is beginning to move. “That was three winters ago. Don’t let him tell you otherwise.”
