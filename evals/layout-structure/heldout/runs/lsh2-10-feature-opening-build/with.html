Medway Estuary

The Last Salt Barge

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

Words by Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minutes to read

The barge leaves before the light does. At four in the morning the Medway is a black sheet with a seam of mud along its edge, and the only sound is water working at the hull. Tobias Marlow stands at the tiller in a wool jersey older than his daughter, one hand on the oak, reading the river by feel. He does not use the radio. He does not use the chart. He says the river tells him where it is shallow, if he is polite enough to listen.

Salt was once the reason for all of this. For three centuries, flat-bottomed barges carried it from the pans along the estuary to the wharves upriver, where it cured fish, preserved pork, and sharpened the profits of men who never touched a rope. At the trade’s height, more than two hundred vessels worked this water. Marlow can name the last eleven, and the families who sailed them. He recites them the way other people recite prayers, quickly, without looking up.

What ended it was not a storm or a war but arithmetic. A lorry can carry in an afternoon what the barge carries in a week, and it does not wait for the tide. By the time Marlow’s father took the tiller, the wharves had been sold for flats, and the pans were rented to a company that preferred a motorway. The Marlows kept sailing anyway. Nobody can quite explain why, least of all the Marlows, who change the subject.

Their cargo now is about forty tonnes, sold mostly to a handful of bakers, two smokehouses, and a distillery that likes the story on its label. It does not cover the cost of the rigging, let alone the three people it takes to sail her. Marlow’s wife keeps the books in a school exercise pad and will not show them to visitors. “They’re not sad,” she says, finally. “They’re just honest, which is worse.”
