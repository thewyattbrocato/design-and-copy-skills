Working Waters

The Last Salt Barge

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

Words by Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

Before first light on the Medway, the water makes a sound like someone turning over in bed. Dan Pellow hears it from the wheelhouse of the Marguerite P., forty tons of Cheshire salt settled low behind him, the mud on both banks still wet and shining. He does not glance at the tide table pinned beside the compass. He has not needed one since he was eleven. “She tells you,” he says, one palm flat on the cold brass of the wheel. “The river always did. It’s the money that stopped talking.”

There was a time when this stretch of water never stood empty. Sailing barges queued below Rochester with bricks, cement, grain and straw, and salt came up from the north in coasters to be shipped on into the marshes, where it cured fish and kept winter beef from turning. Men were paid by the tide, not the hour, and nobody thought that strange. Today the quays are flats with river views. The Marguerite P. is the only vessel left on the estuary that still earns her keep by carrying salt.

Keeping her afloat has become a family arrangement, loosely documented and fiercely held. Dan skippers; his sister Ines keeps the books and the rigging; his daughter Tamsin, twenty-six, took a leave from a logistics job in Maidstone three summers ago and never quite returned to it. They share a rented shed at Upnor, a thermos of tea the colour of creosote, and a disagreement about whether any of this is sensible. On that last point, Ines admits, the vote has never once been unanimous.

The arithmetic is not complicated, only cruel. A lorry can haul the same load to the same depot in a morning, for a fraction of what the barge costs to run, and no one has to wait for the water to rise. Fuel, insurance, mooring fees and a surveyor’s bill that arrives like weather: Ines lists them without looking up from the ledger. “We lose a little on every cargo,” she says. “The trick is losing it slowly enough that someone, eventually, decides it was worth it.”
