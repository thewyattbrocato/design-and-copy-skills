Long ReadRivers & Coast

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

By Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

Before the light comes up over Upnor, the Medway smells of iron and cold mud. Dennis Pryor stands at the wheel of the Marguerite, a sixty-foot sailing barge with her tan sails still furled, and watches the water the way other men watch a bank balance. The tide is making. It will carry him upriver to the wharf at Aylesford, or it will not, and for forty years the difference between those two outcomes was the difference between a living and a loss. Nobody on the bank is awake to see it.

The cargo is salt, eighteen tonnes of it, coarse and grey and bound for a pickling works that once took a delivery every week. Now it takes one a month, when it remembers. Dennis loads by hand because the crane at the old quay was sold for scrap in 2011. He does not complain about this. He talks instead about the weight of the sacks, how the barge settles, how she answers the wheel differently when she is low in the water, like a person carrying something precious.

His daughter Imogen, thirty-one, works the foredeck in a borrowed waxed jacket and a pair of boots that were her grandfather’s. She left for Leeds at eighteen, trained as an accountant, and came home nine years later with a spreadsheet she has not yet had the courage to show her father. It lists every voyage since 2019: the freight charged, the diesel burned, the mooring fees, the repairs. The column at the far right is red all the way down.

There were once more than two thousand barges working the Thames and its tributaries, brown-sailed and flat-bottomed, nosing into creeks that no lorry could reach. They carried brick, hay, cement, grain, and the salt that kept a county’s fish from spoiling. Roads and railways ended that world within a lifetime. What remains is a few dozen restored hulls, mostly kept for charter weddings and school trips. The Marguerite is something stranger, a working boat that still, stubbornly, works. Dennis says that is the whole problem.
