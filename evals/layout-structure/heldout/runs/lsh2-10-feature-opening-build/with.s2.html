The Last Salt Barge

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

PhotographPhotographer Name / Magazine Name

By Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

Before the light has properly arrived, Tomas Ardley is already standing on the foredeck of the Marguerite Anne, one boot against the hatch coaming, reading the water. The Medway at this hour is the colour of pewter and moves with the patience of something that has never been hurried. He cannot say what he is looking for. His father could not either. Both men, in their separate decades, simply looked until the river told them whether to go or wait.

The barge is eighty-one feet of oak and iron, tarred black, her sails the rust-red of old brick. She was built in 1923 to carry salt downriver from the pans at Upnor, and for most of a century that is what she did. Now she carries it less often than she carries memory. Ardley loads twenty tonnes when he can find a buyer, which this year has meant eleven times, and each run costs him more in fuel and insurance than the cargo returns.

His daughter Imani, twenty-six, keeps the books, and she does not soften them. She sits at the galley table with a ledger that her grandmother started, columns in three different hands, and shows me the line where the trade turned. It was not a collapse. It was a slow slackening, like a rope paid out a hand at a time, until one season the figures no longer reached the bottom of the page. “We’re not losing money,” she says. “We’re buying something.”

What they are buying, I come to understand over the following weeks, is hard to name and harder to price. The salt itself is ordinary; a lorry could move it in a fraction of the time. But a lorry cannot work the tide, cannot slip through the shallows at Hoo on a spring flood, cannot make a village stand on the quay at dusk simply to watch it come in. That, Ardley says, is the real cargo. The rest is ballast.
