The Last Salt Barge

On the Medway, one family keeps a trade alive that the tide stopped paying for.

By Rhea Okonkwo-Hale 22 minute read

Upnor, Kent — At ten to five the mud is still the colour of pewter, and Tom Harker is already standing on the foredeck with a mug going cold in his fist. He reads the water the way other men read newspapers, quickly and with suspicion. The ebb is nearly spent. In forty minutes the river will hesitate, then turn, and the Marguerite will either be ready or she will sit another day. Nobody has paid him to be here for years, and he is here anyway.

She is eighty-one feet of oak and pitch pine, built in a Rochester yard in 1924, and she carries a cargo most people assume stopped moving when the railways arrived. Forty tonnes of coarse grey salt, shovelled from a quay at Grain, destined for a smokehouse, a tannery and a handful of road gritters. The sums have never been kind. Fuel, insurance and the cost of keeping her afloat swallow nearly every pound the salt earns, and the tide takes its own cut.

Tom’s daughter, Ayo, sleeps below with a laptop for a pillow. She is thirty-four, an accountant by training, and she can recite the barge’s losses to the penny: eleven thousand and forty pounds last year, nine thousand the year before. She says the figures aloud the way some people say grace, as a form of respect rather than hope. Then she gets up, pulls on yesterday’s boots, and goes to help her father haul the mainsail up its creaking mast.

The Medway has carried cargo for two thousand years, and at its peak hundreds of these flat-bottomed sailing barges worked its reaches, hauling bricks, grain, cement and rubbish to London and bringing back whatever the city had left over. Today the Marguerite is among the last that still earn a wage. Most of her sisters are houseboats, or restaurants, or rotting at moorings with ferns growing from their hatches. She is not a museum. That, her owners will tell you, is exactly the problem.
