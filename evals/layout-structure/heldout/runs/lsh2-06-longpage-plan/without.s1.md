# Ledgerlark landing page plan

## What the top of the page has to do

Within about five seconds, a visitor on their phone should know four things:

1. **What it is and who it's for.** Name the trades. "Bookkeeping for freelance electricians and plumbers" is a stronger filter than any clever line.
2. **What changes for them.** Lead with the outcome, such as no receipt pile and no tax surprise. Don't lead with the feature list.
3. **What it costs.** Put "30 days free, then $12/month" right beside the button. Skeptical, time-poor buyers want the price before they commit to anything.
4. **What to do next.** Use one primary button, "Start your free 30 days", and no competing links.

The hero contains:
- The headline, one supporting sentence, the button and the price line.
- A phone mockup showing a receipt becoming a categorised entry. This is your most visual, most immediate feature.
- One short customer quote, small, under the button. This is early proof without a testimonial section.

Put the video in its own section just below, not in the hero. Autoplaying video competes with the headline and button.

## Section sequence

| # | Section | Job | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hero | Who, what, price, act | Two-column: text and phone visual |
| 2 | Demo video | Show it working | Full-bleed band on a different background, with a short caption |
| 3 | The problem | Make them nod | Text only. Two or three lines in large type, no boxes |
| 4 | Feature chapter: **On the job** (receipts, mileage) | Capture without thinking | Large, alternating text and visual |
| 5 | Feature chapter: **Getting paid** (invoices with card, bank feed) | Money in, money matched | Same content, mirrored layout |
| 6 | Feature chapter: **Tax time** (quarterly estimate) | The payoff, with no April surprise | Single centred statement and one visual |
| 7 | Quote, big | Proof, tied to a feature | Pull-quote in oversized type, with name and trade |
| 8 | Spreadsheet comparison | Handle the "I already have a spreadsheet" objection | The one real table or side-by-side on the page |
| 9 | Two quotes, small | More proof, light touch | Compact side-by-side row |
| 10 | Pricing | Remove doubt | One plan, one big number, trial terms |
| 11 | FAQ | Overflow for everything else | Collapsed accordion |
| 12 | Final CTA | Repeat the hero ask | Short band echoing the headline |

## Avoiding the wall of boxes

- **Group the five features into three chapters.** Receipts and mileage are about capturing, invoices and the bank feed are about getting paid, and the tax estimate is the result. Five equal cards would be the wall you're worried about.
- **Give features unequal weight.** Receipts get the biggest treatment, since it's the most demonstrable. The bank feed can be a single supporting line inside "Getting paid".
- **Change the unit of layout.** Across the page you have a two-column hero, a full-bleed video, a type-only statement, alternating text and visual, a pull-quote, a table, a row of small quotes, a single price and an accordion. No two neighbours should look alike. If three sections in a row are a heading over a row of three cards, merge or redesign one.
- **Alternate density.** Follow every busy section (the table, the feature visuals) with a sparse one (the problem statement, the big quote).
- **Shift the background deliberately.** Use a different tone for the video band, the quote and the final CTA. Keep the rest light. Contrast marks the page's beats without adding any boxes.
- **Spread the quotes out.** One in the hero, one large mid-page and two small before pricing. Don't put all three in a testimonials block.
- **Use the FAQ as the overflow valve.** Anything that's true but not persuasive goes there, such as security of the bank connection, switching from a spreadsheet, cancelling and which tax rules it follows.
- **Write for the van.** The page will mostly be read on a phone between jobs. Design for mobile first, use short paragraphs, and keep the button visible with a sticky CTA on small screens.

## Things to settle before you write copy

- **The comparison needs real, defensible numbers.** For example, time spent per month or what a spreadsheet can't do, like snapping a receipt or tracking mileage automatically. If you can't back a figure up, compare by task instead.
- **The quarterly tax estimate is country-specific.** Say which tax system it covers in the feature copy or the FAQ, so nobody has to guess.
- **Is a card required to start the trial?** The answer belongs next to the button, and "no card needed" is a strong line if it's true.
- **Quotes should name the trade.** "Dan, electrician" does more than a first name alone, because the visitor is looking for someone like them.
