Vanishing trades

The last lighthouse keepers

Automation has darkened the watch rooms of nearly every beacon on the coast. A handful of people still climb the stairs each night, tending lamps that no longer strictly need them.

By Imogen Hartley · Photographs by Tomas Reyes

At 4:40 each afternoon in late October, Ansel Moray puts down his tea, zips his coat to the chin and starts up the 118 steps of Skerrin Head light. He does not hurry. The lamp turned itself on years ago, triggered by a sensor the size of a coin, but he climbs anyway, one hand on the cold iron rail, to look at the weather before the weather looks at him.

Moray is one of fewer than a dozen full-time keepers left on this coast, and by his own count the youngest by twenty years. The service that employed thousands a century ago now calls his post “a heritage presence.” He prefers “keeper.” It is a word, he says, that implies someone is waiting up for the ships.

“A light that nobody watches is just a lamp. I would like to be the reason it means something.”Ansel Moray, keeper, Skerrin Head
