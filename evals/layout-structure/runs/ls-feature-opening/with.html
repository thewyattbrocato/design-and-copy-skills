The last lighthouse keepers

The machines took over the lamps decades ago. A handful of people stayed on the rocks anyway, to watch the weather, mind the brass and keep a trade that was never really about the light.

By Marta Ellison Photographs by Tomas Reyes 14 min read

At 4:40 on a November morning, Anders Voss climbs the 112 steps of Harrow Rock light with a flask in one hand and a rag in the other. The lamp does not need him. A sensor switched it on at dusk, as it has every evening since 1998, when the last salaried keeper was handed a carriage clock and a ferry ticket. Voss wipes the lens anyway.

He is one of perhaps a few dozen people left who sleep beside a working light, and on paper none of them are keepers. Their contracts say “site attendant” or “volunteer custodian”. The sea does not read contracts. Salt eats brass in a single season, gulls nest in the gutters, and when the fog comes in thick, somebody still has to log it by hand.

“The lamp was always the easy part. The job was staying awake for everyone else.”

Anders Voss, Harrow Rock
