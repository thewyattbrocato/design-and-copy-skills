**Offer a trial, but not a free, self-serve 30 days for everyone.** Your cofounder is right that trials make sense for this product. You're right that the setup cost changes the math. The fix is to make the trial conditional on the things that make it work.

**Why the unconditional version is risky:** The trial doesn't really test your software. It tests whether the clinic gets its schedules into the system. If the office manager spends a day loading data, and then the clinic decides it's not worth it, you've lost a day of their time and a month of your support. Trials also tend to convert poorly when nobody has committed to using the product. Free, self-serve, and unowned is the most common way for a trial to end in silence.

**What I'd do:**

1. **Make setup your job for trial clinics.** Do the schedule import yourself, or sit with the office manager for 30 to 60 minutes while you enter it together. This cuts their cost from a day to an hour, and it shows you how long setup really takes. If it's still a full day with you helping, that's a product problem to fix before you scale trials.

2. **Require a named owner and a real schedule before the trial starts.** Ask for the office manager's commitment and a sample of their actual rota. No owner, no trial. This filters out tire-kickers cheaply.

3. **Use a 14-day trial with a day-7 or day-10 check-in.** Thirty days is long for a product where value depends on the rota running through a full cycle. Two weeks is enough if setup is fast. The check-in is where you find out whether staff are actually swapping shifts in the app.

4. **Charge a small amount, credited toward the first invoice.** Even a nominal fee or a paid pilot (say, one location or one team for a month) cuts non-serious prospects and gives you a real conversion signal. Make it refundable or credited if they convert.

5. **Define success before the trial.** For example: schedules loaded, at least one swap or time-off request processed through the app, and the owner agrees to a go/no-go call on day 14. Write these down so the end of the trial isn't a judgment call.

**On the two prospects asking:** Ask them what they're worried about. Usually it's "will this disrupt the front desk" or "will staff actually use it." Answer that directly, and you may find a paid pilot satisfies them. If they refuse any commitment or payment, they're probably not ready to switch, and you've learned that for the cost of one conversation.

**Tell your cofounder:** "Yes to trials, but the trial is for clinics that commit to setup and a check-in. We're making setup cheap enough that saying yes costs us an hour, not a day." That keeps the spirit of their point (low friction, self-serve where possible) without giving away the setup labor for free.
