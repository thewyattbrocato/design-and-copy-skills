**Don't say yes to everyone, and don't skip trials. Offer a guided trial, qualified and shorter than 30 days.**

Your cofounder is right that trials help. But "self-serve" is the wrong frame for a product where setup costs a day of an office manager's time. That setup is the real barrier, and a 30-day clock doesn't remove it.

**Why open trials are risky for you**
- **Setup is the drop-off point.** Most trial signups at clinics will start entering schedules, get interrupted by a patient or a no-show, and never come back. You'll count those as "trial churn" when the cause was friction.
- **Shift-swapping only works with the whole team on it.** Value shows up when staff actually swap shifts and request time off. A trial with only the manager's data entered shows nothing, so the trial can't prove the product.
- **30 days is too long and too vague.** Clinics have no urgency, so the trial drifts. Each schedule cycle is probably 2-4 weeks, so they only need about one cycle to judge it.

**What I'd do**
1. **Qualify first.** Take a 15-minute call, or have them fill out a short form with clinic size, current scheduling method, and who decides. Say yes to anyone with a real pain point, such as spreadsheets, a group text, or a paper board. Say no to tire-kickers.
2. **Remove the setup cost.** Offer to import their schedule yourselves from a spreadsheet, photo, or whatever they have. That's maybe 30-60 minutes of your time, against a day of theirs. Do this for the first 10-20 trials even though it doesn't scale. You'll learn what the import needs, and you can automate it afterward.
3. **Shorten it to 14-21 days, anchored to a real schedule cycle.** Set the end date at the start. Tell them: "By day 7 your staff should have done their first swap."
4. **Define success up front.** For example: 50% or more of staff logged in, and at least 3 swaps or time-off requests handled in the app. Check in at day 5-7. If they aren't hitting it, find out why while they're still engaged.
5. **Make conversion easy.** Ask for a card up front or ask for a decision on a set date. Keep their data, so that after the trial, leaving means abandoning a schedule that's already built.

**If you want to test it cheaply**
Run it for the next 5-10 prospects and track three numbers: the share that finish setup, the share where staff actually use it, and the share that convert. If setup completion is low even with your help, the problem is the product's onboarding and not the trial design.

**A fallback if you're short on time**
Offer a paid pilot, like one month at a low price with a refund promise. Clinics that pay even a little tend to finish setup, and it screens out people who aren't serious.

For your cofounder, the short version is that "free for everyone, self-serve" works when setup takes 10 minutes. At a day of effort, you'd just be handing out homework. A concierge trial keeps their yes and fixes the part that would make it fail.
