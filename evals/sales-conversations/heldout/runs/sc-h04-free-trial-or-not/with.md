**Don't say yes to everyone, and don't say no. Offer a trial that has a qualifying step at the front and a defined finish.**

Your cofounder is right that a trial costs you little. But "self-serve" doesn't hold here. The office manager's cost is a full day, and that is the price of the trial to them. A trial that is free for you and expensive for them mostly produces abandoned setups. Those leave you no signal, and the prospect remembers the product as "the thing that took a day and went nowhere." Your shift-swapping value also only appears once staff actually swap shifts. A manager who enters schedules and then waits 30 days with nobody using it has proven nothing.

**What I'd do**

1. **Ask what they want to be sure of.** A trial request is a request for proof. Ask: "What would you need to see in the first few weeks to know this works for your clinic?" Their answer becomes the success test. I can't give you a number for it, so write it as `[to learn]` per prospect, e.g. how many swap or time-off requests they handle now, how long each takes, and how many get resolved in the app.

2. **Check three things before you start the clock:**
   - **A felt problem.** Swaps and time-off are currently handled by texts, a whiteboard, or the office manager chasing people, and it costs them something they can describe.
   - **A signer.** Find out who approves a purchase, an owner or practice manager, and get them to agree to a decision date. If the office manager can't buy, the trial tests the wrong person.
   - **Their input.** They send their current schedule, and 5–10 staff get invited in week one.

   If one is missing, the next step is to fill it, not to start the trial.

3. **Remove the day of setup.** Have them send you their schedule as a spreadsheet, photo or export, and load it yourselves. Setup then costs them roughly an hour of checking instead of a day of typing. That is a bigger lever than the trial length, and it screens out nobody who is serious. Whether you can afford this depends on volume. At a couple of prospects it clearly pays for itself. If it scales badly, you can make it a perk for qualified trials only.

4. **Write down the terms and send them the same day:**
   - the success test they helped choose
   - start and end dates, with a checkpoint around day 10–14
   - a named owner on each side
   - the price they'd pay afterward and the decision date
   - what happens at the end, which should be opt-in only. Nothing converts to paid unless they say yes in writing, and you don't pull access to rush a signature. If they need longer, offer a stated extension or a read-only period.

5. **Consider shortening it to 14–21 days.** I'd base the length on how long it takes to run through a real scheduling cycle, which I don't know for your clinics. A 30-day window with no checkpoint drifts. If a prospect needs a full cycle to judge it, keep the 30 days, but keep the checkpoint.

**Where your cofounder is right.** If a prospect is small or has already decided, and the signer is also the office manager, skip most of this. Say yes, do the setup for them, and keep the end date. The gate matters most when the trial is a substitute for deciding.

**For the two prospects asking now,** the next step is a 20-minute call with each, with the signer on it if possible. Use it to get the three items above and agree the success test. End by putting the trial start date and a checkpoint date on both calendars, and send the terms in writing that day.

Two things to check: whether you can afford doing setup for every trial, and what a trial costs you per clinic in support time, which I can't see from here.
