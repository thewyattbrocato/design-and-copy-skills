**Recommendation: don't say yes to everyone, and don't refuse. Offer a trial that's shorter, set up with you, and tied to a success test.**

**Why "it's self serve anyway" doesn't hold.** The product may be self-serve to use, but it isn't to start. The prospect pays for the trial with a day of their office manager's time. Many of them will never spend that day, and the trial will sit empty. Then you'll have an unconverted "trial" and a prospect who feels they already looked at it. A 30-day window also doesn't fit your product. A swap or time-off tool only proves itself when real staff request real swaps, and that means the schedule has to be in first.

**What I don't know (please fill in):**
- Whether the person asking is the one who signs.
- Whether you can import schedules from a spreadsheet or their current tool.
- What your trial-to-paid rate is today, if you've had any trials.
- Whether these prospects are in a rush, for example a bad scheduling season.

**What to do**

1. **Ask what they want to be sure of.** A trial request is really a request for proof. On a 15-minute call, ask:
   - What goes wrong today with swaps and time off? How many requests a week, and who handles them?
   - What would make them confident enough to switch?
   - Who else has to agree, and who signs?

   If nobody feels the problem or can say who signs, a trial won't fix that. Use the call to get those answers first.

2. **Cut the setup cost yourself.** This is the biggest lever you have. Offer to load the schedule for them: a 30-minute call where they share a spreadsheet or photo of the current rota and you enter it. Their day of work becomes about 30 minutes, and you see exactly where onboarding is painful. If that doesn't scale, it's still worth doing for the first 5–10 trials, because you'll learn what to automate.

3. **Agree a success test in writing before you start.** Something the prospect picks, for example "at least X swap requests handled in the app by staff" or "time-off approvals no longer go through text messages." The numbers are theirs to choose, so I've left them as `[to learn]`. Also agree the following:
   - **Length:** I'd go with 14 days after the schedule is loaded, not 30 from signup. A clinic cycle is usually one or two pay periods, so check what fits yours.
   - **Who does what:** a named owner on each side.
   - **A midpoint check-in.**
   - **The end date and the price afterward.**

4. **Keep the end fair.** On the end date, ask for a yes or no. If they need more time, offer a stated extension or a read-only period. Don't cut off their access to hurry a signature, and don't convert them to paid unless they say yes in writing.

**Where your cofounder is right.** If someone just signs up without asking, let them. That's a normal self-serve front door, and you don't need a gate for it. The gate only applies when a prospect is asking you for a trial. For those prospects, a call and a loaded schedule is the product experience.

**A line you can send the prospects:**
"Happy to set you up. Setup takes about a day if you do it alone, so let's do a 15-minute call first. I'll find out what you most want to fix, and I'll load your schedule for you. Then we'll agree how we'll tell it's working, and when we'll talk again to decide."

**Next step to put on the calendar:** this week, call each prospect who asked and run the 15-minute call. Then the two of you can compare what they said and decide which trials are worth running.
