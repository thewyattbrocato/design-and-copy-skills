Your plan is organized around your product, but this audience will judge it by their workday. A tour of everything it does tells three dispatchers you're good at the product. It doesn't tell them it will fix their problems.

**Main problems**

1. **It's a feature tour.** Dashboard, calendar, routing, texts and app is the order your menus appear in, not the order their day happens in. People who dispatch 40 techs mostly remember whether you showed their worst moments handled well. They won't remember breadth.

2. **There's no discovery.** You have no stated idea of what they use today, what hurts, or what they'd need to see to say yes. Without that you're guessing which 8 of your 45 minutes matter. Email the head of dispatch today with two or three questions, for example:
   - What do you use now?
   - What's the most frustrating part of a typical day?
   - Who else weighs in on a decision?

3. **The intro is 5 minutes of you talking.** They've seen your website. Spend 90 seconds on it at most, then ask questions.

4. **There's almost no discussion time.** Q&A and pricing share 4 minutes at the end. Questions will come up mid-demo, and the plan has no slack for them. A good call has about 15 minutes of talking with them, not at them.

5. **The dashboard overview is the weakest slot.** Dashboards look the same everywhere and don't answer any of their questions. Cut it, or show it only as the place you land when the scenario starts.

**What I'd do instead**

Build the demo around one or two realistic scenarios in their world, and let the features appear as they're needed. For example:

- **Monday 7:30am, with a no-heat emergency call coming in.** Show how a dispatcher finds the right tech, bumps or reshuffles jobs, and sees the route impact. This covers the calendar, drag-and-drop, auto-routing and the map in one story, and it's the heart of the demo.
- **The customer and tech side of that same job.** Show the text notification going out, the tech seeing the change on their phone, and the status flowing back to dispatch. This replaces the separate notification and mobile app segments.
- **The "what if" moments.** Show a tech calling in sick, a job running long, or a cancellation. Dispatch leads care most about how your product handles the day going wrong.

Suggested timing:

| Block | Time |
|---|---|
| Discovery questions, confirming what you heard | 8 min |
| Scenario demo (dispatch, reroute, customer and tech side) | 18 min |
| Reporting and integrations, only what they said matters | 4 min |
| Discussion, objections, and who else needs to see this | 10 min |
| Pricing and next step | 5 min |

**Other points**

- **Integrations may decide the deal.** If they run QuickBooks or an existing field-service system, whether you connect to it may matter more than any feature. Find out in discovery and don't leave it to a quick slide at minute 40.
- **Use their kind of data.** Load the demo with HVAC and plumbing job types, about 40 techs, realistic addresses and a few emergency calls. Generic demo data makes the routing look trivial.
- **Ask about the budget.** The head of dispatch is likely a champion, not the buyer. Ask who else signs off and what would help them make the case internally.
- **Close with a specific next step.** Examples are a trial with their real schedule, or a follow-up for the owner or ops manager. "I'll send you some info" isn't one.
- **Have a fallback.** Keep screenshots or a recorded run-through of the routing segment in case the live demo or wifi fails.
- **Your fluency can work against you.** Because you know the product so well, you'll be tempted to show everything. Decide in advance what you will skip, and be willing to say "we can cover that next time."
