**Goal:** Learn whether walk-in and prep-kitchen refrigeration failures are a problem she feels and can size. This is not a pitch call. Don't offer a proposal or price on it.

**End on this next step:** Before you hang up, get a dated, owned next step. For example: "She sends the last 12 months of refrigeration repair invoices by [date], and we book 20 minutes on [date/time] with whoever signs off on service contracts." Confirm it in writing the same day.

**Known and unknown**
- Known (from you): 6 locations, ramen, she's the ops director, and she agreed to 30 minutes. You have no prior relationship.
- Assumed: she's the right person for this. Nothing says she owns vendor and repair spend or that she signs.
- Unknown: everything else, including how refrigeration is handled today, what failures have cost, and who signs. Anything you haven't verified stays `[to learn]`. Check her LinkedIn and the locations' public pages beforehand, and only confirm facts you actually find.

**Opening (about 2 minutes)**
"Thanks for making time. I sell prepaid maintenance plans for walk-in coolers and prep-kitchen refrigeration. I'm not sure it fits a chain your size, so I'd like to ask how you handle it today. If it's not a fit, I'll tell you. Does 30 minutes still work?"
Then confirm one thing: "I understand you run six locations; is that current?"

**Questions, in order (the tag is the number each should yield)**
1. "Walk me through the last time a cooler or walk-in went down. What happened, who got the call, and how long until someone fixed it?" *(hours to repair, who was involved)*
   - If vague: "Roughly how long, and how long did you need it to be?"
2. "What did that cost you: product lost, a closed or limited service, the repair bill?" *(dollars, lost service hours)*
   - If she has no figure: `[to learn]`. Don't suggest a number. Ask whether someone can pull it.
3. "How many refrigeration failures across the six locations in the last 12 months?" *(count per year, by site)*
4. "Who do you call today: a regular vendor, whoever's available, or each manager's choice? What do you pay per year, including emergency calls and scheduled service?" *(vendors, annual spend)*
   - Spend probably already exists, so you're finding where it goes.
5. "Is any scheduled maintenance happening now, or is it fix-when-it-breaks? Who tracks it?" *(visits per year, owner)*
6. "What would have to be true for this to stop being a concern? Why that bar?" *(target response time or downtime, plus her motive)*
   - Motive might be health inspections, food-loss budget, or an owner's expectations. Listen for who cares most.
7. "What have you tried or considered?" and "If nothing changes, what happens?"
8. "Who else weighs in on a service contract like this: owners, finance, location managers? How did you choose your current vendor?" *(signer, process, timing)*
9. "What should I have asked that I didn't?"

**Gate check (before any proposal)**
Before you propose anything, you need three things on the table:
- A problem she feels and can size.
- A signer, or a route to one.
- What she'll put in, such as invoices, a second stakeholder, or site details.

If one is missing, that is the next step, not a proposal.

**If it's a weak fit**
Say so on the call. Examples are rare failures, an existing vendor that already responds fast, or a landlord who covers the equipment. Then either shrink the offer (for example, cover only the worst site) or step out kindly. Don't enlarge the problem.

**Price**
If she asks, give a range and say what moves it, such as units, sites, and visit frequency. Fill in your real plan structure beforehand. Don't give a savings figure you can't back up.
