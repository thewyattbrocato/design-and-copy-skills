**Short version:** this deal is stalled, probably not dead, but treat it as unlikely to close until something changes. "We'll circle back in Q1" after five weeks of silence is usually a polite deprioritization. I can't tell you the exact cause from what you've written, but the pattern points to a few candidates.

## How to read it

**What went right:** Priya named a real pain (fuel and overtime), and two people saw the demo and liked it. Interest and pain were there.

**What's missing:** the facts you listed don't show any of these:
- **A compelling event.** Nothing forced a decision by a certain date, so the deal lost to everything else.
- **A budget owner or signer.** Priya and the VP of ops liked it, but you haven't said who approves spend. At an 11-truck distributor that's often an owner or CFO who never saw the demo.
- **An internal business case.** Priya felt the pain, but did she have numbers to justify the price? Without them, a quote just sits in her inbox.
- **A clear next step after the quote.** If the quote went out without a scheduled follow-up, the momentum had nowhere to go.

Other plausible causes:
- Sticker shock.
- Fear of rolling out new software to drivers.
- Bad timing. If they're a bakery distributor, Q4 is their busy season. "Things got busy" may be literally true.
- A competitor or a "just keep using spreadsheets" decision they haven't told you about.

"Q1" is also a vague, soft answer. A real intent to buy usually comes with a date or a named blocker.

## What's worth doing

1. **Stop the "just checking in" emails.** Two unanswered ones means a third of the same kind will do nothing.

2. **Send one email that gives her something.** Build a one-page summary using her own numbers: current fuel and overtime spend, a conservative savings estimate, payback period. Make it easy to forward to whoever signs. Frame it as "here's something to help with your Q1 planning."

3. **Ask one direct, low-pressure question.** For example: "Is the holdup timing, budget, or something about the product? Any of those is fine, I just want to know whether it's worth keeping this on your radar." That gets you the real reason far more often than a status check does.

4. **Reach the VP of ops directly if you can.** They saw the demo and liked it, and they're likely closer to budget authority. Keep it light and don't go around Priya. For example, send a short note that cc's her, or ask her whether the VP should be looped in on timing.

5. **Offer a lower-risk way in.** Examples: sign now and start in January, or pilot on 2–3 trucks. That removes the "we're too busy to implement" objection and still gets you a signature.

6. **Set a real Q1 plan.** Put a specific date on your calendar, such as the first week of January, and reach out then with a concrete ask. Move the deal out of your active forecast into nurture until you hear something firm.

If you get no reply to the email with the one-pager and the direct question, take that as your answer. Move on, and revisit in January.

## For your own post-mortem

Ask yourself:
- Did I ever learn who signs?
- Did I tie the quote to her numbers?
- Did I leave the demo with a dated next step?

If any answer is no, that's the lesson, and it applies to your next deal too.
