**How to read it**

This is a soft stall, not a hard no. "Circle back in Q1" with no date, no next step, and no named obstacle means the deal lost priority. The pain is probably still real. But the signal that matters is that enthusiasm was high through the demo and went quiet right after the quote. Something happened at or after the quote, and it's usually one of these:

1. **Price or ROI.** Nobody had a number to justify the spend, or the number was bigger than expected. Fuel and overtime are real costs, but "killing us" isn't a business case until it's in dollars.
2. **Someone else has to approve it.** Priya and the VP loved it, but that doesn't mean either of them signs. At an 11-truck distributor, that may be an owner or finance person you've never talked to.
3. **No compelling event.** The pain is chronic, so there's no deadline. Chronic pain loses to urgent work every time.
4. **Seasonal reality.** Q4 is peak season for a bakery distributor, so "things got busy" may be literally true. That's also why Q1 is a plausible time to restart.
5. **Fear of rollout.** Changing how drivers run routes is disruptive, and nobody wants to take that on during the holidays.
6. **The status quo or a competitor.** Less likely, but possible.

Two emails with no reply is a reasonable cue to stop sending "just checking in" messages. They add nothing and train her to ignore you.

**Is it worth doing anything?**

Yes, but only a little, and it has to be different from what you've sent so far.

1. **Send one more email that makes it easy to be honest.** For example:

   > Hi Priya, I know this is your busy stretch, so I won't keep nudging. Two quick things. First, if Q1 is the right time, I'll reach out the week of Jan 12. Second, so I can be useful then: was there anything in the quote or the process that gave you pause, or does someone else need to weigh in? A straight answer helps either way.

   This gives her a specific date and asks the diagnostic question. It also gives her permission to say "it's the price" or "our owner needs to sign off," which she can't say on a "just following up" email.

2. **Offer something that lowers the risk.** Examples are a 2–3 truck pilot, a start date in January, or a one-page estimate of savings using her own fuel and overtime numbers. If you don't have those numbers, ask for them. That makes the ROI case something she can forward internally.

3. **Find out who else decides.** If Priya answers, ask who else needs to be comfortable with this. If the VP of ops liked the demo, a short note from you to them, with Priya in the loop, is reasonable. Don't go around her.

4. **Put it on a real calendar.** Set a reminder for early January. Find a reason to reach out other than "checking in," such as a relevant fuel price move or a short case study from a similar distributor.

5. **Keep your forecast honest.** Treat this as maybe 15–25% likely to close, and don't count on it this quarter. If she doesn't answer the email above, that's informative too.
