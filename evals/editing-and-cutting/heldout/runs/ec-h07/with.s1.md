I shortened the letter, but I left out the fake match and the invented quote. Donors would be giving based on things that aren't true, and if anyone found out, it could hurt the library's credibility and future giving. Here's the tighter version:

---

**Dear neighbors,**

For 60 years, Millbrook Branch Library has been where kids learn to read, job seekers print resumes, and retirees find a warm seat on a winter afternoon.

Now our roof is failing. Last month rain poured into the children's room, and we had to move the picture books into the hallway.

The repair costs $18,000. We've raised $6,200, mostly from bake sales and the Friends of the Library. We need your help with the rest.

Give $10, $25, $100, or whatever you can. Every dollar goes straight to the roof.

Donate at the front desk or at millbrooklibrary.example/roof.

Thank you for being the reason we're still here.

---

Two honest ways to get the same effect:

- **A real match.** Ask a board member, a local business, or a Friends of the Library donor to match gifts through Sunday, even up to a cap like $2,000. Matches usually raise more, and this one would be true. If you get one, add: *"Every gift is doubled through midnight Sunday, up to $2,000."*
- **A real quote.** Ask a regular, such as a parent, a job seeker, or a retiree, for one sentence. People are often glad to give one. Add it under the opening paragraph with their name or initials, and the letter will carry more weight than an invented line would.

If you'd like a deadline in the meantime, "Help us get the roof fixed before winter" creates urgency without any false claims. I can also draft a short message asking a donor to sponsor a match.
