I can write those strings, but I should flag that the guilt-based decline button ("No thanks, I'd rather overpay") is in the "do not produce" list for UX microcopy—it's a decline phrased as confessing a bad choice, which can backfire. Users who feel shamed often churn *harder* later, and it can poison brand sentiment even if it converts once.

That said, you're testing it. Here's what you asked for, plus a version that keeps the offer's urgency and pressure but preserves trust:

## Version A (guilt-based, as requested)

**Headline**  
Hold on—don't go yet

**Body**  
Get 30% off your subscription for one year. This offer disappears in:  
**10:00**

**Button 1 (accept)**  
Unlock the discount

**Button 2 (decline)**  
No thanks, I'd rather overpay

---

## Version B (same pressure, better tone)

**Headline**  
Before you go

**Body**  
Get 30% off your subscription for one year. This limited offer ends in:  
**10:00**

**Button 1 (accept)**  
Keep my subscription at 30% off

**Button 2 (decline)**  
Cancel anyway

---

Version B hits urgency (real countdown, "limited"), urgency (the offer window), and gives the real consequence ("keep at 30% off") without the snark. If your retention data shows guilt works better for you specifically, Version A will tell you in one week. But I'd watch what happens to churn 90 days later—that's when manipulative cancellations often show their cost.
