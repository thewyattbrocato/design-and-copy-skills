# Wrenfield Plants voice guide

## The short version
Write like a knowledgeable person talking across the potting bench: plainspoken, specific, a little dry, never selling. State the fact, give the instruction, stop.

## What defines the voice

**1. Specifics over adjectives.**
Numbers, days, and counts show up where other writers would put praise. "Forty-two of them," "eleven are perfect, six are fine," "three weeks." Never "beautiful," "premium," "perfect for any home." If you don't have a real number or detail, leave the sentence out.

**2. Honest about problems.**
Mistakes and flaws get said plainly, and often first: the dead fiddle-leaf fig, the squashed ferns, "no, you can't save it." Don't spin bad news. Admitting it builds trust and is often where the humor comes from.

**3. Answer first, then the detail.**
"Short answer: no. Long answer: ..." Lead with what the reader most wants to know, then give steps. Instructions are short, direct, and in the imperative: "Water them on Saturdays." "Don't fuss with it."

**4. Dry humor, deadpan.**
The jokes come from understatement and repetition, never exclamation. "Not Sundays, Saturdays." "Unless it's raining sideways." One per piece is plenty. Don't announce that something is funny.

**5. Asides in parentheses.**
A small, offhand detail can go in parentheses: "(the courier stacked them)." Use sparingly, once per piece at most.

**6. Offers help like a neighbor.**
"Bring it in and I'll look." Invitations are personal and low-pressure. No "don't hesitate to reach out," no hard sell.

## Mechanics
- **First person, singular.** "I," not "we." Even for signs and newsletters, it's one person running the place.
- **Short sentences,** with the occasional longer one that runs on a comma or two. Fragments are fine ("Closed Mondays.").
- **Plain words.** "Came in," not "arrived." "Fine," not "excellent." Common names are fine, and so is saying so.
- **Contractions: yes.** Hours written as "nine to five," numbers under about a hundred spelled out in running text (forty-two, eleven). Digits are fine on signs if space is tight.
- **No exclamation points.** None in all four samples.
- **Brief.** A newsletter item is 2 to 4 sentences. A sign is one or two lines.

## Never
- "We are excited to announce," "pleased to," "proud to"
- "Premium," "beautiful," "stunning," "perfect for any home," "low maintenance" (say what the care actually is)
- Marketing calls to action like "Visit us to browse our full range"
- Exclamation points, emoji, hype
- Corporate "we" and "our selection"

## Quick test
Before sending, check: Is there a real number or detail in every sentence? Did the bad news come out straight? Could this have been written by any nursery? If yes, rewrite it.

## Rewrite

> The succulents came in Tuesday: thirty jade plants and twenty-five aloe. They're on the front bench. Both want a lot of light and not much water, so if you're unsure, wait another week before watering. Open nine to five, Tuesday through Sunday. Closed Mondays.

**Note on what I invented:** I don't know when the shipment actually arrived or where it's displayed, so "Tuesday" and "front bench" are placeholders. Swap in the real details. I also turned "low maintenance" into the actual care instruction (bright light, water sparingly), which is standard for jade and aloe, but adjust it if you'd tell customers something different. If you have a real flaw or story about this shipment, such as a damaged pot or a few that didn't make it, lead with that. It's the most "you" move available.
