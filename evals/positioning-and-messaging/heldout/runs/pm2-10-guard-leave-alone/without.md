I'd keep most of it. It isn't flat. It's specific and honest, and it has a voice: the kettle, the sewing machine older than either of you, "we say so and charge you nothing." Those details are what a repair shop's customers trust. "Premium" and "brand-y" language (*artisan, bespoke, timeless craftsmanship, restoring your treasured pieces*) would make you sound like every other shop and cost you that.

If your cousin's complaint is that it reads a bit like a list, here is a light polish that keeps your voice and changes the rhythm:

> **Tallow Street Repair** mends zippers, soles and torn linings on everyday bags and boots. We quote the price before we touch anything, and if we can't fix it, we'll tell you and charge you nothing.
>
> We're two cobblers in a small shop on Tallow Street, with the kettle on and a sewing machine older than either of us.
>
> Bring in the thing you're about to throw out. We'll tell you honestly whether it's worth saving.

What changed:
- **Split into three short beats:** what you do and your promise, who you are, and what to do next. It's easier to scan, and the last line now works as a call to action.
- **Broke up the first sentence:** it was carrying a lot, so the no-surprises pricing and the no-charge promise now each get room.
- **Left the personality alone:** the kettle, the old machine and the "honestly" are the brand.

If you want it to feel a little more premium without losing honesty, add one confident line about the work itself, like "Resoled boots, new zippers, linings that last another decade." A concrete claim does more than an adjective.
