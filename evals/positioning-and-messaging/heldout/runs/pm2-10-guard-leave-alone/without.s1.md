Your cousin's note is fair, but I wouldn't make it more "premium." The text works because it's specific and honest. It has the upfront quote, "charge you nothing," the kettle, and the sewing machine older than either of you. Brand-speak like "artisan craftsmanship" or "timeless quality" would replace those details with phrases every repair shop uses, and it would sound less trustworthy.

If it feels flat, the problem is rhythm and a slightly administrative opening, not tone. Here's a version that keeps your content and your voice but gives it more shape:

> **Tallow Street Repair**
>
> We fix zippers, soles and torn linings on everyday bags and boots. We quote the price before we touch anything, and if we can't fix it, we'll tell you and charge you nothing.
>
> We're two cobblers in a small shop on Tallow Street, with the kettle on and a sewing machine older than either of us.
>
> Bring in the thing you were about to throw out. We'll tell you honestly whether it's worth saving.

What changed:
- **Opening:** it's now about what you do, not the shop's name repeating itself. The name works better as a heading.
- **Sentence breaks:** the long first sentence is split so the price promise and the no-fix, no-charge promise each land on their own.
- **Paragraphs:** three short ones (what you do, who you are, what to do next) are easier to skim than one block.
- **Last line:** "the thing you were about to throw out" is a little more vivid than "the thing you're about to throw out."

If you'd like more personality rather than more polish, add one concrete detail, such as the worst thing you've ever rescued, or what you'll fix in an hour versus a week. That would do more for the "flat" feeling than any brand language.
