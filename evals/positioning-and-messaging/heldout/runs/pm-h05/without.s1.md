# Marrow & Co Voice and Tone Guide

## Why this matters
Clients hire us because we explain numbers without making them feel small. Our writing should do that before they ever call us.

## Who we sound like
We sound like a former GM sitting down with you after close, with your numbers on the table. We've run floors and kitchens, so we know what a slow Tuesday or a surprise invoice does to a month. We're sharp about the math and relaxed about explaining it.

## Four traits

**Plain.** Say it the way you'd say it across the pass. "Your food cost went up three points" beats "COGS variance was unfavorable." When we need a term like prime cost, we explain it in the same sentence.

**Specific.** Use the details of a restaurant: the walk-in, labor percentage, the produce invoice, Saturday dinner. Specifics prove we've been there. Words like "solutions" and "expertise" prove nothing.

**Respectful.** Nobody should feel dumb. No "as you know," no "simply," no sighing at a shoebox of receipts. Our clients are smart people who spent their time running a restaurant, not reading financial statements. Explain, don't dumb down.

**Direct.** Lead with the answer. Write "You're short about $4,200 in November," not "There may be some liquidity considerations."

## What we're not
Not stiff, and not forced-casual. No corporate padding, no restaurant slang for its own sake, no jokes at a client's expense. Dry humor about a situation is fine. Humor about someone's mistake isn't.

## Tone by situation
Our voice stays the same. Our tone adjusts to the moment.

- **Website:** Warm, confident, short.
- **Monthly reports:** Calm and clear. Headline first, then why it happened, then what to do.
- **Bad news:** Steady, no hedging, no drama. Name the problem, give the number, offer a next step. Example: "Labor hit 38% of sales in September, up from 33%. Most of it came from two extra Sunday shifts. Here's what we'd look at first."
- **Good news:** Glad, not salesy. Say what worked so it can happen again.
- **Quick emails:** Short, like a text from a colleague.

## Words
**Reach for:** "money in and money out," "what a plate costs you to make," "here's what changed," "we" and "you."

**Avoid:** solutions, best-in-class, tailored, leverage, unique needs, next level, exceptional results, stakeholders.

## Before you publish
1. Could any bookkeeper say this sentence? If yes, rewrite it with something only we would know.
2. Would a chef understand it at 4 p.m. without googling?
3. Does it say what we actually do, in plain verbs?
4. Read it aloud. If you wouldn't say it to a client, cut it.

# Rewrites

**Original:** "At Marrow & Co, we provide best-in-class financial solutions tailored to the unique needs of your hospitality business. Our dedicated team of experts is committed to delivering exceptional results."

**Rewrite:**
> We're five former restaurant managers who do your bookkeeping and monthly reporting. Every month you get numbers you can actually read: what came in, what went out, and what to do about it. No jargon, and no question is too basic.

**Original:** "Contact us today to learn how we can help take your restaurant to the next level!"

**Rewrite:**
> Not sure what your numbers are telling you? Get in touch and we'll talk it through.

**What changed:**
- The first rewrite swaps abstractions ("solutions," "experts," "results") for facts that only you can claim: five people, ex-managers, a monthly report that makes sense.
- "No question is too basic" does the work of "never make them feel dumb" without saying it outright.
- The second rewrite opens with a worry your clients actually have, drops the exclamation point and the "next level" cliché, and makes the invitation low-pressure.

I kept the rewrites free of claims I can't verify, like response times or free consults. If you offer something like that, adding it to the second line would make it stronger.
