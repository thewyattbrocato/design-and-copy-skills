## Homepage line

**Recommended:**
> **The right part, shipped within a day. A video to show you how to fit it.**

**Alternates:**
- Fix it this week. Parts ship from Ohio within a day.
- Your dishwasher's not dead. It needs a part. We ship it within a day.
- Replacement appliance parts, shipped in a day, with a repair video on every page.

I chose the first because it covers the three things a person with a broken dishwasher worries about: getting the right part, getting it fast, and being able to do the repair themselves. It also doesn't promise delivery by the weekend, which you can't guarantee for every address. If you'd like a more emotional line, the second alternate is the strongest.

The returns and brand limits belong lower on the page. The 30-day return policy works well as a reassurance near the buy button. The brands you can't carry are better handled with a plain note and a search that says so.

## Team doc: What Spindle is for

**Why we exist**
We want repairing things to be the normal response when something breaks. Too many products are built to be thrown away, and we make repair easy enough that people choose it.

**What the customer is buying**
A working appliance, soon, without a big risk or a lot of confusion. The mission is why we do this work, but the customer is buying a working appliance, so every decision should serve that first.

**How we settle priority arguments**
When two goals conflict, ask these in order:
1. **Does it get the appliance working and keep it working?** A repair that fails in three months teaches the customer that repair doesn't work, which is the opposite of our mission.
2. **Would we put our repair video on it and stand behind it?** If we wouldn't, we don't list it.
3. **Does it make repair easier or cheaper for the customer to start?** Price, speed, and clarity all count here.
4. **Only then: does it sell well?** Sales matter because they pay for the rest, but they don't override the first three.

**Applying this to knockoff parts**
Cheap aftermarket parts aren't automatically against the mission. A lower-priced part that works can be the reason someone repairs instead of replaces. What counts against a part is a poor failure record, not its price or origin. So:
- We stock a non-OEM part when its return and failure rates are in line with the OEM equivalent, or close enough that the price difference justifies it.
- We label it clearly as non-OEM, so customers choose with full information.
- We track returns and complaints per part, and delist anything that fails the second test above.
- We don't stock a part just because it sells. Sales without the first test passing are the pattern we're trying to avoid.

Note that our 30-day return window is shorter than the time many cheap parts take to fail. Return data alone will flatter bad parts, so we should also look at repeat purchases and support contacts after the window.

**Things we've accepted**
- We don't carry brands that won't sell to resellers. We say so openly and don't work around it.
- Some customers will still buy a replacement appliance. That's their call, and we don't pressure them.

**When this doesn't settle it**
If two people apply the tests honestly and still disagree, the founder decides and we write the reasoning down here, so the next argument starts from the previous one.

Two things I'd check before you circulate this: whether you actually have return and failure data per part (the knockoff rule depends on it), and whether "the founder decides" is how you want to break ties. I put it in as a default and it's easy to change.
