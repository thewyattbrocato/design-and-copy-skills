## Homepage line

**Fix it this weekend.**
*Replacement parts for your dishwasher, dryer, oven, and more. Ships within a day from Ohio, with a repair video on every part page and 30 days to send it back.*

Alternatives:
- "Your appliance isn't dead. It needs a part."
- "The right part, a repair video, and it's on its way tomorrow."

The mission is in the first alternative: it tells customers that repair is normal, so it makes the point without a lecture. "Weekend" is a promise about delivery, though, and shipping within a day doesn't guarantee a delivery date. If you can't back it up, use "Fix it fast" or "Fix it yourself."

## Team doc: How we decide

**What we're for:** Making repair the normal thing to do. A customer who fixes their dishwasher for the price of a part, and it stays fixed, is the mission working.

**Customer test:** Does this get someone's appliance working again quickly, and keep it working? Speed, the repair video, and easy returns all serve that. Anything that adds friction or risks a second repair doesn't.

**Rules for arguments:**
1. **A part has to fix the appliance and keep it fixed.** A cheap part that fails in three months makes repair feel like a gamble, and that works against our mission. Sales alone don't justify stocking it.
2. **Cheap isn't the problem. Failing is.** A lower-priced compatible part that holds up is fine. Judge each part by return rate, failure reports, and whether it matches the spec. Don't ban or approve a whole category.
3. **Check the numbers before we argue.** If a part has a high return rate or "didn't last" reviews, we drop it or label it plainly. If it performs, it stays.
4. **Be honest on the page.** If we sell a non-manufacturer part, say so and say what to expect. That keeps trust for the next repair.
5. **Some brands are out of reach, and that's okay.** We don't carry brands that won't sell to resellers. We say so clearly so customers can look elsewhere, and we don't fill the gap with questionable substitutes.
6. **When mission and revenue conflict,** ask which choice makes the customer more likely to repair again next time. That one wins.

The knockoff question is the one I'd settle first, since it's the one that comes up most. The doc takes a position: judge parts by how they perform, not by whether they're knockoffs. If your team would rather draw a harder line, such as OEM only or a stated failure-rate threshold, tell me and I'll tighten rule 2. A concrete number, like pulling any part above X% returns, would also end more arguments than the principle does.
