# Coldnote messaging doc

## Who it's for
Food producers who keep product in coolers and freezers and check it by hand: creameries, butchers, small caterers, farmers-market vendors. Most keep a paper log and read a dial thermometer.

**Not for:** anyone who needs a certified food-safety system, freezers colder than -25°C, or a cooler with no Wi-Fi.

## The line
**Coldnote texts you when your cooler leaves its range, and keeps the log so you don't have to.**

It replaces the dial and the paper log, which only tell you something when a person looks. Coldnote tells you at 3 a.m. Category word: *wireless temperature sensor and app for coolers and freezers.* Use that plain phrase beside the name every time. Don't use "smart," "platform," or any compliance language.

## How we describe it
**One sentence:** Coldnote is a small wireless temperature sensor and app that texts you when a cooler or freezer drifts out of the range you set.

**Proof we can use:** "The alert came at 3 a.m. and the walk-in was at 46°F. We moved the dairy before it turned." — Marisol Quen, owner, Quen Family Creamery.

## By buyer
Each version keeps the same claim. Only the emphasis changes.

- **Creameries and butchers (walk-ins):** Lead with the 3 a.m. alert and the Quen quote. Say up front that a metal-walled walk-in usually needs the $79 hub inside the cooler.
- **Small caterers:** Lead with the log. It replaces the clipboard, and the PDF export is there when someone asks for records. [add: a caterer's own words]
- **Farmers-market vendors:** Lead with price: $29 a sensor, $12 a month per location. Sensors need Wi-Fi, so this fits a home, kitchen or storage cooler, not a stall without a connection. [confirm how vendors would use it]

## The awkward bits
Say these plainly and first, not in fine print.

| Topic | What we say |
|---|---|
| Compliance | "Coldnote is not a certified food-safety system, and we make no compliance claim. It's a sensor, alerts and a log." |
| Cold limit | "Rated to -25°C. Below that, we can't guarantee it." |
| Wi-Fi | "Sensors need Wi-Fi. Metal walk-ins often block it, so they need our $79 hub inside the cooler." |
| PDF log | "Laid out like the temperature log forms used in Marlow and Dunmore counties. Everywhere else you get a plain PDF." |
| Cost | "$29 per sensor, $12 a month per location. The battery lasts about 14 months." |
| Size | "We're two people in Madison. Nine kitchens have used Coldnote in a pilot since spring." |

## Backed today vs. still needed
**We can back up today**
- Prices, the 14-month battery figure, the -25°C rating and the hub requirement.
- Text alerts when a reading leaves the owner's range, and the stored log.
- The two county-style PDF layouts.
- Nine pilot kitchens since spring.
- The Quen quote, used word for word with attribution.

**Still needed**
- Case studies and survey numbers. We have neither, so don't say "customers save X" or "trusted by."
- A second customer quote, ideally a butcher or caterer.
- Data on alerts sent and product saved in the pilot.
- Battery test results behind "about 14 months." [unproven: log from pilot units]
- Confirmation from Marlow or Dunmore that the layout matches what they expect. Until then we say "laid out like," never "accepted by."
- How often walk-ins need the hub across the pilot.

**150 words**

Coldnote is a small wireless temperature sensor and app for food producers who keep product in coolers and freezers: creameries, butchers, small caterers and farmers-market vendors. Most of them still keep a paper log and check a dial thermometer by hand. The sensor sits in the cooler and the app texts an alert when a reading leaves the range the owner sets, then keeps the log, which exports as a PDF. In Marlow and Dunmore counties that PDF follows the layout of the temperature log forms health inspectors use. Sensors cost $29 each, the records and alerts service is $12 a month per location, and the battery lasts about 14 months. Coldnote is not a certified food-safety system and makes no compliance claim. It is a sensor, alerts and a log. It is made by two people in Madison, and nine kitchens have used it in a pilot since spring.
