The politely-nodding usually means the description is too abstract. "Helps bookstores run events" is accurate, but people can't picture what you do. Naming the concrete pain works better.

I don't know exactly what your product does, so I'm guessing it's software. Correct me if it isn't.

**Out loud (pick whichever sounds most like you):**

1. "You know how indie bookstores host readings and book clubs? We make the tool that handles the sign-ups, reminders, and book sales so the owner isn't doing it all from a spreadsheet and their inbox."
2. "We help independent bookstores fill the chairs at their events."
3. "Every indie bookstore runs events, and most of them run them on sticky notes and email. We replace that."

Option 1 is the most concrete, so it should get the fewest follow-up questions. Option 2 is the shortest, but only use it if filling seats is really your main value.

**For the site:**

> **Run your bookstore's events without the scramble.**
> Readings, book clubs, and signings, all in one place. Announce them, take RSVPs, remind people to come, and sell the book. [Your product name] gives independent bookstores the event tools big retailers have, built for a staff of three.

**Why this works better:**
- It starts with something people can picture (a reading, a book club) before saying what you do.
- It names the old way (spreadsheets, email, sticky notes), so listeners recognize the problem right away.
- It avoids words like "platform," "solution," and "streamline," which are what make people nod and tune out.

- What did stores do before they used you?
- Has a store ever described your product in their own words? Those descriptions often beat anything we'd write.
