**No, the chatbot interviews shouldn't go in as quotes next to your four real ones.** Here's why, and what to do instead.

**Why not**
- The 12 "interviews" aren't evidence of anything a customer thinks, did or would do. No pet owner said those words. The chatbot produced plausible text from patterns in its training data. It reads well because it's fluent, not because it's grounded in real experience.
- Leadership will read a quote in a research summary as something a person said. Put next to your four real ones, the fake ones are indistinguishable and dilute them. Twelve invented voices would also outnumber your four real ones three to one.
- Leadership could make pricing or product decisions on those quotes. If someone later finds out they were generated, it damages trust in the whole summary, including your real interviews.

**What the summary can use instead**
1. **Your four interviews as the evidence base.** Report them honestly as "4 of 4" or "3 of 4", not percentages. State that it's a small, early sample and say how those people were recruited.
2. **Existing evidence, if any.** Look at support tickets, sales or enrollment call notes, reviews, search queries and quote-abandonment data for the pet plan. All of that is real behavior and costs little.
3. **The chatbot output, if the lead wants to keep it, as a labeled hypothesis source.** It's fine for brainstorming what to ask next or what themes to check. Put it in a separate clearly marked section, for example: "Hypotheses to test (AI-generated, not customer data)". Don't use quotation marks or attribute it to "pet owners". Phrase these as questions, like "Do owners worry about pre-existing conditions being excluded?"
4. **A next step.** Propose a small round of real interviews, say 5 to 8 more pet owners, recruited by what they did recently (for example, a vet visit or a coverage search in the last few months). Use the chatbot's themes to shape the interview guide. That turns her work into something useful, and someone on your side has to recruit and run the sessions.

**Handling it without a fight**
- Present it as protecting her work and the credibility of the summary, not as rejecting it. You could say: "These are useful for deciding what to ask. Since leadership will read quotes as customer voice, I'd like to keep them in a hypotheses section and add a few more real interviews to confirm the themes."
- If she objects that they match your real interviews, that's a point in favor of the approach. Any theme that shows up in both can be listed as "worth validating", and the real interviews carry the weight.
- If there's disagreement, ask the leadership audience's question: "If someone asked who said this, what would we say?"
