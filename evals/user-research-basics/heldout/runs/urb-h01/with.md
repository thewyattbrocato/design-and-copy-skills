**Assumptions:** you have booking data and email access to past customers, a support inbox, and checkout analytics. You have no existing interviews on this topic.

## Reframe "validate" for the VP

Two weeks and no budget can't tell you whether customers will buy it. Asking "would you buy this?" gets polite yeses that don't predict behavior. You can find out three other things:

1. Whether owners have a real, recurring problem that insurance solves (vet bills or incidents during a stay).
2. Whether the concept makes sense to them at checkout.
3. Whether it's feasible, since selling insurance usually means a licensed partner or regulatory limits.

The decision for the planning meeting is whether to invest in a real test, and what to test. It is not whether to build.

## Days 1–4: use what you already have (you, no designer)

- **Support tickets, reviews, and dispute or refund records:** look for injuries, illness, escapes, property damage, vet costs, and who paid. Count them as "n of N tickets reviewed", and note that people who write in are not a typical customer.
- **Existing coverage:** check whether Kestrel already has a sitter guarantee or protection policy. If it does, the add-on overlaps with it, and that's the first thing the VP needs to know.
- **Checkout analytics:** find the drop-off at checkout and the average booking value. A paid add-on has to fit into the price sensitivity you already see.
- **Feasibility, 1–2 calls:** talk to legal or compliance, plus one embedded-insurance partner if you can find a contact. This is the cheapest way to kill the idea early, so do it now.

## Days 3–12: 5–6 owner conversations plus 2–3 sitters

You run these. The designer takes notes, because the person who designed the mock shouldn't facilitate its test.

**Recruit from booking data, with no mention of insurance.**
- **Owners:** people who booked in the last 90 days. Include at least one who had an incident or complaint, one with a puppy or senior dog, and one repeat booker.
- **Sitters:** they handle incidents first-hand.
- **Invitation:** frame it as "30 minutes about how you arrange care for your dog."
- **Incentive:** with no budget, ask marketing for a small booking credit. Otherwise expect a slower response rate.
- **Who this leaves out:** people who looked and didn't book. Pull a few of them from abandoned checkouts if you can.

**Guide, 30 minutes:**
1. Walk me through the last time you booked a sitter.
2. What were you worried about, and what did you do about it?
3. Tell me about the last time your dog was sick or hurt. What happened, what did it cost, and who handled it?
4. Do you have pet insurance today? How did you choose it?
5. At the end only, show a mock checkout screen with the add-on. Ask them to explain in their own words what it covers and what they'd do next. You're testing comprehension and trust, not intent.

**Designer's 3 hours a week:**
- **Week 1:** about 1.5 hours on the single mock checkout screen, plus about 1.5 hours reviewing the guide and joining the first session.
- **Week 2:** note-taking at the remaining sessions.

## Day 13–14: write it up for the meeting

Keep it to one page:
- **Findings:** each tied to its source and given as "n of N" (for example, "3 of 6 owners"). Don't use percentages at this sample size.
- **What's still unknown:** mainly willingness to pay.
- **Recommendation and cheapest next step:** most likely a labeled fake-door or waitlist at checkout, once legal agrees. It needs a pre-stated metric and whole weeks of traffic. A few days and hundreds of visitors is only a lead.

## What you have to do yourself

- Send the invites.
- Get consent for recording and for any observers.
- Run the sessions.
- Get legal's view before showing customers anything that looks like an offer.

Tell your VP now that this plan produces evidence for a go or no-go on a real test, not a demand forecast. That keeps the two-week readout from being over-read.
