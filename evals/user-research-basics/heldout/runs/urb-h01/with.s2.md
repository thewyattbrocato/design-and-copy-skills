# Plan: pet-insurance add-on, two weeks

**Assumptions:** you have no existing interviews on this topic, you can email past bookers, and you have some checkout traffic. Change the plan if any of those is wrong.

## 1. Reframe "validate" with your VP (today, 15 min)

Customer conversations can't prove people will pay. People overstate what they'd buy. In two weeks you can answer three narrower questions:

1. **Is there a real problem?** Do bookers worry about injury, illness, or vet bills during a stay, and does it affect how they book?
2. **Is checkout the right moment?** What do they do today when something goes wrong, and what already reassures them (sitter reviews, a Kestrel guarantee, their own pet insurance)?
3. **Does anyone act on the offer?** A fake-door test can show this, if traffic allows.

**Decision this feeds:** whether to put the add-on on the roadmap and spend partner and legal effort on it. Writing that down gives the planning meeting something concrete.

**Result that would change the plan:**
- If bookers rarely describe vet or injury incidents or worry about them, and a fake-door shows little interest, deprioritize.
- If you hear recent real incidents, or many people already have their own pet insurance, rethink the offer.

## 2. Mine what you already have (days 1–3, you, no designer)

- Support tickets, reviews, and chat logs mentioning injury, vet, illness, escape, damage, refund, or "who pays".
- Whether Kestrel already offers a guarantee or reimbursement policy, and how often it's used. Support or finance should know.
- Checkout analytics: drop-off, average booking value, and repeat-booking rate. Add-on pricing has to be small against booking value.
- Count incidents as "n of N tickets" and note the bias: tickets come from people who write in.

## 3. Talk to 6–8 recent bookers (days 3–10)

- **Who:** people who completed a booking in the last 30–60 days. Include at least 2 who booked more than once and 2 who booked for a dog with a known health issue or age concern. Say who this leaves out: people who abandoned checkout and people who never booked.
- **Recruit:** a short email from you to recent bookers asking for a 20-minute call about "how you arrange care when you travel". Don't mention insurance. If there's any way to offer a small booking credit, ask for approval. Otherwise expect a lower response rate and send more invitations.
- **Add 2 sitters** if you can. They hear first about incidents and know what owners ask.
- **Who runs it:** you. Use the designer for notes or the mock in section 4, not facilitation.
- **Consent:** ask permission to record and say who will hear it.

**Guide (stories, not wants):**
1. Tell me about the last time you booked a sitter. Walk me through it.
2. What, if anything, made you hesitate before booking?
3. Has a dog of yours ever been hurt or sick while someone else cared for it? What happened, and what did you do?
4. Do you have anything in place today for vet costs, such as pet insurance, savings, or a card? How did you choose it?
5. Last time you were at checkout, what were you thinking about? (Probe: what else did you consider paying for?)
6. Who did you ask or what did you check before you trusted this sitter?

Don't ask "would you buy this?" or "do you like this idea?" Show nothing until the end. If you want a reaction to the concept, show the mock only after question 6, and treat the reaction as a weak signal.

Stop when new calls stop changing your conclusions. If after 6 they still do, add 2 more.

## 4. Fake-door test (week 2, uses the designer's ~3 hrs)

- **Designer, ~2 hrs:** one checkout row, "Add pet coverage for $X", with a click-through to an honest page: "Not available yet. Leave your email to hear when it is."
- **Engineering:** ask for a small flag and event tracking, a few hours at most.
- **Pre-state one metric:** the share of checkouts that click the add-on.
- **Run it for whole weeks and don't peek early.** With a few days of traffic and a few hundred checkouts, treat the result as a lead, not proof. Never present a percentage from a small sample as a finding.
- **Check with legal first.** Insurance is regulated, so even showing a price may need review. If you can't get a yes within the time, skip this and say so.

## 5. Output for the planning meeting (days 11–14)

One page:
- What the tickets and calls showed, as "n of N" with the sample's bias.
- What's still unknown: price tolerance, which underwriting partner, the regulatory path, and whether non-bookers behave differently.
- A recommendation: pursue, pause, or test a cheaper alternative, such as a clearer guarantee on the booking page.
- The next cheapest check if the answer is "maybe".
