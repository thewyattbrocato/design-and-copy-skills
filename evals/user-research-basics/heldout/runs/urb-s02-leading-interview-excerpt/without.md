## Line by line

**1. "right, and that gets pretty chaotic I imagine?"**
This puts your assumption (paper is chaotic) in his mouth. He agreed weakly ("sometimes, yeah"), which suggests it isn't a big pain for him.
- **Instead:** "Tell me about the last time something went wrong with your diary or bookings." Or "What's the hardest part of that process?"

**2. "we've got a feature where clients pick a slot themselves... would you use that?"**
This has three problems:
- You pitched a solution before you understood his problem.
- "Would you use that?" is a hypothetical, and people say yes to hypotheticals to be polite.
- You skipped over how clients currently choose times, how often he reschedules, and how he plans his driving.
- **Instead:** "Walk me through the last time a client needed to change or pick a time." Then: "How do you decide what order to do visits in on a given day?" Save concepts for later, and ask for past behavior, not future intent.

**3. "it uses your home address and adds travel time automatically, so you never double-book."**
You answered his question with a sales pitch. "So you never double-book" is a benefit claim he didn't ask for. His question was also a clue: where he's driving from matters to him, and you didn't follow it up.
- **Instead:** Answer briefly and neutrally, then turn it back: "It would use a home address. Is home usually where you start from?" or "Why do you ask?" You'd find out that his starting point varies, which could be a major requirement.

**4. "great, that's exactly what we were hoping to hear!"**
This is the biggest problem. You told him which answers please you, so everything after it is contaminated. He now knows what you want and will lean that way. Also, "oh okay, that sounds handy" is lukewarm, not validation.
- **Instead:** Stay neutral: "Thanks. What makes it sound handy?" or just "Mm, tell me more." Don't react to an answer's content, only encourage him to keep talking.

**5. "actually most of them are around 15 now, it's gotten a lot cheaper."**
You corrected him and defended the market. He brought you useful data: he tried a competitor, found it too expensive, and presumably stopped using it. You shut that down. His "huh, okay" shows he disengaged.
- **Instead:** "Tell me about that app. What made you try it? What happened after?" Why he tried it, what he liked, and why he left is likely the most valuable part of the interview.

**6. "and wouldn't it be easier if reminders went out automatically too?"**
This is a classic leading question: it's yes/no, hypothetical, and the answer is built in. "I guess so, sure" is a polite shrug.
- **Instead:** "What happens today when a client forgets or doesn't show?" If no-shows aren't a problem for him, you've just saved yourself building a feature.

## The pattern

1. **Pitching instead of probing.** You moved to features within a couple of minutes. Interviews should uncover the problem, not test your solution.
2. **Hypotheticals.** "Would you use...", "wouldn't it be easier..." get you politeness, not truth. Ask about the last time something happened.
3. **Signaling approval.** "That's exactly what we were hoping to hear" teaches the participant what to say.
4. **Ignoring his cues.** The home address question and the $40 app were openings that you shut down.
5. **Closed, yes/no questions.** They give you one-word answers. Open prompts ("tell me about...", "walk me through...", "what happened next?") give you stories.

## A habit to practice

After each answer, default to one of these before asking anything new:
- "Can you tell me more about that?"
- "What happened next?"
- "Why was that?"
- Or just silence for a few seconds.
