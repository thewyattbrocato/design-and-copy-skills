Your lead is right, and it gets worse as the transcript goes on. Here's each spot.

**1. "right, and that gets pretty chaotic I imagine?"**
This is a leading question. You supplied the problem ("chaotic") and invited him to agree. He gave a lukewarm "sometimes, yeah," which is a weak signal. You didn't follow up on it.
*Better:* "Tell me about the last time a booking went wrong." or "What's that like when it's busy?" Ask for a specific story and let him say whether it's painful.

**2. "we've got a feature where clients pick a slot themselves... would you use that?"**
This has three problems:
- You pitched your solution before you understood his problem.
- "Would you use that?" is a hypothetical. People say yes to hypotheticals because it's polite and costs nothing.
- It's a closed yes/no question.

*Better:* Stay in his current behavior. Ask "Walk me through the last time someone needed to reschedule." or "What do you do when two bookings are too far apart to make?" If he brings up scheduling pain on his own, that's real signal.

**3. "it uses your home address and adds travel time automatically, so you never double-book."**
His question ("how does it know where I'm driving from?") was a good one. It suggests travel time matters to him, and you could have explored that. Instead you answered with a sales pitch ("you never double-book"). You turned a research session into a demo.
*Better:* Say "Good question, why do you ask? Where are you usually driving from?" That could have told you whether he starts from home, from a previous job, or from somewhere else, which would change how the feature should work.

**4. "great, that's exactly what we were hoping to hear!"**
This is the most damaging line. It tells him which answers you want, and every answer after it is contaminated. It also treated "that sounds handy" as validation. "Sounds handy" is polite, not a commitment.
*Better:* Stay neutral. Say "Okay, thanks," and move on, or probe: "What would make it handy for you, specifically?"

**5. "actually most of them are around 15 now, it's gotten a lot cheaper."**
He gave you something valuable: he tried a competitor and it cost too much. You corrected him and defended the market. You missed the chance to learn why he stopped, what he disliked, and what he thought it was worth. His "huh, okay" suggests he shut down.
*Better:* "Tell me about that app. What made you try it, and what happened?" Then ask what he'd expect to pay for something like this.

**6. "and wouldn't it be easier if reminders went out automatically too?"**
This is a leading question again, and it stacks a feature on top. "I guess so, sure" is the weakest possible agreement.
*Better:* Ask about the existing behavior: "What happens today when a client forgets or no-shows?" If no-shows cost him money, he'll tell you. If he shrugs, reminders probably aren't a priority.

**The pattern**
- **You introduced the problems and solutions.** Your questions contained the answers.
- **You asked about hypotheticals** ("would you use...", "wouldn't it be easier...") instead of past behavior.
- **You reacted to answers.** You celebrated the ones you liked and corrected the ones you didn't, so he learned what to say.
- **You skipped the strongest signals:** the diary and texting workflow, the travel question, and the competitor app. Those were all real, unprompted details.

**What the transcript actually tells you**
Only three things are reliable: he books by text and paper diary, he's wondering about travel time, and he tried a paid app and balked at $40 a month. Everything else ("sounds handy," "I guess so") is politeness you prompted.

**A better opening sequence**
1. "How do you book tuning visits?" (you did this well)
2. "Walk me through the last booking from first text to finished visit."
3. "What's the most annoying part of that?"
4. "Have you tried anything else to manage it? What happened?"
5. Only near the end, if at all, show the idea and ask for reactions, not commitments.

Your first question was good. The trouble started when you moved from asking about his life to asking about your product.
