Your lead is right. The problem isn't one big mistake. You do four small things, and each one pushes him toward yes.

## Where it went wrong

| Your line | What's wrong | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| "that gets pretty chaotic I imagine?" | You supplied the answer. His "sometimes, yeah" is a polite agreement, not a problem he described. | "Tell me about the last time booking didn't go smoothly." |
| "we've got a feature… would you use that?" | You pitched too early and asked for a prediction. People say yes to hypotheticals, and it tells you nothing about what he does. | Stay on past behavior: "Walk me through the last time two jobs clashed, or you had to work out the drive between them." |
| "it uses your home address… so you never double-book" | His question, "how does it know where I'm driving from?", was the best moment so far. It suggests he may not start from home. You answered with an assumption and a sales claim. | "Where are you usually driving from when you get to a job?" Then "Why do you ask?" |
| "great, that's exactly what we were hoping to hear!" | You told him what you want. Everything after this is shaped by it. "Sounds handy" was also mild politeness, and you treated it as validation. | Stay neutral: "Okay, tell me more about what would make it handy." Or just say "Thanks" and move on. |
| "actually most of them are around 15 now" | He gave you a real past experience, a paid app he tried, and you corrected him. That's arguing, and it ends the topic ("huh, okay"). | "Tell me about that app. What made you try it? What happened? What made you stop?" |
| "wouldn't it be easier if reminders went out automatically too?" | This is a leading tag question. "I guess so, sure" is weak agreement, not a need. | "What happens today when a client forgets or doesn't show? Tell me about the last time." |

## The pattern

- You ask for opinions and predictions ("would you use", "wouldn't it be easier") instead of stories about the last real time.
- You pitch and defend the product mid-interview, so he's reacting to your feature instead of describing his work.
- You reward agreement ("exactly what we were hoping") and correct disagreement (the price). Both teach him what answer you want.
- You skip the two openings he gave you: the starting-location question and the earlier app. Those are your best leads.

## What this stretch actually tells you

- He books by text and a paper diary.
- He wondered where travel starts from.
- He once tried a scheduling app and thought $40 a month was too much, or at least remembered it that way.

His "handy" and "I guess so" aren't evidence of demand. I'd drop them from your notes.

## Fix for next time

Keep the feature out of the conversation until the end, or leave it out entirely. Ask for stories ("tell me about the last time…"), follow what he raises, and use silence and "tell me more" instead of filling gaps. If you're also the designer, ask a teammate to run some sessions. The person who built the idea is the worst person to test it.

The app and the price are the most useful things to follow up on. A recontact or the next participant could start with "Have you ever used anything to manage bookings? What happened?"
