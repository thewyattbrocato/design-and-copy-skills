It's partly a cop-out. The physics is real, but "that's just how print works" isn't a complete answer, because there are things you and the printer can do about it.

**Why your orange went dull**

- **Your screen emits light and ink only reflects it.** #ff6a1a is a very saturated orange. A monitor can show it because it makes its own light, and ink on paper can't.
- **Standard CMYK can't reach that orange.** It's outside the range (gamut) of standard CMYK printing. The press gets as close as it can, and the result is a duller, slightly browner orange. The printer is right about this.
- **Uncoated paper makes it worse.** Matte or uncoated stock soaks up ink and mutes it further. Coated stock keeps more of the color.
- **The printer's answer is still incomplete.** A browner result also happens when the file was converted badly, for example when an RGB file is sent straight through without a proper profile conversion. They can also tune the CMYK mix and use other processes. They should have told you about those options.

**What to do**

1. **Ask what the proof was made from.** Find out whether it came from your RGB file, which CMYK values were used, which paper it's on, and whether it's a calibrated proof. Then ask for the CMYK build of your orange. A common starting point is about C0 M70 Y100 K0, but that's a guess, so test it.
2. **Ask for a Pantone (spot ink) orange.** This is the real fix for a brand color. A single premixed ink can be much brighter than a CMYK mix. Pantone Orange 021 C is one candidate. Choose by holding the physical swatch book next to your website on screen, and don't pick from the screen alone. The cost is an extra ink and usually a higher price, which is often worth it for a menu.
3. **Consider coated stock, or a coated spot for the orange areas.** Coated paper will brighten the orange noticeably. The trade-off is that menus often use matte or uncoated paper on purpose, for the look and for writing on.
4. **Judge the next proof from a physical swatch.** Lay the swatch next to the proof under the light the menu will be used in. Don't judge it against your screen or a photo of the proof, because photos shift the color too.
5. **Keep the orange in small areas.** Use it for headings, rules and accents rather than big blocks, where dullness shows most. If you're sticking with CMYK, a slightly warmer, more yellow orange will often look closer to the website than a straight conversion of the hex.
6. **Accept a close match, not an identical one.** Even with the best process, print and screen will never match exactly. The aim is for the orange to read as the same brand color.

**What to say to the printer:** "Can you quote the menu with Pantone [your chosen orange] as a spot color, and send a physical proof on the actual stock?" If they refuse to discuss anything beyond "that's how print works," it's reasonable to get a quote from another printer.
