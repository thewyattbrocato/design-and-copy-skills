Partly. "Just how print works" is true, but it doesn't explain brown, so the printer may have a fixable problem.

**What's true:** Your screen makes #ff6a1a from emitted light, and ink can't match that brightness. This orange is saturated enough that CMYK will always look somewhat duller than the screen. A good conversion lands at roughly 70% magenta and 95% yellow, with no cyan and no black. That should still be a clearly warm, lively orange, not a brown one.

**What isn't:** Brown usually means one of these:
- **Black or cyan in the mix.** Either one muddies orange. This happens when the file was converted with the wrong profile, or when the orange was built from the wrong inks.
- **Uncoated or matte stock.** The ink soaks in and mutes the color. Coated stock gives the brightest result.
- **A conversion with no color profile.** This happens when the printer converts an RGB file automatically.
- **The wrong comparison.** A proof held under yellowish indoor light, or an inkjet proof that isn't a true press proof, can look browner than the finished job.

**What I'd do:**
1. **Ask the printer three questions.** What CMYK values did the orange print at? What profile and stock were used? Was the proof made on the actual press stock? If the CMYK values contain black or cyan, that's the fix. You can reasonably push back.
2. **Consider a spot color.** Ask for a Pantone ink matched from the physical swatch book. Check Orange 021 C and 1585 C against your screen. A spot ink is mixed to be bright, so it avoids the CMYK limit. It usually costs extra, but if the orange is your brand color on a two- or three-color menu, it's often worth it.
3. **If you stay with CMYK, choose a print-friendly orange.** Pick the color from a printed CMYK swatch you like, not from the screen. Then use that CMYK value on the menu. Give the printer a CMYK-native file (not RGB), and ask for a press proof.
4. **Choose coated stock** if the menu design allows it.
5. **Judge under daylight or a daylight-balanced lamp**, not under warm bulbs.

Expect a close match, not an identical one. Your website and the menu can share the same brand orange, but the two numbers will differ. Treat the printed value as the menu's version of that color. Don't try to match it to the screen by eye.
