# Data on slides

Load this when a slide carries numbers: a single figure, a chart, a comparison or a table. Whether the chart is honest (baselines, axes, scale, labels) is chart-design's job. This file covers what changes when the chart is shown to a room, where nobody can zoom and the speaker is talking over it.

## Decide the conclusion first

Write the sentence the slide proves before choosing a form. "Foggy-morning sailings are cancelled three times as often as clear ones" decides what is shown (two bars, or two numbers) and how it is emphasized. A slide titled by a metric name ("Cancellations by weather") has no conclusion yet.

## One number, big

If one number carries the point, show the number large with its comparison beside it, and skip the chart. "41% of crossings now run at least half full", set in large type with "up from 29% two winters ago" smaller beneath it, beats a two-bar chart of the same two values. Use a chart when the shape or the comparison across several things is the point.

## Three layers

Build the chart in layers, quietest first:

1. **Scaffolding:** axes, gridlines, tick labels, legend. Keep only what is needed, make it neutral and light, and drop gridlines when the values are labeled.
2. **Data:** the series or marks, in one neutral tone for context.
3. **Emphasis:** the one series, bar or point the conclusion is about, in one strong color, with a direct label or a short annotation.

If everything is loud, nothing steers the eye. The title carries the conclusion in words, and the emphasis puts it in the picture.

## Introduce the chart

The first time a chart type or a measure appears, say what the axes are and what a mark means before the finding: "each bar is one month; taller means more sailings cancelled". This costs a sentence and saves the room from decoding while you speak. Prefer familiar chart types; an unusual one spends the audience's attention on the form.

## Build a chart on screen

When the speaker will talk over the chart, show the frame first (title, axes, what a mark means) and say how to read it, then bring the data in at most about four steps that follow the spoken order: context first, then the series that matters, then any target or threshold line, after the axes have been explained. For an unfamiliar form, start from a form the room knows and change it into the new one a step at a time. Check that the steps match the spoken order and that every step leaves a chart that is true.

## Keep tables off the slide

A table of more than a few numbers belongs on the handout or in the appendix. If an exact value is needed in the room, mark it on the chart. Do not show a chart with its data table beside it: pick the chart for the room, give the table to the reader afterward.

## A run of chart slides

When the audience will flip between several charts, hold constant:

- the axis ranges and units, so the same height means the same amount;
- the color of each entity (the same product is always the same hue);
- the position of the title, the source line and the legend.

Change the one thing the slide is about. A chart whose axis silently changes between slides makes the comparison false.

## Colors that mean something already

Do not use a brand red for a series when red also means loss, alarm or an overdue state in the room's work. Do not use green and red alone to separate two series. Check the slide in grayscale; the emphasis should still be the strongest mark.

## Source and uncertainty

A small source line under the slide (name and year, not a citation block) is enough in the room; the full reference goes to the handout. If the numbers carry uncertainty that changes the decision, say so in the title or a note on the slide, not only in the appendix.

## Complex figures

A dense figure can stay if the speaker marks the one or two things to look at, with an arrow or a highlight, and says that the rest is in the handout. Without a marked key point, redraw it simpler.

## Common failures

- Default 3D columns, legend on the side, every series equally loud → flat marks, direct labels, one emphasized series.
- A title that names the metric → a title that states the finding.
- The same chart reused with a rescaled axis on the next slide → fixed ranges.
- A table pasted under a chart → table to the handout.
- A big number buried in a paragraph → set it large and say what it is compared with.
