# Audience and argument

Load this when the room is mixed, when someone in it holds the opposite view, when the material is bigger than the slot, or when the case rests on a single example or an analogy. Do it before ordering anything. Every default in the main file still holds unless a trigger below is met; a trigger is something the user said or the brief makes plain, never a guess.

## Who decides

List who will be there and mark the one person (or the few) who must act on the ask. Write the takeaway and the ask for them. Everyone else gets at most one line on what the outcome means for their group. When nobody decides (an all-hands, a conference), say so and write for the most common listener. The plan names the decider or says there is none.

## A mixed room

When specialists sit with people from other functions, open each part at a level everyone follows, put the specialist depth in the middle, and close the part on a result anyone could repeat. Say once, early, that some detail is left for the appendix or for afterwards, and where it is. If the user can reach the detail-seeker beforehand, send them the detail first so the room is not spent on it.

## A room that opposes the takeaway

Default: the verdict comes early. Change when the user says, or the brief makes plain, that the people who decide or must carry it out already hold the opposite position (they built what is being replaced, chose the option being dropped, said so in writing). General doubt is not opposition; the trigger is a stated prior position. Then:

1. Open on the question both sides share and the criteria for answering it.
2. State their strongest reason fairly, in words they would sign, before contesting it.
3. Show the evidence against those criteria.
4. Then the conclusion and the ask, with time left to talk.

Do not plan to convert the opponent in the room; aim at the undecided and at the opponent feeling heard. Change back to a verdict-first order when the slot may be cut or interruptions are likely, even in a cool room. State the room's stance in the plan, and the order that follows from it.

## What is left out

When the material is more than the slot carries, pick the two to four parts that carry the takeaway. Time follows how much a part carries the takeaway, never how much work it took. Say in the opening, in one sentence, what the talk does not cover and where it is. For a room that does not yet trust the method, organise the appendix by the same parts as the talk so any question is one jump away, and say it exists.

## The chain

When the takeaway is far from where the room stands, order the supporting points as a chain: each is a claim the room must accept before the next makes sense, from most accepted to least, each with its own proof. Read the titles in order; none should depend on a later one.

## Match the proof to the claim

Name the kind of proof each supporting claim needs:

- "This works" or "this is bigger" needs a measurement or a test.
- One case shows that something can go wrong, never that it is safe or typical. Say how many cases there were.
- A comparison holds only between like things: same period, same base, same unit.
- An analogy makes a size or a mechanism easy to picture. It is never the only support; put the evidence next to it.

No slide's only support is an analogy or one favourable case.

## Known, suspected, and the result that does not fit

Mark a conjecture as one, on the slide or in the spoken line ("we think", "not yet tested"), and keep it apart from what is established. When the user mentions a result that does not fit the takeaway, show it and say what it might mean; it will come up in questions anyway. A hedge that changes the decision belongs in the title.

## Worked case (invented)

A planner has fifteen minutes to propose moving the bakery's delivery vans to a single morning wave. The shop owner decides. The two drivers who designed the current rounds are against it and have said so. Plan: name the owner as decider; because the drivers hold a stated position, open on the shared question (how to get bread out before the first rush) and the criteria (stale loaves, fuel, driver hours); state the drivers' best reason (split rounds let them fix a missed stop the same day) before contesting it; then the evidence, the verdict and the ask, leaving five minutes. The two-week trial is one case, so the slide says "one fortnight, 12 routes" and the claim is "worked for the trial routes", not "works".
