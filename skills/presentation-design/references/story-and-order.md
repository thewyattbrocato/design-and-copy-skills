# Story and order

Load this when planning a deck or talk from nothing, from a pile of material, or from a document someone wants presented. The default failure is structure by inheritance: the deck copies the source's table of contents or the order the work was done in. The audience did not live through the work. They need to know why they are in the room, what you want, and then why they should believe it.

## Audience action and takeaway

Write two lines before any slide.

- **Action:** who does what after the talk, with a date when there is one. "The harbour council votes on the winter timetable on the 14th" is an action. "The council understands the timetable" is not; ask what the understanding is for.
- **Takeaway:** one sentence a listener could disagree with, with the recommendation or the key number in it. "Cutting the 06:10 sailing saves more than it costs in riders" can be argued with. "Timetable update" cannot.

If you cannot write the takeaway, the material is not ready for slides; say so in one line and write the best version from what you have. Then sort everything you hold into three piles: supports the takeaway, explains how you got there, and neither. The first pile becomes slides, the second becomes appendix or notes, the third is dropped.

## The order

1. **Need.** Why this audience cares, in their terms: a cost, a risk, a deadline, a question they own. One or two sentences. Skip it only when the room already lives with the problem.
2. **Who did what.** One line: what you looked at or did. This is where the speaker is placed; a name and job title read out first spend the best seconds on nothing.
3. **Takeaway.** The sentence from above, said plainly.
4. **Preview.** A spoken line that names the supporting points by their short labels. In a short deck this is a sentence, not a slide.
5. **Support.** Two to five points, each with its own slide or slides. Each opens with a transition that reuses its label.
6. **Recap.** The labels again, in a breath, then the takeaway.
7. **Ask.** What you want, from whom, by when.

An optional attention line can come before the need. It must be short and about the subject: a concrete case, a surprising number. A joke, a rhetorical question or a show of hands usually costs time and signals nothing.

## How long the opening runs

Keep the opening, from first word to the preview, proportional to the talk: roughly a quarter of a six-minute slot (a minute and a half), a tenth of an hour-long one (five to six minutes). Past that, the audience has waited too long for the point. A room that already knows the context needs a shorter opening, not a skipped takeaway.

## Bad news and hard asks

The verdict still comes early; delaying it makes the room guess. Frame it first by what is at stake and what decision is needed, then state it: "We need to decide how to handle a smaller grant. The short version: the pilot cannot run at the original size." Offer the options and your recommendation in the next breath, so the room leaves with a path and not only a loss.

When the room needs context before the takeaway makes sense (a technical audience new to the problem, a joint session), put the context in the need and keep it to what the takeaway depends on.

## Previews, transitions and labels

- A label is two to four words naming a supporting point ("rider counts", "crew cost", "winter fog"). Use the same words in the preview, in each transition and in the recap. Synonyms make the audience re-learn the map.
- A transition closes one point and names the next: "Rider counts are the first reason; the second is crew cost." One sentence, spoken.
- A map slide that returns at part boundaries earns its space only in long sessions. In a ten-minute talk it is overhead. It names only the two to four parts (never "Introduction" or "Conclusion"), the same label marks each part's slides, and it is cut before any content slide.

## The close

End on the recap and the ask, in a firmer or slower tone than the body. The last content slide holds the takeaway and the ask with owner and date, and it stays up during questions. Do not end on "That's all", "Thanks for listening" or a bare "Questions?"; thanks can be said, not shown. If nothing is being asked, close on the takeaway and what changes because of it.

## Worked outline (invented)

Context: a ferry operator's planner has twelve minutes at a harbour council meeting. Action: the council approves a revised winter timetable at its next vote. Takeaway: dropping the 06:10 sailing from November to March frees crew for the foggy-morning backup and costs under one rider in twenty.

Opening (about ninety seconds): the council has heard complaints about cancelled sailings on foggy mornings (need); the planner's team compared two winters of sailings against crew rosters and rider counts (who did what); the 06:10 is the one to drop (takeaway); three reasons: who rides it, what it costs, what the freed crew can do (preview).

Support, five content slides at about two minutes each: who rides the 06:10 (one number, large), what the sailing costs per rider, how many cancelled sailings the freed crew would have covered, what the revised timetable looks like, what could go wrong and the fallback. Ask on the last slide: vote on the 14th, owner named, start date. Everything else, including the sailing-by-sailing table, sits in the appendix and a one-page handout given at the end.
