# Notes, handouts and cutting

Load this for speaker notes, handouts, appendices, decks that serve two purposes, backup slides, and every request to cut a deck or talk to a slot. The root problem is usually one deck doing three jobs: staging a talk, holding the script, and being the leave-behind. Give each job its own place.

## Three places for detail

| Place | What goes there | Who sees it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The live slide | The claim, the one visual, the labels it needs | The room, while you speak |
| Speaker notes | What to say: the point, the number to quote, the transition | Only the speaker; never projected |
| Handout or appendix | Tables, full references, equations, long diagrams, methods, anything a reader wants to check | Readers, afterward (or on request) |

Each fact lives in one place. When a table moves to the handout, the slide gets the conclusion from it and nothing else.

## Declare the deck type

- **Live:** read by nobody in isolation. Short claims, large visuals, details elsewhere.
- **Read alone:** a pre-read or a leave-behind. Complete sentences, fuller captions, denser pages; it must work with no speaker. Count pages, not minutes.
- **Both:** build two artifacts. A deck that tries to be both fits neither: too sparse to read later, too crowded to speak with. Default: build the live deck, then say what the document version needs (the notes expanded into prose, the appendix, the tables). Change when the user needs one file only; then pick the dominant use and label the other as a compromise.

## Speaker notes

Notes are for the person talking. Write them as a short outline per slide, not a script:

- the point of the slide in a phrase;
- the number or name to say exactly;
- the line that hands off to the next slide, reusing the next label.

A script read aloud sounds like reading, invites the speaker to stare at the page, and still drifts out of date. Write a full script only when the user asks for one (a recorded talk, a speech with legal wording), and then keep it separate from the slides. Never put notes on the slide itself, and do not project them.

If the user did not ask for notes, do not append them. The slide list is the deliverable.

## Handouts

- Give the handout at the end, not at the start; handing it out early turns the room into readers.
- It holds the tables, the sources, the definitions and the fuller reasoning. Sources on the slide are name and year; the handout has the full citation.
- It repeats the takeaway and the ask on the first page.
- Say on the closing slide or in the notes that it exists, so cut material is not lost.

## Backup slides

For a decision or a Q&A session, list the two or three hardest questions you expect and park one backup slide per question after the closing slide. They are not shown unless asked. Skip this for a routine update.

## Cutting to a slot or a slide count

The default failure is to shrink text, speak faster and keep every section. The work is deciding what the audience can do without.

1. **Fix the minutes** and derive the slide count (about one content slide per two minutes) and the opening share.
2. **Restate the takeaway and the ask.** They survive every cut.
3. **Rank the supporting points** by how much each one carries the takeaway. Ask of each: if it vanished, would the audience still make the decision?
4. **Cut whole branches first.** Remove the lowest-ranked points together with their slides. Then cut detail within the points that stay. Cut wording last.
5. **Keep the frame.** The need, the takeaway, the preview, the recap and the ask are the last things to go. A cut deck that loses its recap and its ask has lost its reason to exist.
6. **Move, do not delete.** Cut slides go to the appendix or the handout, and the recap or notes says they exist.
7. **Write a drop list:** the order in which things fall out if the slot shrinks by two minutes, then by five.
8. **Check fit.** At about two minutes per content slide plus the opening and close, the arithmetic should come out under the slot with a minute spare. Say the arithmetic.

Cutting by slide count ("get this down to eight slides") follows the same steps; check whether the minutes also changed.

## Worked example (invented)

A seed library's coordinator has a twenty-slide deck for a ten-minute slot at a funders' breakfast. Action: renew a two-year grant. Takeaway: the library lends more seed each year than any other program in the county for the same cost per packet.

Budget: ten minutes, opening about a minute, close about a minute, leaving eight minutes of content: four content slides, plus a title and a closing ask. Ranking: loan volume per year, cost per packet, who the borrowers are, how the grant would be spent. Cut: the history of the library (appendix), the catalog of varieties (handout), the volunteer roster (handout), three of the five borrower stories (one stays, as a photograph with a one-line claim). Drop list: borrower story first, then the spending breakdown moves to a spoken sentence.

The result states: "10 minutes, 4 content slides plus title and ask; 14 slides moved to appendix". The takeaway sits on the first content slide, the ask with the renewal date on the last.

## Common failures

- 20 slides for 10 minutes, with a plan to speak quickly → count the minutes, cut to the budget, move the rest.
- Shrinking the font until everything fits → cut or split.
- Deleting the recap and the ask because they feel like repetition → they are what the room acts on.
- Notes that are a verbatim paragraph per slide → three short lines.
- One deck handed out ahead of the meeting that is also projected → two artifacts.
