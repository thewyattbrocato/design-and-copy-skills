# Slide craft

Load this when writing individual slides or fixing a slide that is crowded, vague or dull. How a slide looks (emphasis, spacing, type scale, color, grid) belongs to the neighboring skills; this file decides what a slide is allowed to say.

## One slide, one message

A slide makes one point and the title is that point. If the title needs "and", there are two slides. When you cannot say in a sentence what the slide is for, it is a holding pen for material that belongs in the appendix.

Build order for each slide:

1. Write the claim as a full sentence.
2. Choose the one visual that proves it: a number set large, a chart, a diagram, a photograph, a quote being discussed.
3. Add the fewest words that let the visual stand alone: labels, a unit, a source in small type.
4. Delete anything the speaker will not mention. If it is not said, it is competing with what is.

## Titles as claims

A title is a sentence with a verb that states what the audience should take from the slide, kept to two lines at most (usually under about fifteen words). It reads as an argument when strung together with the others.

- Label: "Rider counts". Claim: "Fewer than one rider in twenty boards the 06:10".
- Label: "Risks". Claim: "The only real risk is a fog week with no spare crew".
- Label: "Next steps". Claim: "We need a vote on the 14th to start in November".

Exceptions: the opening title slide (a picture of the subject or a sentence stating the main result, talked over rather than read; the first slide cut when time is short), section dividers in a long session, and the closing ask may be short. For crafting the line itself, use headlines-and-leads if installed.

Test: put the titles in a list. Read them in order with nothing else. They should give the argument and the ask. If one reads like a topic, the slide does not yet have a point.

## Word budget and size

- A live slide carries under about 30 words in the body, and a hard ceiling near 50. Past that, people read instead of listening, and the speaker is competing with the screen.
- Anything near 75 words is a page from a document. Reclassify it as a handout page or split it.
- Body text about 36 px on a 1920-wide frame, never below 32 px (about 28 and 24 points). Scale with the frame width. If the content does not fit at that size, the answer is fewer words or a second slide.
- Use full sentences only where a sentence is the content: a quotation under discussion, the one-line takeaway, a definition the room must agree on.
- Leave the slide's empty space alone. If a slide needs the whitespace spent to fit its content, split or cut it.

## Do not repeat the speaker

Three common ways slides duplicate speech:

- The slide lists the talk's points and the speaker reads them. Put the list in the speaker's outline and say it aloud.
- The slide holds the full sentence the speaker will say. The audience reads ahead, drifts, then waits for the speaker to catch up.
- The title says one thing and the body says it again with different words.

Fix: keep what the voice cannot do (show a number, a shape, a picture, a name spelled right) and drop what it can.

## Stand-alone test

Cover the speaker. A reader looking at the slide should be able to say what it shows and why it is in the deck. If they cannot, add a claim title or a label, not more text. Then do the reverse: take away the slides. The speaker's outline should still carry the argument. A deck where neither passes is a pile of props.

## Pictures, diagrams and quotes

- Photographs and illustrations should carry content: the thing itself, the place, the person affected. Decorative stock pictures, clip art and metaphor photos (a handshake, a lightbulb) add nothing and invite a joke.
- Keep one image family across the deck: the same treatment, lighting and level of realism. Do not put text over a busy photograph.
- A diagram earns its place when parts and their relationships are the point. Label parts directly. Show an overview before the detail, and keep the same layout when the next slide zooms in.
- A screenshot gets a marked region or a crop to the part that matters.

## Consistency across the deck

The same role sits in the same place on every slide: title, body, source line, page number. Structure slides (section break, recap, ask) look different from content slides so the audience feels the turn. Logos go on the first and last slide, not every slide. Two type roles are enough; neutrals plus one accent is the default palette. A break from the pattern is the strongest signal a deck has: spend it once, on the slide that matters most. Mechanics: visual-hierarchy, layout-structure, typesetting, color-palette.

## Motion

Off by default. A build is fine when it shows items in the order the speaker will say them, and when one change is on screen at a time. No entrance effects on titles, no transitions between slides that carry no meaning.

## When the speaker talks without the screen

During a story, a discussion or a pause, blank the screen, or leave up only the part's claim, so the room looks at the speaker. Mark those moments in the slide list. Over a call or in a recording a blank frame reads as a fault: show a quiet slide tied to the moment instead.

## Talks given over a call

The speaker cannot see faces to repair confusion, so each content slide states its claim and carries its proof on its own. Name a person before handing them a question. Send the leave-behind after the call.

## Backgrounds and rooms

Default: a light or dark background, picked once for the room. Dark suits a dim room, light suits a bright one and printouts; mid-tones lose contrast in both. Avoid a pattern or image behind text. Do not rely on color alone to say which series or status is which; the deck should survive grayscale.

## Common slide failures

- Five bullets under a label title → one claim, one visual; the rest into notes or a handout.
- A slide that lists "Challenges" and "Opportunities" in two columns → pick which one the decision turns on, or make two slides.
- A screenshot with no marked region → crop to the part that matters, or draw the arrow.
- A timeline of twelve milestones → show the two the audience must remember; the rest go to the handout.
- A "thank you" slide with a logo → end on the ask.
