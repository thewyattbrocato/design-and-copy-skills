# Grounds, dark themes, meaning and media

Load this when a color behaves differently in place, for dark themes, images, audience meaning, naming, or screen versus print.

## Grounds and edges

- Colors read relative to what surrounds them: a tinted or dark background pulls neighboring colors toward its opposite and flattens contrast with anything close to it. A light color on a light ground, or a dark one on a dark ground, loses both differences; a token chosen on white can be absorbed by a tinted card of the same hue. Give each token a distinct lightness from every ground it will touch, or move it a step.
- Strongly different hues at near-equal lightness vibrate, which some viewers see and some do not. Use it only for a deliberate alarm, never for text, charts or labels. Neighboring hues at equal lightness make the edge vanish; fine for decoration nobody parses, a defect for anything counted, found or read.
- Judge two close colors by looking between them, not by flicking back and forth, which lets the eye adapt. Compare on the real ground, at the real size, on a real screen: small patches, thin strokes and legend swatches need more separation than large fills, and blues separate worst.
- Dense fine patterns (dotted rules, stripe rows, icon interiors, halftone-like textures) retint a color. Test at the pixel density of use. A third color that exists only when zoomed out splits back into its parents on a sharp screen.
- Warm and cool are relative; do not use them as a depth system. Test the pair.
- Pairs that collapse for red-green and blue-yellow color-vision deficits: dark red with black, blue with purple, light green with white, dark red with dark green. Roughly 8 percent of men are affected; run a simulator and rely on lightness plus a second cue.

## Text on images

- Contrast is a property of the image, so fix the image: an overlay (dark under light text, light under dark), lowered image contrast, a colorized duotone, or a soft glow-like text shadow with large blur and no offset. A little of two methods beats a heavy dose of one.
- Check the brightest or busiest region under the text. Translucent white text on a photo or a colored panel looks washed out and lets the picture show through the letters; use a solid hue-matched tint.
- A scrim is a veil that shifts everything beneath it at once; account for that in the image's colors.

## Dark themes

- A dark theme is its own ground. Colors that were quiet on white because the ground subtracted them can shout on black, and the reverse.
- Surfaces are dark gray, not pure black, with lighter surfaces meaning nearer (higher elevation). Text is off-white; secondary text is a lower value of the same neutral, still above the floor.
- Retune the accent: lighter and calmer, chroma reduced so it does not glare. Remap status colors to lighter steps and re-measure every pair, including borders and focus rings.
- Keep the same intervals as the light theme. Test by dropping a dark-theme token onto the light ramp; it should land on the matching step. Light and dark should be one system in two settings, not two unrelated palettes.
- Re-justify the brand color on dark, and check logos, illustrations, shadows (which barely show on dark) and photos. Follow the system preference; offer an override; keep near-black only where it is the point (cinema, camera, OLED-first).

## Meaning and naming

- Name the audience and what sits beside a color before trusting what it means: red reads as danger, luck or gain depending on region and companions. Check mourning and celebration colors for global products, and do not encode gender.
- No pair is illegal in principle; a pair earns its place by adding a force the design lacks. The exception is a pairing that carries a public meaning you do not want.
- Defend a color with a reason tied to audience, job or tradition, not a compliment ("bright", "nice"). Give colors names that help discussion (role and character), and never design from a remembered color: sample it.
- A color that must be recognizable (brand, packaging) should survive reversed, on white, on dark and at small size.

## Screen and print

- Screen light is additive and brighter than ink; a screen value is not a print value. Hex does not describe spot colors, foil, fluorescent or metallic inks; specify inks or a named system, the stock and a tolerance, and look at a physical chip under the light it will live in.
- Delicate tints drift between displays and presses; a screenshot or compressed image shifts colors and flattens the middle, so do not sample a palette from one and call it the original.
- Stacked translucent layers behave differently on screen (lighter overlaps) than multiplied ink (darker); know which blend you are specifying.
