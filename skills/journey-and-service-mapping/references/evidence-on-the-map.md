# Evidence on the map

Load when research is supplied, when there is none, or for drop-off, "which moments matter" and opportunity ranking. How to run the research itself belongs to user-research-basics; this file covers what the map must show about its footing.

## Three honest states

| State | Status line says | What the cells may hold |
|---|---|---|
| No material | Assumption draft from the brief, to be corrected by people near customers | Plausible steps in the person's words, marked as guesses; no quotes, counts or durations |
| Some material | Built from what was supplied: name each source, its size and date | Facts from those sources tagged to them; the rest marked as inferred |
| Fieldwork | Built from n people in this segment, dates, method | Patterns across people, with counts as n of N |

The line is one sentence and also says what is thin ("no evidence for the renewal stretch"). A confident picture gets read as fact, so this line is the cheapest protection.

## When research is supplied

1. Tag every cell to its source with a short code and keep the key under the map. A cell with no source is inferred; say so.
2. Build from the pattern across people, not one person's story. A claim from a single source is a lead; mark it as one.
3. Where people split, show the split ("some wait, some call") rather than averaging into a middle nobody lives.
4. A phase with no evidence says "no evidence yet" in its cells. Do not fill it from the template.
5. Keep what people said apart from what you read into it. Interpretation goes in its own row or in the notes.
6. Cluster from below while holding the draft map from above; the map is the aggregate, the transcripts are the proof.

## When there is none

Deliver the assumption draft, then name the cheapest way to turn it into evidence, in order:

- Read what already exists (tickets, call logs, reviews, search terms, old tests) and sort each source into what was seen, possible causes and what it implies for the person.
- Ask about six people across roles, front line included, for half an hour each. Their picture of the experience is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Walk the experience yourself as the person would and note every place it touches them. Then look for the odd flows: a trial account, a joint account, a person who comes through a partner.
- Then talk to the people themselves. Method, counts and consent are in user-research-basics.

## What to ask for when the map is the goal

Ask about the last time, in order: what started it, what they did next, what they waited for, who they asked, what they felt. Add one bad incident: what happened, how it felt, what should have happened. Ask about the waits and the stretches between visits, since no system records them.

## Numbers on the map

- Absolute values as text, relative amounts as bars, change across phases as a line, volume as the width of a funnel shape. Each is fine; precision that was not collected is not.
- Survey after the conversations have told you what to count. Reach of each touchpoint, how often, importance and satisfaction on one scale throughout.
- Time is the strongest number on a service map: minutes the person spends, minutes they wait, elapsed days. Use `[to measure]` when absent.

## Drop-off across a whole service

- Drop-off lives at the transition between two phases: of the people who entered one phase, how many entered the next. It needs a count of entrants per phase, not page views.
- Many exits are off the screen: a phone call, a visit, a friend's advice, a competitor. List them, with the place each is visible, or the picture of a "leak" will only show the part that has analytics.
- Without counts, deliver a ranked list of suspected exits, each with the one measure that would confirm it, and say that a count shows where people leave but never why. Pair the worst transition with conversations with people who left.
- A drop at the first phase is often the wrong people arriving; check who entered before fixing the exit.

## Which moments matter

Weigh each phase and transition against: a decision is made, a hand-off occurs, a wait has no status, an earlier promise meets delivery, the step cannot be undone, effort peaks, or a competitor does it better. Evidence outranks reasoning: complaint volume, abandonment counts, time spent, repeated workarounds. Choose one or two, say why, and label the choice "suspected" until a measure supports it.

## Ranking opportunities

With about thirty needs or outcomes gathered from interviews, ask a survey sample to rate each for importance and for how well it is served today on one scale. Score importance plus the gap where importance exceeds satisfaction (importance 8, satisfaction 5 gives 11). It ranks customer opportunity, not revenue, and it cannot replace the conversations that produced the statements. Below ten items, rank by reasoning and say so.

## Sensitive subjects

For maps about harm, illness, money trouble or fear, do not draft a research approach beyond the outline. Say a trained researcher must run it, with a private room, a way out and support afterward, and that people may be best placed to draw their own map.
