# Rows, cells and feeling

Load when drawing, building or reviewing the phases, rows, cell wording, feeling, numbers and the page. The shapes below are one workable convention, not a standard; a house format wins.

## Phases

- Name the first phase by what the person does or tries, not by what the organization does ("Start looking", not "Acquisition").
- Repeated behavior is a loop. Unordered activity is a cluster in one column. Something ongoing appears once, where it begins, then continues as a bar. Keep decision branches few; a branch more than two deep belongs in a separate flow.
- The stretch between visits (deciding, asking a friend, forgetting) is a phase when it shapes what happens next.
- Day and week maps use real time units instead.

## Which rows

No fixed list. A row exists because someone asked its question:

- Where does it hurt? A pain row. Where could we change something? An opportunities row. What are they trying to get? A goal row, often once at the top.
- Who else is involved, or does anything differ by role? One row per actor.
- Is the cause inside the organization? Backstage rows (see blueprints-and-other-views).
- Will anyone read a measure, a rule or an owner here? Then add it. Otherwise leave it out.
- A row whose cells would be true of any service ("provides support") is padding. Remove it.

Order top to bottom: the person's side first, then touchpoints, then the organization's side, then pain and opportunities.

## Touchpoints in context

A bare channel name tells the reader nothing. Give the need, the time and the place with it.

- Weak: "SMS", "Website", "Phone".
- Better: "Reminder text the evening before pickup", "Reservation page, from a phone at lunch", "Call to the desk when the ladder is still out".

## Cell shapes

Pick one shape per row and keep it down the row.

- Actions: verb first. "Compare two quotes."
- Thoughts: a question in the person's voice. "Will they hold it for me?"
- Feelings: one plain adjective. "Uneasy."
- Pain points: an -ing phrase. "Waiting without a confirmation."
- Touchpoints: a noun with context.
- Opportunities: verb first, naming the change. "Confirm the hold by text within a minute."

To compress a long observation: use the person's words, one voice, drop articles and pronouns, state the root cause, shorten, abbreviate only what everyone knows, and let the row and column headers carry what position already says.

Before: "Borrowers sometimes get anxious while they wait at the desk because nobody tells them whether the tool they reserved has been returned yet."
After: Feelings row, Wait column: "Uneasy". Thoughts: "Is it back yet?" Pain: "Waiting without an update." The sentence moves under the map with its source.

## Feeling, honestly

- Default: one or two named feelings per phase, mixed allowed ("relieved, a little wary"). Words or simple faces are enough.
- A plotted line implies a measurement and squeezes mixed feelings into one value. Draw one only when feeling was measured with a stated scale and sample (say both), or label it "estimated from the interviews" and show only high, middle and low, never decimals.
- Name the most common positive and the most common negative feeling per phase if the material supports both. The tidy stated reason for a choice is usually the rational one; the social or emotional one has to be inferred from patterns, so mark it as inferred.
- Never use red against green as the only cue for good against bad.

## Numbers

- Text for an absolute value, a bar for a relative amount, a line for change across phases, shape width for volume in a funnel.
- Round to what was collected. "About two days" or a range beats "47.3 hours" from nothing.
- If a number would sharpen the map and you do not have it, write the placeholder and who could supply it.

## Ink

- Draw only the lines that mean something. Tints of one color or background regions instead of a grid of borders.
- One color for one facet (pain points, marked moments) all across the page, with a second cue such as a symbol or a label.
- Icons in one style, labeled where ambiguous; the map should read without a key. If a key is needed, it is short.
- One or two typefaces, mostly sans, never shrinking text to fit more in. Align to a grid; phase headers larger than cell text. Choices of color and type go to color-palette and typesetting.

## Text answers

Phases as columns up to six; beyond that, one row per phase with columns for the same rows, so each cell stays short. Put the framing and status lines above the table and the notes under it. The marked moments are bolded in place and named in one line below.

## As a page

- The first screen shows the framing line, the status line and the whole phase strip, with row labels fixed at the left; at 1280 wide, up to about eight phases fit without sideways scrolling. Past that, let the map scroll inside its own frame with the row labels pinned, not the page.
- Cell text no smaller than 13 px; headers larger. Marked moments carry a visible mark and a short reason; the key, if any, sits beside the map.
- Slide software is usually too small for a map. Use a large canvas or a scrollable page and a printable version.
- Page structure, spacing and responsive behavior go to layout-structure and responsive-layout.
