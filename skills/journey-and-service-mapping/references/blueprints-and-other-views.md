# Blueprints and other views

Load for a blueprint, hand-offs and fail points, or a day, job, mental-model or ecosystem view. A blueprint is the same chronology as a journey map with the machinery exposed.

## Layers

From top to bottom, for one episode in real time:

1. What the person touches: screens, objects, rooms, documents.
2. What the person does.
3. What staff or the system does where the person can see it (front stage).
4. What happens where they cannot (back stage): people, services, partners.
5. What supports it: tools, data, policies, contracts.

Two lines run across the layers: one between what the person can see and what they cannot, and one between teams or systems that talk to each other behind the scenes. Everything above the first line is what the person experiences; everything below explains it.

## Fail points and timing

- Mark where the service is most likely to break and why, not every step. One to three marks per blueprint.
- Give an expected time to every real-time step that can stall, and the person's total time at the foot in minutes. Two providers that each look quick can add up to a long wait for the person, which only shows once both timelines sit in one picture.
- A step the person cannot see but waits for is the commonest finding. Show it with its wait.

## Hand-offs

People get used to one way of working, predict the next step, then have to reorient when the thing changes hands. Inconsistency between touchpoints feels disjointed. The aim is coherence, not uniformity: the next step should be what they expected even if it looks different. Flag every hand-off to a third party the person does not know exists.

## Variants

- Several roles: one row per actor.
- Wanting emotion: give the person's side more room and use the provider's own role names.
- Reading online: color-coded stacks read more easily than swim lanes.
- Fixing waste: a view with only the backstage and time spent in it.
- Do not mix without reason, and add rows (rules, metrics, critical moments) only when someone will use them.

Weaknesses: no sense of the environment, and it drifts toward a flow chart. Keep the person's side vivid.

### Example (invented)

An at-home piano tuning service. Person-side: books online, clears the room, waits at home between 9 and 12, pays at the end. Front stage: confirmation text, the tuner's arrival call. Back stage: the dispatcher assigns a tuner the evening before, the tuner's route app sends the arrival window, the payment provider sends the receipt. The visibility line sits above the dispatcher row. Fail point: the window is promised before routes are built, so the 9-to-12 slot can slip; the person's total at home is about four hours for a ninety-minute job. Third-party hand-off flagged: receipt comes from the payment provider, not the service.

## Day and week views

Real time units, colored by what mode the person is in (at work, on the move, at home), built from short real stories of actual days. If there is no typical day, ask about yesterday, compare several days, or map a week.

## Job view

The bare process of getting something done, with no feelings and no brand. Use it as a coverage check: does your map include what happens before the first step and after the last? Look at each step for loss in speed, variation and quality. It excludes emotional and social aims, which are gathered separately. It can seed an experience map as one lane with the experience rows added.

## Mental-model view

- Each item is one thought, reaction or principle, written from the person's side ("I want the mower to stop leaving stripes", not "use the lawn-care category filter").
- Group from below by what the speaker was after; name the groups in their words; put the supporting products beneath a dividing line.
- Comb the transcripts yourself; the understanding comes from the combing. A rapid variant uses one- to two-page stories gathered a week ahead and a few hours in one workshop for about a hundred items; a formal study of twenty to thirty people takes weeks.
- The groups can become navigation labels in the people's own words. It is long, detailed and has no chronology, so it is the wrong tool when the reader needs a sequence.

## Ecosystem view

- Shows relationships, not time: a network or spatial layout, concentric rings for how close, each element deliberately simple so the links stand out, overlays for several stories on one base.
- Choose a lens (gaps in service, cost to provide) instead of drawing every link.
- Device variant: people start on one device and finish on another and invent workarounds between. Decide per device whether behavior should be consistent, continuous or complementary.
- Content variant: how information is made and moves, and where departments duplicate each other.
- Slow to build, hard to build in a group, little emotion. Wrong for a single episode.
