# Using the map

Load for a workshop, a future state, hypotheses, follow-through or a review of a map. The conversation does the aligning; the picture holds the agreement still so people drift less. A map gives no answers and tends to expose uncomfortable facts between teams, so plan for some resistance.

## Reading it for decisions

1. Everyone corrects the map first: wrong steps, missing rows, field stories. Large maps are split among groups.
2. Mark the important moments with dot votes.
3. Each group rates how well each phase is supported, on a simple three-point or five-point scale. Talk where groups disagree; a split often means one group sits closer to the recent complaints.
4. Look for weak points, gaps, effort and where an alternative does better, and overlay the pattern.

Hold the room in one mode at a time: reading, reframing, diverging or converging, and say which.

## Reframe before brainstorming

State the problem as who has it, what it is, when it occurs and why it matters, then ask what could change it. Add constraints with "what if". Or ask for ways to make it worse and invert them. While diverging: volume, no judging, build on others, alternatives, wild ideas welcome, work visually. Prompts that help: the barriers at each phase, including emotional and social ones; a set of industry assumptions written alone, voted on, then inverted.

Converge on a grid of value against effort. Quick wins first, then high-value harder items; redesign low-feasibility ideas to move them up.

## Future state

- A future map is a vision, not a finding. Label it a hypothesis.
- Append it beneath the current map so cause and cure sit together. Use a separate diagram only when the future sequence differs (for example, a deposit is taken at booking instead of at pickup).
- A fast version: sketch the imagined experience against today's to find highs, lows and one standout moment.
- At each major division, ask who the person becomes by using this. Offerings that ask people to become someone they do not want to be fail. The question is weak for pure utilities.
- Plan content by plotting the ideal story, listing what each point needs, listing what exists and finding the gaps.

## Test the problem, not only the answer

Within hours, make a cheap artifact for the leading ideas (a written scenario, storyboard, sketch) and get fast reactions from two or three people. Better, make an artifact that embodies what the team presumes and show it to people who supposedly have the problem; a planned redesign can turn out to be aimed at the wrong problem. This also softens the loudest voice in the room. It is not controlled research.

## Hypotheses, owners, budget

- Write each belief with its measure: this change, for this person, at this moment, should produce this effect, seen as this number by this date. Without a measure it is untestable.
- Cheap experiments: an explainer video, a landing page, a prototype test, a hand-delivered version of the service, a limited release. A cheap test is roughly four to eight weeks and a few thousand in budget.
- Ideas are rarely scarce; uncertainty kills them. Name an owner, make the experiment the stated outcome, place many small bets, and commit people and money before ideation ends. Workshop output is hypotheses, not ready ideas.
- Shelve what is not chosen with the reason.

### Example (invented)

A tool-lending library reduces overdue returns. Before: 40 ideas on a wall, "better reminders" among them. After: three hypotheses, each shaped "for weekend borrowers, a text the evening before the due date should lower overdue returns, seen as a drop of at least a quarter within eight weeks", each with an owner, a budget line and a start date; the rest shelved with reasons.

## Session logistics

- All in person or all remote, never a mix. Six to twelve people. A kickoff call about a week before; named roles for facilitator, scribe and timekeeper; something social.
- Remote: one shared canvas, short chunks (four sessions of about two hours, or two of four), more pre-work and follow-up.
- Groups that have never met the people should hear real stories before they work.

## Follow-through

Survey the group, update the map, send the output to people who were not there, print it large where people pass it, name an owner for each experiment and check weekly. Give the map an owner, a review date and a link to live numbers; keep a basket of indicators rather than one summed number. Maps age slowly but not never.

## Reviewing a map

Read in this order and give each finding its evidence from the page, a severity and a fix:

1. Scope and status: is there a framing line, a status line, a decision?
2. Family against the question: a journey for a hand-off problem, a funnel for a life goal, lifecycle and one call on the same map.
3. Evidence: invented persona details, no sources, no counts.
4. Rows and cells: touchpoint lists passed off as the experience, groups that mirror the org chart, paragraphs, more than twelve phases.
5. Feeling and numbers: a smooth curve with no source, one feeling per phase, false precision.
6. Use: marks, votes, owners; a jump straight from picture to quick fixes that loses the person's goal; a file nobody owns.

Start with what works. Rank by consequence, not by order of appearance, and rewrite only what was asked.
