# Families and scope

Load to choose the kind of map, set its altitude and breadth, pick the primary person, or size the effort. Starting values; change them when the question says otherwise.

## Choose by the question and who must act

| Kind | Answers | Reader acts on it | Time axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Journey | How a person finds, picks, uses and stays with one offering | Marketing, sales, customer success | Yes |
| Blueprint | What the organization does, seen and unseen, during one episode | Service owners, operations, engineers | Yes, real time |
| Experience map | How a person pursues a goal whatever brand they use | Strategy, product direction, new openings | Yes, no brand in the phase names |
| Day or week view | How the person's time is actually spent | Anyone fitting a service into a life | Strict time units |
| Mental-model view | How the person thinks about the topic | Content, navigation, product gaps | No |
| Ecosystem view | How parties, devices or content relate | Leaders deciding ownership and partnership | No |

- Names blur and many real maps are blends. Keep the purpose and the viewpoint clear instead of arguing labels, and say which family you chose in one line.
- If the request says "journey map" but the trouble is a hand-off between teams, say in one line that a blueprint of that episode answers it better, then produce it unless the user insists on the original.
- A journey map explains why people choose and stay, with the reasons behind it. It is not an inventory of touchpoints.
- An experience map has no purchase phase by default. It includes positive stretches, loops and the places people give up, and it answers how the offering fits into their life rather than how they move through the funnel.

## Altitude and shelf life

- Three altitudes: the whole relationship over months or years, the stretch from first need to first use of one offering, and a single interaction. Pick the one the decision needs; a three-year relationship and a phone-call step do not belong on one map.
- Concrete maps tied to today's screens and staff go stale within months. Goal-level and thought-level maps can last years. Broad scope needs fewer maps.
- Make several maps only for significant differences in the person or situation (a first-time borrower and a contractor who borrows weekly), not for every segment.

## Scope: say what the boundary costs

- Start at the obvious start, then step one earlier. A borrower's map that begins at the reservation misses the afternoon they decided to fix the shelf themselves instead.
- End where the person's goal is met or they leave, which is often after the organization's process ends (the tool is back on the shelf, the project is done).
- Wide gives the big picture and thin detail; narrow gives depth on one stretch. State which you chose and what it leaves out in the framing line. A session that tries both sinks.

## One person, one stub

- Fix one primary viewpoint and align other actors to it. The same reader of one magazine is a different unit of analysis as a buyer, a news consumer or across a day.
- A persona stub is one line: a behavior pattern and a goal, traceable to sources ("weekend borrower, needs one tool for one job, returns it within two days"). No name, age, photo or quote unless the material has them. Without sources, label it "assumed".
- Several people on one timeline work only when every one of them gets the same kinds of rows.

## Whose relationship

For a chain of parties, write them in a line (maker, shop, delivery partner, household) and choose which pair the map is about. Different pairs need different maps and different participants. This takes minutes and settles who to ask.

## Effort

Size the work to the decision. Start from: a draft from the brief and what already exists, a day or two; a short study with a few interviews, a week or two; fieldwork, analysis and review rounds, several weeks. Say which you are delivering. A lone designer on one product can sketch; a group spanning channels needs a proper effort, a two-page plan (aim, measurable goal, who takes part, scope, kind of map, main risks) and a pilot to persuade doubters. A stakeholder who has already asked for a map does not need the pitch.

## Other shapes, other axes

Time is the default. Drop it for a hierarchy when many things happen at once, a spatial layout for physical settings, a network for relations that are neither sequential nor nested. A circle suits something used on repeat. Check the shape against how the reader will use it: to diagnose, to plan or to discover.
