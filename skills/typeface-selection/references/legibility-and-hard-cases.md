# Legibility and hard cases

Load when someone cites a readability claim ("serif is proven easier"), or when a face must work for codes, tiny labels, far or glanced text, an unusual brand letterform, or readers the usual advice does not cover. These are tendencies from small, mostly adult, mostly lab tests; they never replace setting the real text.

## Claims that hold and claims that do not

- **Serif versus sans.** Default: no class winner for running text. Real fonts differ in contrast, width, x-height and weight at once, so a gap between two says little about serifs. Never quote a percentage for one class beating the other.
- **Ordinary text faces.** Among sturdy faces in everyday use, reading speed hardly differs. Faces lose at the extremes: blackletter, ornate script, hairline weights, heavy flourish. Between two sturdy faces, call it taste and decide on the hard cases.
- **Capitals.** Continuous text in capitals reads slower. Default sentence case; change for a two- or three-letter tag or a short label.
- **Tight spacing.** Tightening letter spacing on its own slows reading. If a face needs it, widen the word gaps too. Amounts belong to typesetting.
- **Word shape.** "Capitals lose the ascender silhouette" and "serifs hold the line together" are weak reasons; letters, not outlines, are what get recognised.
- **Emphasis.** Bold makes one word stand out; whole paragraphs of bold or italic read slower.
- **Liking versus speed.** Preference follows familiarity and can disagree with speed; a vote does not show a face is fast.
- **"Hard-to-read type helps memory."** It did not hold up on retesting; do not use it to justify a quirky face.
- **Size is not the number.** Compare by x-height. A face with an x-height of 0.42em needs to be about a quarter larger than one at 0.52em to look the same size, so equal font-size values mislead.

## Confusable letters: check, do not assume

For codes, IDs and part numbers no sentence rescues a misread letter. Default: set a test line in the real weight, smallest real size and real ground, and drop the face if any pair merges:

`Il1 O0 ecoa q hb nu tfr ij rn m`

For prose, context rescues most pairs, so accept more risk there.

These tendencies come from letter-by-letter tests, often with large on-screen letters and adult readers. Treat them as letters to check, not rules:

- Open apertures on e, c and s hold up better than closed ones. An e with a high bar and a tiny counter is the weak variant.
- A single-storey a is mistaken for o or q more often than a two-storey a. Default two-storey in adult body text; change for a young readers' product, or where a code context makes the letter obvious.
- Very narrow i, j, l, t and f are the easiest to misread. Moderately wider drawings tend to survive distance; judge them in words, where wide letters strain spacing.
- Serifs help some stems (the tops of l, b, h) and hurt others (i, j). Evidence for any single "key feature" is mixed; do not pick a face on one.
- Do not chase maximum difference between letters; they must still look like one family.

## Small, far and glanced text

- **Small.** Default: the text or micro cut if the family has an optical-size axis. Otherwise choose a larger x-height, lower contrast, slightly wider letters and sturdier joins. Never scale a display cut down.
- **Far or glanced** (wall screens, kiosks, vehicle panels, signs). Default: regular to wide width, medium or bold weight, low contrast, open counters, a little extra letter spacing, a clear i, l, 1; no extra-light or condensed cuts. Test at the real distance or a scaled one.
- **Dark or dim grounds.** No firm evidence on polarity here; say so and test on the real screen.
- **Size floors.** The smallest size read at full speed in a lab is a floor, not a target. Go well above it, remember that a more distant screen makes the same CSS size subtend a smaller angle, and state the distance you assumed.

## Unusual or new brand letterforms

- Expect liking to dip before speed does. In one small experiment (adults, printed text, about 20 minutes, speed only) readers of unusual letters caught up in speed while still liking them less. Do not present that as settled.
- Default: conventional letter skeletons at text sizes, unusual a, e, g or s shapes at display sizes. Change when the face passes the confusable test at the smallest size.
- Judge a launch by errors on codes, comfort feedback and complaints, not task time alone; speed seldom separates faces.

## Readers the evidence barely covers

Older readers, low vision, dyslexia-specific fonts, contrast and polarity are thinly tested or untested. Say so rather than inventing a rule.

- Supported: modestly looser letter spacing helped young and some dyslexic readers on single words; adults reading stories gained nothing. Default normal spacing; offer looser as a reader setting.
- For these groups default larger sizes, clear letterforms and sturdy weights; change when testing with the group says otherwise. Do not call a "dyslexia font" proven.
