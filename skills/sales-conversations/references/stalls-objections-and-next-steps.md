# Stalls, objections and next steps

Load for a silent or stuck buyer, an objection, "send me more information", a price pushback, a lost deal, or the close of any conversation. Every example is invented. Spoken objection handling rests on thin evidence, so treat the sequences below as defaults to adapt, not laws. The wording of terms and concessions is in offers-and-value-propositions, the wording of a written message in email-and-sequences, and whether a move is pressure in fair-persuasion's sales reference.

## First question: has the buyer said they want to change?

- **No.** The buyer is not stalled; the problem was never agreed. Go back to discovery and size it in their numbers.
- **Yes, and now they are quiet or deferring.** Either they prefer what they have, so the problem was not big enough (ask what would have to be true for it to matter), or they want to change but cannot commit, because choosing wrong would cost them personally and standing still costs nothing visible. The second is common after a good meeting, and its remedy is the opposite of more pressure.

"I need to think about it" after clear interest is the strongest warning, unless the buyer names a specific dated wait (a board meeting on the 14th). Postponing with an intent and a date differs from drifting with "revisit next year".

This diagnosis is a judgment call, not a measurement. Use it to pick a first move and revise when the buyer's reply says otherwise.

## Which fear

Name the fear before choosing a reply. More than one can be present; handle the one that blocks first.

| The buyer sounds like | Fear | First move |
|---|---|---|
| "I can't tell which of these is right", comparisons keep changing | Cannot rank the options | One recommendation with a reason and a flip condition |
| "I want to look into a few more things", asks for more material or references | Has not done enough homework | Name the few things that matter, and offer the one route that answers them |
| "What if it doesn't deliver?" | Will not get what they pay for | A believable expected result and a real safety net |

## The four moves, in default order

1. **Recommend.** "Given [their reason], I'd start with [option]. If [condition], [other option] fits better." Asking a stuck buyer more needs-questions adds load. Offering a recommendation needs earned credibility and a disclosed interest: if the recommended option pays the seller more, say so. Change when the buyer already has a clear, informed preference.
2. **Limit research.** The buyer does not need every document. Be their guide: name the short list of things that settle the decision, or point to one or two independent sources. Decide before certainty; more material past a point delays the decision rather than improving it. When someone asks for a fourth reference or another demo, ask the question behind it first: "What would that settle?" If a reference call will not settle it, propose what would (a test on their data). Say plainly when a request will not help, and never discourage checking a claim that matters.
3. **Take risk off the table.** Set a believable expected result, not the best case, and build the plan on it. Offer a safety net that exists (a window to change plan, a scoped pilot with an owner and a success test, a short first term, named support). If none exists, say what does. Say what the product will not do; a seller who says so is believed on the rest.
4. **Start smaller.** Suggest the smaller first step and what the later one would be.

Do not do the following to a worried buyer: re-list the cost of waiting, add a deadline, or send "last chance". They already know the cost; more fear raises the cost of acting wrongly in their mind. A cost-of-delay figure belongs only before the buyer has agreed the problem, and only in their own numbers.

## Testing intent

To tell intention from politeness, ask for a step that costs the buyer something real (a meeting with their legal or security reviewer, a pull of their data), or ask plainly what is going on ("Where did this land after you took it back to the team?"). Skip the test when the request would itself be an imposition.

Deeply indecisive deals exist. Rank them lower in the seller's own plan, say so, and do not forecast them as late-stage. Never quietly penalise such a buyer in price or service.

## An objection, step by step

1. Let the buyer finish. Ask what exactly worries them and what is underneath it.
2. Tie it to their stated outcome: "You said [outcome] is the priority. Where does [objection] sit against that?" Then stop talking. Do not act out confusion you do not feel; if you see a mismatch with their own earlier words, state it and ask what changed.
3. Answer with a figure or a visual the user supplied. If the objection is a real gap in what is sold, concede it and say what you would do.
4. Check that it landed with a specific question ("Does that settle the integration worry, or is there more to it?"), not "does that make sense?" Invite "I don't know".
5. Return to the decision, and ask whether anything else is in the way. Answer every stated objection; when agreement sounds soft, raise the unstated one gently.

## "Send me more information"

Ask what it would help them decide, and then send the one thing that answers it, with a date to talk about it. If the request is a way to end the call, accept it gracefully and set a dated check-in. Sending a document is fine when it is the right thing; the message goes through email-and-sequences.

## Price in conversation

Default: price comes after value, as one figure with what it covers and what it replaces, then stop talking. If asked before value is built, give a range and what moves it. "That's more than we expected": restate their outcome, state the price once, ask how it compares with what they had in mind, and stop. Ask how far apart you are. A small difference is a trade conversation; a large one means rebuild the return together and ask where it breaks. Offer a smaller scope before a lower price, and give nothing without getting something back for a stated reason (term, volume, payment). Money often already goes to the problem (overtime, a service, a hire): ask where, and consider a later start. Setting the price is pricing-and-packaging; concession wording is in the offers skill.

## A dated, owned next step

- Before ending, say what you propose next and get a yes. It names who, what and when, and goes on both calendars.
- "I'll follow up", "call me anytime" and "let me know" are not next steps.
- Confirm it in writing the same day, in the original thread if one exists.
- If the buyer misses a step, say so without heat and ask whether this is still a priority.
- A check-in to a stalled buyer names both exits: "Shall we keep going on [step], or close this out and talk again in [month]?"

## When it ends

A stated no is a no. Ask one clarifying line if useful ("Is it timing, or not a fit?"), stop, and do not switch channel to get round it. Record the reason in the buyer's words and set a dated return only if they welcomed one.

## Worked example

Invented: a compost-pickup service ran a six-week pilot with an office tower. The facilities lead said the team loved it, then went quiet.

Before: "Just checking in! Every week without pickup is more waste going to landfill, and our summer slots are filling up."

After, spoken or written: "You told me the pilot met your target of [number] and the tenants asked to keep it. The worry I usually hear at this point is whether it will hold up across all floors. I'd suggest we extend to the two floors you named, on a month-to-month term, and agree the three numbers we'll judge it by. If it misses [target], you stop with nothing further owed. Would Thursday at 2 work to set those numbers together?"

This one names the likely fear, recommends a smaller step, offers a real exit, and ends on a dated question. It states no deadline and repeats no cost of waiting.
