# Buying groups

Load when more than one person has to agree: several signers, a finance, security or legal review, "I'll take it to the team", or a deal that went quiet after a good demo with one contact. Every example is invented. A purchase one person decides alone skips this file; ask only for the date and answer the question asked.

## Which agreement is missing

A group has to agree on three things in order: that there is a problem, what kind of fix suits it, and which supplier. Sellers mostly argue the third, because it is the one they can influence, while the group is still split on the first or second. Name which one is open and put the call there. If people describe different problems, no demo helps; the next step is getting them to one description.

## One statement every role can sign

Per-person discovery is still right (see discovery and qualifying). What goes wrong is selling each person a version tuned to them: each says yes alone, then they compare notes and the deal shrinks or stalls. After the one-on-ones, write one problem statement in plain words that each role would accept, with the figure each role is held to beside it, and show every person what the others need. Check: the plan contains the shared statement, not four pitches.

## Whom to build with

The person to work with is the one who drives change, not the most senior, friendliest or most available contact. Test without tricks: do they engage with a point that challenges them and push back with questions; do they talk about what the group needs and not only their own gain; will they do a real piece of work you ask for (pull a figure, name the reviewer); can they get the right people into one meeting. A plan names the test it will use, not a title. A senior sponsor who is warm and does none of these is a route to a signature, not an ally.

## The cost of the current way

If the group has not agreed there is a problem, size what the current way costs in their own figures, and bring more evidence the more surprising the claim is. Once they have agreed and stalled, do not repeat it: use the stuck-buyer moves in stalls, objections and next steps. No fear, no seller-made deadline.

## Convene, then facilitate

When people disagree, bring them together rather than presenting to them.

- Brief each person beforehand and ask permission to raise the disagreements you have heard. Do not name one person's private worry in front of the others.
- Keep the session to the problem and the kind of fix, not your product. Keep it small; split a large group.
- One person runs the room, another writes.
- End with one agreed statement, a named owner for each open item and a date.

## Equip the ally

Give the ally what they can forward without looking like your agent: what each role will compare you with and how to answer in that role's terms, a business case in the buyer's own figures that they can edit, and the supplier section last.

## Blockers

Ask early who could stop this and why. Assume a quiet influencer has an objection until you have heard it. Order of moves: let supporters and the group discussion answer it; then talk to the blocker about their specific concern; then offer a narrow concession that leaves the core intact and that the supporters accept; escalate to a senior sponsor last, with a fair account of the blocker's reasons.

## Progress is what the buyer did

Define each stage by an observable buyer action: criteria written down, the signer named, the reviewer's slot booked. What the seller sent does not count. In a review of the deal, ask "how do you know it is there?".

## Late price pressure

If the group asks for a cut after agreeing, the agreed way of working stays; trade only items that do not change it (term, payment, volume steps). Price wording is in stalls, objections and next steps.

## Worked example

Invented: a seller of a shift-swap app to a regional ambulance service. Contacts: the operations chief, a payroll lead and two paramedic supervisors. After the one-on-ones the chief wants fewer overtime calls, payroll wants clean hours and the supervisors want fair swaps. The missing agreement is the first: nobody has said whether the problem is cost, accuracy or fairness.

Plan: one shared line ("Swaps are arranged by phone, so overtime is unplanned, hours are rekeyed and supervisors take the blame for unfair ones"), each role's figure beside it marked `[to learn]`, and a 45-minute session with the chief asking permission beforehand to raise that the roles want different things. Not a demo. The ally test is whether the payroll lead pulls three months of rekeyed hours by Friday. Stage reached: "criteria written down", not "proposal sent". The session ends with the statement, an owner for each figure, and the next meeting date.
