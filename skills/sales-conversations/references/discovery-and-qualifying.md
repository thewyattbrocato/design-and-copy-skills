# Discovery and qualifying

Load for a discovery or qualifying plan, a question list, call notes to review, a second stakeholder, or a decline. Every example is invented. A call whose purpose is to learn rather than sell goes to user-research-basics, and the two are never mixed in one call: say at the start which one this is.

## Start from what is known

Default: before any question, write two lists. Known: what the user supplied or the buyer published, with the source beside each. Unknown: what the plan must learn. Open the call by confirming the first list in a sentence or two, framed as saving the buyer's time ("I read that you run [number] sites and recently opened [role]; is that current?"). Ask only about the second list. A researched fact asked again as an open question tells the buyer nothing was prepared.

Change when the user supplied nothing: do not pretend to know. Open with why you are asking and what the buyer gets from answering, then begin with their situation.

## A number for every vague answer

Default: each question in the plan carries a tag for the number it should yield, and the follow-up for a vague answer is written next to it.

| Buyer says | Ask for | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| "Too slow" | How long it takes now, and how long it needs to take | minutes, days |
| "A lot of errors" | How many last month, and what the last one cost | count, money, time |
| "We're growing" | What changes in size, by when | units, date |
| "It's a hassle" | Who does it, how often, how long each time | people, hours per week |

When the buyer has no figure, write `[to learn]` and make getting it the next step ("could your operations lead pull the last three months?"). Do not estimate it for them, and do not suggest a number in a question so the buyer can agree with it.

Change when the stake is not money (safety, a legal date, standing with a customer). Then record the outcome in the buyer's words, say plainly that it is not priced, and do not manufacture a dollar value.

## Fault, consequence, cause, motive

Four questions separate a good plan from a checklist. Each is answered in the buyer's terms.

- **The technical fault.** What is not working, in the buyer's words.
- **The business consequence.** What that costs this buyer, on a measure they are held to. The same fault costs a warehouse lead and a finance director different things; ask each.
- **The cause underneath.** What the problem sits on. Two buyers with the same symptom can have different causes, and only one may be something you can change. If the cause is outside what you sell, say so and stop there.
- **The motive.** Why the target is what it is. "Why 95 and not 99?" often exposes a promise someone made to someone else, and it names who really cares. If the contact is not the person with the motive, find that person next.

Ask what has been tried and what happens if nothing changes. Close by asking what you should have asked and did not.

## Who else, and how they buy

Default: ask who uses it, who decides, who can stop it, how a purchase like this was made last time, and what date is driving it. Change when the buyer is a single owner who decides alone; ask only the date.

- A new stakeholder is a new discovery. A peer in the same role can want something different, and one more function (legal, security, finance) can turn a yes into a no. Plan a short discovery for each, using their measure, not the first contact's, then reconcile them into one shared problem statement (see [buying groups](buying-groups.md)).
- Check the buyer's selection criteria against their own stated outcome. When a checklist leaves out what they said matters most, say the mismatch plainly and ask what changed ("You told me [outcome] matters most; the list doesn't mention it. Is the list the final word?"). Ask it only when you really see a mismatch, and quote their words correctly.
- "No budget" and "can't afford it" are different. Ask which. Where a problem is large, money usually already goes somewhere (a service, overtime, a hire): ask where.

## Qualify the account, not only the contact

Default: write the account traits that must be true for a conversation to be worth having (the problem exists, the size is right, the buyer type fits) apart from the traits that make it bigger (more sites, more users, more pain), and rank by the second. Change when the traits cannot be seen from outside: ask for them in the first call, early, and without making them a quiz.

Four things a buyer must agree to, in their own words: the problem is real, it can be fixed, they want it fixed, and they will work with you on it. A pleasant contact at a good account is a route in. A poor account gets a quick, kind decline, which may bring a referral.

## The kind decline

State the reason and what would change it, wish them well, stop. Written versions go to email-and-sequences.

"From what you've described, [volume] a week is below where [product] pays back, and I would rather say that now than sell you something that sits unused. If that number grows past [threshold] I'd be glad to talk again. If it helps, [type of alternative] fits that size better."

Fill every bracket from user-supplied facts. Never pad a decline with a discount.

## Reviewing call notes

Default test: strip the buyer's name. If a colleague could not tell which deal it is, the notes are generic; list the missing specifics (size, current process, cost, owner, date, who signs). Then check for a number for every pain, the buyer's own words for the outcome, a next step with a date and owner, and what the buyer committed to. "Interested, send pricing" is not a note.

Rank findings: goal and fit, then numbers, then stakeholders, then wording.

## Worked example

Invented: a seller of door-access alarms for university research freezers has a lead from a conference list. Known (from the lead form): the lab runs [number] freezers and recently lost samples. Goal: learn whether the problem is worth solving, then book a second call with whoever manages the building.

Before: "Tell me about your pain points. What's your budget? Who's the decision maker?"

After, in order:
1. Confirm: "You mentioned a sample loss last quarter; is that the event I should have in mind?" (confirms a supplied fact)
2. "Walk me through how a freezer fault is caught today, who gets the alert, and how long it took last time." (minutes)
3. "What was in the freezer, and what did replacing it cost or take?" (money, weeks)
4. "What has to be true next quarter for this not to repeat? Why that bar?" (target, motive)
5. "Who else decides on equipment in that room, and who would object?" (people)
6. "What should I have asked that I didn't?"
7. Next step: "Can the facilities manager join a 20-minute call Thursday at 10? I'll send the invite and a one-line summary today." (who, what, when)

If the answer to step 3 is "we replaced a shelf of cheap samples", the plan's response is to say the fit is weak and offer a lighter option, not to find a bigger story.
