# Calls and voicemail

Load for a cold-call opening, a voicemail, a gatekeeper, or booking a meeting by phone. Every example is invented, and every fact in a script is a bracket for the user to fill. Written outreach, cadence and the stop rule are in email-and-sequences; whether persistence has become pressure is in fair-persuasion. Evidence for gatekeeper handling is thin, so treat that part as defaults.

## One purpose per touch

Choose one: book a meeting, learn one fact, sell outright (only for a small, simple purchase), or stay familiar through something useful. Default: for a costly or complex sale ask for a meeting; for a small one ask for the sale; where you know nothing ask for one fact. Change when the buyer has told you their window (a renewal date, a launch): ask for the meeting that fits it.

A scheduled meeting gets a written plan. A cold dial gets one line of context and a clear ask.

## The opening, in about ten seconds

Five parts, spoken without a pause: the person's name; who you are and where from; why you are calling, in one clause; a true reason this person in this role is worth calling; one specific ask with a time.

"Hi [name], this is [your name] at [company]. I'm calling because [one true thing the user gave you]. Teams in that position often [problem the user named]. Could we take [a short slot] on [day, time] so I can ask how you handle it now?"

Then stop talking. The silence after the ask belongs to the buyer.

- Skip "how are you today?"; it invites a brush-off at the pause. Centre the buyer: "I'd like to learn how you handle it" beats "I want to tell you about our platform".
- The reason must be true and checkable. With no supplied detail, use a bracket or a role-level statement. Never invent a mutual contact, a prior meeting, an article they wrote or a result you were not given. Name feelings only as a hedged guess.
- Ask for a concrete slot, not "sometime next week". A meeting is booked when it sits in both calendars at a stated time: say you are sending the invite now, with the purpose in its title.

## Answers on the call

A yes: confirm time and attendees and send the invite before hanging up. A request for information: ask what it would help them decide, offer the one thing that answers it and a time to discuss. A "maybe": one question about what would make it worth their time, then accept the answer. A clear no: thank them, stop, and do not return through another channel.

## Gatekeepers

Give your name, your company and the reason in plain words. Ask who handles [the area] and how best to reach them. Do not imply a relationship ("they're expecting my call"), pretend to be someone else, or hint at going over their head. If told to send an email, ask for a name and write it through email-and-sequences.

## Voicemail

Default: leave one only where it matters (a short list of important accounts, or someone in a known buying window). Change when the buyer asked you to call back, or you are returning a call they really made.

About 30 seconds, roughly 75 spoken words: your name and company; your number once, slowly; the reason in one honest sentence; one specific thing you can share as a reason to call back; your name and number again.

"This is [name] with [company], [phone]. I'm calling because [reason]. I have [one specific item] I'd like to share. Again, [name], [company], [phone]."

Never disguise it: no "returning your call" when they did not call, no borrowed familiarity. If it goes unreturned, the next touch adds something or you stop. The number of touches is a judgment call to label as one; any series ends at a clear no and does not hop channels.

## Worked example

Invented: a pallet-wrap supplier calls a warehouse manager who, per the user's list, advertised a second-shift lead opening.

"Hi [name], this is [your name] at [company]. I'm calling because [site] posted a second-shift lead opening on [date]. Sites adding a shift often find wrap usage jumps and reorder timing slips. Could we find a quarter of an hour Thursday at 10 so I can ask how you're planning for that?"
