# Demos and trials

Load for a demo plan or critique, questions during a demo, or any trial, pilot or "can we try it first" request. Every example is invented. Slide design for a pitch is in presentation-design; whether a trial's terms are fair is in fair-persuasion; the wording of a pilot offer is in offers-and-value-propositions.

## Is a demo the right next step?

Default: no demo until the gate in the main skill is met: a problem someone feels and can size, a person who signs or a route to one, and something the buyer will put in. Keep the demo out of the discovery meeting and give it its own slot, so what is shown can follow what was learned. Change when the product is self-serve and a two-minute walk-through is how the buyer learns what they need, when the meeting is openly labelled exploratory, or when a trade-show visitor will not wait; use the menu below.

If the gate is not met, the reply names what is missing and proposes the step that fills it (a call with the person who signs, a figure the buyer pulls, a ten-minute check of their data), not a softer demo.

## One purpose per meeting

Choose one: prove abilities the buyer has already named, help the buyer see a solution to a problem they have not yet put into words (say it is that kind of session), or inform. An inform-only demo has no basis for choosing what to show; push it toward one of the other two.

## Plan around people, then cut

Default: for each attendee write three things.

- **Problem:** what they are measured on this quarter or year.
- **Reason:** why that is not being met today.
- **Ability:** what the product must do to remove the reason.

Follow the chain upward: one person's reason is their manager's problem, so show the gain to the most senior person who will be in the room. A junior attendee who cannot name a goal can be asked how their work is judged. If nobody in the room feels the problem, do not demo; go back to discovery.

Then sort every feature into three lists and write them down before building anything: **in** (answers a named problem), **out** (never shown here), **reserve** (shown only if someone asks or interest shows). Mark the cut line: if the time halves, everything below it goes. Order by importance, not menu order.

## Shape of the session

Default for a product with a short path to a result:

1. **Situation line** (a few seconds): who, the problem, the reason, the ability needed, the change in numbers, ending "is that right?"
2. **The result first**, finished and in a form the audience recognises (the report or screen they would actually use), before any steps.
3. **The shortest path** to it with no commentary.
4. **Summary**: the needed ability and the change.
5. **Detailed pass**, only for the abilities agreed, ordered by what they asked about.
6. **Summary**, then parked questions, then a final summary naming each ability and the next step with who and when.

Timing defaults for a short-path product: the fast pass in one to two minutes, the whole content in roughly a quarter to half an hour. Change when the path to a result is long, the audience is large, or the time slot is fixed; state the minutes your plan rests on. End each segment on the strongest screen and leave it up during summaries and questions.

## Data and honesty

- Use the buyer's names, formats and a real sample of their output only where they agreed to share it; check confidentiality before showing it to others.
- Label made-up data "sample data" on screen. A seller saying "ignore that" or "imagine this is yours" is a sign the data is wrong.
- Replace empty adjectives ("seamless", "powerful") with a count, a measured figure, an attributed quote or a thing shown. If none exists, drop the line. Whether a figure is true is honest-claims' question.
- A volunteer is real or labelled rehearsed; never stage a "spontaneous" question.

## Questions during the demo

Triage each question as it arrives.

- **Answer now:** it bears on an agreed ability and takes a sentence or two. A plain yes or no is a full answer; stop talking after it.
- **Park:** reasonable but would derail. Say "good question, I'll answer it properly after the last section," write it where everyone sees it, ask the asker if that is fine, and then actually answer it. Answer parked items at the end and let people who are satisfied leave.
- **Hostile or confused:** park it the same way, with the same respect. Answer sooner if the asker feels brushed off.

Restate the question and confirm it before answering; it also tells you whom to look at.

**"Can it do X?"** If yes, show it in a few seconds and stop when they are satisfied. If it can but not today, say so and give the date or the next step. If it cannot: "No, not today." Offer the nearest option if there is one, ask whether this is a nice-to-have or a real need, and record it for the user's product team. Never promise a feature that does not exist, and never ask "would you buy it if we built it?" (that extracts a promise about nothing).

**A question you cannot answer.** Say you do not know; add what you do know only if it bears on their decision; name who will find out and by when, and send it by that date. Never guess a capability, number or date, and if an earlier answer proves wrong, correct it in writing quickly.

**A step fails.** Cosmetic glitch: carry on. Serious: say you hit a bug, show what should have happened (a prepared screenshot), move on and follow up; never retry the same step in front of the room.

## Unknown or mixed audiences

- **Unqualified visitor:** ask one open question ("what are you hoping to get done here?"), then offer a menu of three to five situation-and-result pairs, two minutes each, and stop when none fits.
- **A member who openly opposes:** plan for them by name. Before the meeting, ask for a short separate conversation to hear their reasons. In the meeting, state their strongest reason fairly before your case and agree the decision criteria with the group. Do not try to convert them in the room; aim at the undecided members and at the opponent feeling heard. See [buying groups](buying-groups.md).
- **Unplanned group:** ask each person for name, role and the one thing they want to see; write them on a board, marking what the product does not cover; show the senior and key contacts' items first and tick items off at the end.

## Critiquing a demo plan or recording

Ask: when does the first thing the customer would recognise as their answer appear; does what was shown match what was agreed; how many steps and minutes to the main result; were there summaries; how were questions handled; are there adjectives with no fact. Rank fixes: audience fit, structure, logistics. "Be more engaging" is not a finding.

## Trials and pilots

A request for a trial is a request for proof. Default: ask what the buyer wants to be sure of, and offer the smallest thing that settles it, such as a guided session where the buyer holds the controls on their own data. Run a structured trial or pilot only when all of these exist in writing:

- a business problem with an agreed success test, in numbers the buyer helped choose
- a named signer and a route to purchase
- dates with checkpoints, and a stop point if the first measure fails
- named owners on both sides, and what each will do
- the price or free terms, and the end date

Do not run one because the buyer wants to be rid of you, wants an education, or because you merely hope. Change when a trial is the product's normal front door; keep the success task and the end date.

It ends on the agreed date. If paperwork is late, offer an extension at a stated price or a dated read-only period; do not pull access to speed a signature. Continuation is opt-in: the plan names the price and the decision date, and nothing is bought until the buyer says yes in writing. Both follow fair-persuasion's rules on conversion.

## Worked example

Invented: a vendor sells a bus-routing tool to a school district transport office. Attendees: the director, two dispatchers, a finance clerk. Gate: dispatchers spend most Sunday evenings rebuilding routes (felt); the director signs under a set amount (route); the dispatchers will share last year's route files (input).

Director: problem, late buses reported to the board; reason, routes rebuilt by hand each term; ability, rebuild in minutes after a roster change. Dispatchers: the same ability, felt as Sunday-evening rework. Clerk: problem, fuel overrun; reason, unknown route lengths; ability, a route length report.

In: rebuild after a roster change; length report. Out: parent app. Reserve: driver hours, sign-off workflow. Open on a sample district's rebuilt routes, labelled sample, before any clicks; park the parent-app question on the board.
