# Surveys, measured studies and inclusive practice

Load for a survey or questionnaire to write or review, a sample or response plan, success, time and rating numbers from a test, or recruiting, paying and consenting people. Starting values; change them when the situation says otherwise.

## Survey or interview?

A survey returns a number: how many, how much, which of these. It cannot find out why, and it cannot find options nobody has named yet.

- Default: talk to five to eight people from the group first, then survey. Their stories supply the answer choices and the words; the survey sizes what they found. Change when the themes are already known from tickets, earlier studies or last quarter's survey.
- Before any question, write the decision, the number that would change it and the sentence you will report ("Most new users who quit say setup took longer than expected"). If that sentence cannot be drafted, interview first.
- Do not ask what a log, order table or analytics already knows. Do not ask for a forecast of their own behavior ("would you buy", "how often would you use"); ask about the last time, or test the behavior.
- Do not ask "what do you dislike" to find problems to fix: people forget small frictions. Watch a few of them do the task.
- Someone insists on a survey where watching would do: keep it short, tell them the limit in one line, and run it.

## Length and channel

- Default: one question you really need plus a few that bear on it. Every question maps to a decision or is cut and saved for later; "nice to know" is the cut line.
- A prompt inside a page earns one question. An emailed link supports about ten minutes: roughly ten closed questions and one open box. Phone or in person can run longer if it is a real conversation.
- Many short surveys beat one long one. Keep wording identical across rounds only when tracking a number over time; otherwise fix a bad question.

## Writing the questions

- Use the respondent's words, not the team's labels. One idea per question: "easy and fast" is two questions.
- Name the experience being rated, and one comparison if it matters ("the checkout you just finished"). "How satisfied are you with us" has no answer anyone can defend.
- Ask about recent, notable events. Routine behavior fades fast: "how many times did you open the app today" gets a guess, so ask about yesterday's last session or watch the data.
- Do not presume: "what did you like" assumes something was liked. Offer "none" and "don't remember" where they are real answers.
- Ask recall first, then show a list; a list steers what people recall.
- Closed choices: radio buttons for exactly one, checkboxes for several, and always a "something else" with a text box. Read the write-ins; a crowd of them means the options are wrong.
- Numbers: start with an open box in the pilot. Preset bands tell people what is typical and pull answers toward the middle. Build bands from the pilot's real spread, add "I don't track this", and keep a box if the number could identify someone.
- Default: no drop-downs for long lists, no grids, no sliders, no ranking, no required questions. Radios, a text box or a short list from the pilot do the job and survive phones and screen readers. This is for opinion or optional-fact questionnaires; a form that needs an answer to finish a purchase or sign-up keeps its field rules (form-design).
- Sensitive or identity questions (gender, health, income, family): ask only if the decision needs it and put it last. Free text or "prefer not to say" and one line on why you ask. Never screen people out after they have spent effort; give them one easy question instead.
- Order: easy and interesting first, personal last, grouped by topic and not by who requested the question. Skip an introduction page; put one short sentence above question one.

## Rating scales

- Default: five points, odd, only the ends labeled, positive end consistently on one side. Statements about one attitude each; no reverse-worded items (people misread them); no "should".
- Prefer a direct question with tailored options over a scale when you can: "Did you register? Yes, easily / yes, with trouble / no / don't remember" beats "Registration was easy: 1-5".
- If a scale is mandatory, add an optional comment box. Pair an ease or satisfaction rating with "what were you trying to do" for the story.
- Decide the scoring before collecting (counts per option, top two boxes, or a sum), and keep to it. Report the spread of answers, never only the average: ten ones and ten sevens average to a four that nobody picked.
- An established short questionnaire beats one you invent; score it as published and treat the result as a barometer for tracking or comparing in one study, not a list of what to fix. Recommend-to-a-friend scores are noisy at small n, are not a measure of behavior, and need a follow-up question; use one only if the organization already tracks it.

## Sample, response and who is missing

- Define the group in exact in and out terms ("paid customers who bought in the last twelve months"), then pick the source: a list you own, people caught at the moment, or a chain of referrals. Check the list for staff, duplicates, lapsed and test accounts. Referral chains cannot be generalized.
- Invitations = answers needed divided by the response rate you expect. Starting rates: email to your own customers 5-15%, an invitation inside a page well under 1%, a banner far less. Replace the guess with the pilot's rate. Never invent a sample size from a calculator without saying what rate and confidence it assumed.
- A big response count is not a representative one. Ten thousand answers can be ten thousand fans. Add one or two questions on traits that bear on the answer (how long a customer, plan, role), place them last and compare them with what you know about the whole group. Say who is missing beside every result.
- Trust, effort and reward trade against each other. A small guaranteed reward beats a draw; saying what will be done with answers, and doing it, helps more than a bigger prize. Send from a named person.
- Invitation, under about 200 words: who you are, why this person, what it is for, how many questions, the closing date, how to reach a human. Claim a duration only if tested. Test with images blocked. The thank-you page does not sell.
- Send the questions you wrote to people who will tell you if they break: a one-at-a-time read-aloud with three to five people, fix, then different people. Include someone using assistive technology and someone who reads slowly.
- Pilot on about a tenth of the sample or at least 20 people, run exactly like the real thing, including cleaning and a draft report. Do not cut the pilot to save time; shorten the fieldwork instead. Screenshot every page for the record.
- Clean with a log: remove test entries and duplicate rows, check impossible values (age 5, 20 years of experience), set a speeder rule, redact names, emails and rare job titles from open answers before sharing.

## Reporting survey results

- State the count and the base: "54 of the 78 who answered, out of 300 invited". "69%" with no base is already a claim about everyone.
- A result from a convenience sample is a description of the people who answered. Do not print "significant", margins of error or a confidence level for it.
- When views split, show the spread and the strong minority; an average hides two camps.
- Pull quotes to illustrate a count, never to replace it. Code open answers against the decision (topic, owner, a short phrase), and keep the original column.
- Titles that state the finding ("Setup took longer than expected for most who quit") and a table beside any chart.

## Measured studies: tasks, time, errors, ratings

Use numbers when the decision needs them: a baseline, a target, a comparison. For early sketches, a throwaway prototype or a quick look, skip metrics and do a qualitative round. Say which kind of study it is: diagnosing a design that can still change (small groups, issues by severity), or judging against a target, rival or last release (needs a goal set first and many more people).

- Write the end state of each task and the success rule before the first session. Decide edge cases (right answer, wrong format) and what counts as help. Fix the stop rule (a time cap, or the person gives up) and when the clock starts and stops.
- Success: yes or no per person per task. Use levels (complete, partial, helped, failed) only when closeness matters, and report counts per level; never an average of levels or of severity ratings.
- Time: successful attempts only, unless people chose when to quit. Set the outlier rule in advance (a break in the middle, a rusher far below any feasible time), drop the whole attempt not just the time, and name the rule. Report the median or geometric mean for the typical time; the arithmetic mean follows one lunch break. Show success next to time so a fast failure does not look good.
- Errors: count per chance to make one, define each error type before the session, and do not count one slip twice.
- Think-aloud slows people down. Time silent runs or compare with a replay.
- Ratings after a task flatter a task that went well. Ask how sure they are they finished, and look for people confident and wrong. Report ratings beside performance and do not treat one as proof of the other.
- Learning: the same task over three or four repetitions spaced as real use is; report where it flattens, not only the first run.
- Combining: count the people who met every target together ("3 of 10 met all three"), not the average of each. Averages can pass while few individuals do.

### Small counts

Default: "6 of 8", no percentage. If someone insists on a rate, give the count and then a range, never a bare percent. Use an interval that holds up at small n, not the plain normal formula, which can run past 100%. At 95%, rounded:

| Result | Plausible true rate |
|---|---|
| 4 of 5 | 38-96% |
| 6 of 8 | 41-93% |
| 12 of 15 | 55-93% |
| 16 of 20 | 58-92% |
| 80 of 100 | 71-87% |

- Comparing two designs with different people: heavy overlap of the ranges means you cannot call a winner; say what you can see (where people went wrong, how many) and what would settle it. The same people on both: look at each person's difference, and mix the order. Learning from the first design leaks into the second, so use different people when it matters.
- Sample size follows purpose. Finding problems: five to eight per round and per kind of user, more for high stakes or a broad product. Coverage of tasks finds more problems than extra people. Success rates to judge a target: tens of people per group (30 is the floor and noisy). Small differences or preferences: hundreds, or live traffic. Say these are rough.
- Several observers find different problems; one watcher misses things. Rate severity by what it did to the task (recovered, worked around, failed) and have two people rate independently.
- Do not report a decimal the data cannot support.

## Inclusive and sensitive research

Scale this to the stakes: new flows touching money, health, identity, safety or essential services get the full care; a wording tweak does not.

- Pay every participant, in an amount that respects their time, stated before they agree, and pay in full to someone who stops early or turns out not to fit. A gift card is fine if that is what they would choose. Unpaid asks of people with the least spare time exclude them.
- Consent in plain words: purpose, what is recorded, who sees it, how long it is kept, and that they can skip or stop at any time. Ask separately for recording. Rules about recording and storing personal data vary by place and change, so have the form checked.
- Recruit by behavior, ability and context, not by labels. Use open screener questions ("describe how you last paid a bill") so unexpected, useful people are not filtered out by a checklist. Include people who read slowly, use assistive technology, speak the language as a second one, or have little bandwidth when the product depends on them.
- Do not ask one person to speak for a group, and do not call one account "what people like you want". Ask about their own last time and report it as one person's account. Look for the pattern across several.
- You cannot interview people in the middle of a crisis. Ask about a past occurrence, or use a role-play they agreed to, and stop if they tire or are upset. Sensitive topics and vulnerable groups still go to a trained researcher; give the plan outline only.
- Study the setting where it is reachable: lighting, noise, device, who else is in the room.
- Read a clean result with suspicion: "all eight found the new menu" means little if all eight were calm, fast, sighted and familiar with the product. Say who was not in the room.
- Audit your channels. Feedback that arrives only through in-app chat comes from people who can use in-app chat. Use analytics to show where people struggle, a few conversations to learn why, and name who never appears in either.
- The research materials follow the same care: an accessible consent form, a questionnaire usable with a screen reader, and a phone or paper route for anyone who cannot use the link.
