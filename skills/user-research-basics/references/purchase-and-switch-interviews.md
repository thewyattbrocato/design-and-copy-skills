# Purchase and switch interviews

Load when the people to talk to recently bought, switched, cancelled or looked hard and walked away, and the question is why they chose as they did: a jump in cancellations, "customers say we're easy to use", why people left a spreadsheet for a tool. Not for a product nobody has bought yet (use the main path in SKILL.md) or for watching people do a task. The backward order below is one practitioner's taste; if a person starts at the beginning on their own, follow them.

## Whom to ask

Default: a handful from each of up to three groups, all of whom decided in the last few months: people who bought or switched, people who left, and people who compared seriously and chose nothing. Six to ten in all is enough to start. Ask about the most recent decision, not the best customer's. Change when only one group is reachable (say what the missing ones would have told you) or the decision was a year or more ago (memory has tidied it; mark details as recalled).

## Open and rebuild the scene

Default: a plain frame ("I'm trying to understand how people decide on this; nothing to sell"), no printed question list, then start at the moment of the decision and step backwards: what happened just before, the day before, the first time it crossed their mind. Concrete detail brings a scene back, so ask where they were, what time of day, what was open on the screen, who was with them. "In general" brings a summary. Change when the person cannot place the moment: start from their first use and work back.

- With consent, ask who else was involved and whom they asked, then use those names in follow-ups.
- Find the event that tipped it (a bill, a breakage, a new job, a deadline); it often differs from the first reason given.
- Ask what else they looked at and tried, including living with the old way. Ask why not the one they passed over, and what it would have taken.
- Ask what nearly stopped them, and what they did about it.

## Unpack vague words

"Easy", "convenient", "better", "I just liked it" and "it was an impulse" are labels, not findings. Default: ask for the last time it showed up, then for a reference point ("easier than what?", "what did you stop having to do?") until you reach an action, a person, a number in their own units or a comparison. Use their word back, not yours ("so, fast?"). Change when the word is already concrete (an exact price, a named rival).

For an impulse, ask what they were doing earlier and when the idea first appeared; there is often an earlier history, so ask rather than assert.

## Code each story

Fill one sheet per person from their own words, with the supporting quote:

- what pushed them off the old way
- what drew them to the new one, as they described the hope
- what made them hesitate or nearly stopped them
- what kept them where they were (habit, effort to switch, time already spent)
- the deciding event, its date and any fixed deadline
- for each reason, whether it is practical, emotional or about how others see them

A gap is `[not said]`; never fill it in. A pause, laughter or a burst of detail is a cue to ask more, not proof of a feeling. Treat each reason as an account and check it against what they did (dates, order of events) where you can.

## Read across the stories

Count as n of N within each group ("4 of 6 who left named a billing surprise"). A reason from one person is a lead; a handful of interviews cannot show how common anything is. Rank worries by how many people hesitated over them, not by how vividly they were told. When a synthesis is asked for, give three to five short stories, the counted patterns, the ranked worries, what is unknown and the cheapest next check. Do not add a coding sheet or story list to a plan or guide that did not ask for one.

## Do not stage the room

No second interviewer playing a skeptic, no feigned confusion to draw out detail, no planted question or volunteer. Ask directly ("help me picture that; what was on the screen?") and warn before a sensitive turn ("the next part is about money; skip it if you like").

## Pair

Invented: a bakery that sells a weekly bread box asked why customers pause or stop.

- Before: "Why did you stop the bread box? Was it the price? Would you come back if it were cheaper?"
- After: "Take me to the last box you got. What was happening that week? When did you first think of stopping, and what was going on then? What did you do instead?"

## Checks

- Everyone interviewed decided recently; each group is named.
- No question asks about a future purchase or a wish.
- Each story has a trigger, the alternatives (including doing nothing), a hesitation and a date, or `[not said]`.
- No vague word is left standing as a finding; each is traced to an action or comparison, or flagged.
- Counts are n of N per group, with who is missing.
