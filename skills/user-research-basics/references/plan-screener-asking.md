# Plan, screener and asking

Load for a study plan, screener, interview guide, a leading-question rewrite, bias notes or consent text. Starting values; change them when the situation says otherwise.

## One-page plan

Fields, in order, each a line or two: the decision and who acts; the assumption and what it costs if wrong; evidence already in hand; the question (end-state verb); method and smallest version; who, by behavior; screener; roles; schedule; fallback; the result that would change the design; limits.

- Roles are tasks, not headcount: one person owns the checklist, one asks, a second takes notes, observers stay silent. One person may hold several.
- Fallback before the start: if recruiting runs slow, move dates, relax one criterion, or talk to fewer people now and more later. Name which date each choice hits.
- Always-on recruiting beats a scramble: a standing list of volunteers, a booked window every few weeks. Test participants are single-use; someone who has seen the design is tainted.
- Hire or hand off to a trained researcher for children, sensitive subjects, regulated fields, specialist users, a large or hostile organization, or when nobody has the time. Still deliver the plan outline.
- What a human must do is part of the plan: recruit, schedule, obtain consent, run, record, store files, debrief. Do not describe sessions as if they happened.

## Screener

- Test every question against one thing: would this person do the task this week? Ask what they did recently (a month at most), with the target activity listed among several others, and confirm they do it. Skip knowledge quizzes in a mass-audience domain.
- Name no product or company; describe the study as something generic ("a chat about everyday tools"). Multiple choice with plausible decoys, no yes/no on the target behavior.
- For live sessions add one short open question ("walk me through how you pick a weeknight dinner") to catch people who cannot describe their own thinking.
- Exclude people who work in the field studied, in design, research or marketing for the sponsor, and anyone who joined a paid study recently.
- Keep it to a few minutes. State quotas (for example, recent buyers and non-buyers) as targets, not guarantees.
- Someone who turns out not to fit still gets the incentive; set their data aside.
- Prefer real users from the product, a community the audience uses, or support contacts over friends and colleagues. Say so when only convenience people are available and temper conclusions.

## Interview guide

Order: one-line goal (for the team, not read aloud); introduction and consent; warm-up facts that double as context; six to ten topic prompts as stories; close.

- Prompts ask for the last time something happened, a walk through a typical day or week, what they did next, what went wrong, who else was involved, what they use instead. "Tell me about the last time you..." beats "How do you usually..."; "usually" invites a flattering summary.
- Probes: "say more about that", "what happened next", "why was that", a pause. Do not fill silence.
- Use the person's own words when following up; do not supply labels from your interface.
- The guide is a checklist, not a script. Let people wander; return to uncovered topics at the end.
- Close with "anything I should have asked" and permission to follow up.
- For comprehension checks of a label or message, a direct question about meaning is fine ("what would you expect that button to do").

### Leading-question scan

Rewrite any prompt containing: do you like, would you use, would you pay, don't you think, how much easier, is it important, which features do you want, or the product's name. Each becomes a request for a recent story or a behavior ("Tell me what you did in your first ten minutes. What did you look for and not find?"). Two-option or yes/no prompts become "what happened". Questions about the future become questions about the last comparable decision.

## Where bias enters

Put the ones that apply in the plan, with the counter:

- Who you asked: volunteers, friends and the easy-to-reach leave gaps. Counter: say who is missing beside every conclusion; recruit beyond easy reach.
- How you asked: tasks or wording that flatter the idea, or hint at the sponsor. Counter: have someone outside the idea review them; keep wording neutral; ask about behavior, not judgment.
- Who is in the room: nodding, shared anecdotes, visible observers, a lab; people perform or soften. Counter: rehearse; ask less and wait more; keep observers out of sight; promise anonymity; meet in the real setting when reachable.
- How you read it: the team hears what it hoped. Counter: write down in advance what would change the plan.

## Consent and safety

- Tell people the purpose in plain words, what is recorded, who will see it, how long it is kept, and that they can stop or skip. Say whether anyone else is watching.
- Get explicit permission to record before recording. Minors need a parent or guardian's consent and a design review by a trained researcher.
- End any phone or remote session if the person is driving, in danger or distressed.
- Do not run research that deceives people beyond hiding a product name, or that exists to justify harm.
- Keep identities out of notes shared widely; code participants by letter or number.
- Store recordings and notes in a shared location right after each session; name files consistently (study, participant code, date).
