# Synthesis, findings and evidence you already have

Load for notes or tickets to analyze, usability findings, personas and scenarios, or reviewing existing evidence. Starting values; change them when the material says otherwise.

## Synthesis of supplied notes

- Work only from the text given. If notes are thin (one line each), say what that limits; do not fill in detail.
- Each note is a direct quote or a neutral account of behavior, coded to its source. Interpretations live in a separate column.
- Group bottom-up; do not start from categories you chose in advance. Name a group as a need or barrier in a sentence ("compares plans with a partner away from the product"). Hold solutions until the groups exist.
- Things worth pulling from any session: goals, tasks, triggers, barriers, habits, who else is involved, tools used, the setting, and the exact words people use for things. The words become labels and tone.
- Count as "n of N". Say what the N is. Three of seven is a pattern worth checking, not a proportion of users. One source is a lead.
- Evidence strength, per finding: observed directly across several people; reported by several people; reported by one; inferred by you. Show it as a label beside the finding.
- Outliers: if someone could not be a user, record why and set them aside. If someone is a user and unusual, keep them as a lead, not a headline.
- Where people disagree with your summary or with each other, that is data; report it rather than averaging it.
- Decisions, ranked: each ties to a pattern, names the importance to the stated goal, strength of evidence and what it would cost to be wrong. Ranking uses what the user told you; if no goal was given, assume one in a line.
- Finish with unknowns and the one cheapest next check (a follow-up conversation, a question in the next session, a look at existing records).
- Keep the write-up short: goal, method and sample, patterns with counts and a quote or two, decisions, unknowns. Someone in six weeks should be able to rebuild why a call was made.

## Usability findings

- A usability problem is something that made a task hard, slow or unpleasant. A preference ("I would like dark mode") goes in a separate short list.
- For each problem: the task, N who hit it out of N tested, what happened (words and actions), what they expected, severity, and the cheapest plausible change.
- Severity: blocks the task; lets them finish with difficulty or a workaround; minor or cosmetic. Weight by how central the task is: paying, finding the address, changing an order outrank an avatar.
- Frequency in groups this small is a sorting aid. Four of five is a headline; one of seven on a core task can still be a fix; one of seven on a side task is a note. Do not print percentages.
- Order: blocking and common first; then blocking and rare, or moderate and common; the rest after. Take the cheapest effective change in each: rename, move, remove, default. Explanatory text is the last resort.
- Also ask whether the problem recurs: on many screens or every visit. A recurring minor problem outranks a one-off minor one, and many minor problems are reported together because together they cost trust.
- For each top problem, list two or three plausible causes and the smallest change that would remove each, and let the team choose which to test. One fix per problem is a guess.
- Pass marks for a benchmark are agreed before results arrive and not moved afterwards.
- Plan a retest with new people after fixes; say how many and what result confirms a fix.
- If the test or task design was weak, say what that limits.

## Personas and scenarios

- A persona is a composite of a behavior pattern seen in several real sources. Without sources, produce labeled assumptions ("hypothesis, untested") and a plan to check them. Never invent a name, photo, quote, age, hobby or job title and present it as research.
- Keep few: one per distinct behavior pattern, rarely more than three or four. Fields: behavior, goal, trigger, constraint and a quote with its source. Describe behavior, not demographics.
- A persona is a behavior pattern to design for, not a sales segment; the biggest revenue group is not automatically the one to design around.
- A scenario is a persona with a goal, a trigger and a task, told from the person's side. Use it to test requirements and to write usability tasks as short stories ("a friend is visiting; you found this page; what would you do to buy tickets") instead of instructions.
- Sign it works: the team asks "would she get this?" before "does the boss like it?".

## Evidence you already have

Support tickets, chat logs, sales or call notes, app-store reviews, internal and site search queries, analytics exports, earlier studies.

- Treat them as qualitative data with a built-in bias: the people who write in, review or search are not all users, and volume reflects who is willing to complain. Say so beside every count.
- Read a sample first; code each item by what the person was trying to do, the trigger, the barrier, and the words they used. Count items per code. Pull exact phrases as quoted from the material.
- See through the stated request to the underlying one: someone asking for a clearer error message may want to finish their purchase with another card without an error.
- Look for gaps: things people try that the product does not support, repeated workarounds, words that differ from the team's labels.
- Search queries and ticket vocabulary show what people call things; adopt it for labels.
- Low traffic can mean undiscovered or a setup error; high exits may mean the answer was found or not. Analytics needs a second source or a person to confirm.
- Prior studies and competitor material: check what was asked, who the sample was, who ran it, how old it is. Treat competitors as the real alternatives people use today, including spreadsheets, habits and doing nothing.
- State the sample you actually read and what you did not.
- Finish with the single most valuable follow-up conversation or check.
