# Testing designs and prototypes

Load to plan a test in which people try a design, prototype or live flow: what to put in front of them, how to write the tasks, how far to trust a small round. Not for the moderator's script (see running sessions), the survey-style measured study (see surveys, measures and inclusion) or judging a design without people (design-critique). Starting values; change them when the question or the setting says otherwise. A quick look at one small change needs a paragraph, not this plan: name the question, the artifact, the tasks and the stop rule, and leave the rest out.

## Start from the question, then pick the artifact

Name what the team needs to know, then the roughest thing that can answer it:

- Is the problem real and serious? Watch people do the current thing in their own setting, or show a storyboard of the problem and ask when it last happened. No design needed.
- Is the idea understood and wanted? A sketch, a clickable outline or a one-page description, with a person talking through it.
- Can people complete it? An interactive prototype with the real task order.
- Does it hold up in daily use? A pilot with real work, over weeks.

Default: the cheapest artifact that can still fail the idea. Change when the thing being tested is the fine detail (a label, a spacing, a motion); then the detail must be real.

## Set fidelity one dimension at a time

Look, content, structure and behavior need not be equally finished. Default: a rough look with real content and real structure wherever understanding is the question. Polished screens draw comments on color and spacing and read as already decided, so people stay polite; placeholder text hides whether the wording works. Raise a dimension only when the question depends on it.

When the direction is open, show two or more rival versions, in varied order, and ask what each would be good for. A single prototype invites approval. Do not edit the prototype between sessions in a round unless a blocker stops everyone; fix between rounds and say what changed.

A person can play the system: typing the answers by hand, or delivering the service manually behind a simple front. Use it to learn what people ask for before anything is built. Say in the plan who plays it and what they may not do.

## Write the tasks

Start from a goal in the person's words, not a click path. Task forms, with a default:

- One known right answer ("find the cheapest plan that includes two seats"). Default; it gives a clear end state and a success rule.
- Show the result and ask them to reach it, when describing the goal in words would hand them the interface's labels.
- Ask for their own goal first, then make it specific. Use when engagement decides whether the test is realistic.
- Use their own data or something real at stake (a real order, a real calendar). Use when made-up data makes people careless.
- Recreate a fault, and start where they would look for help.

Never use the interface's own labels or menu names in the task text, because that turns finding into matching. Cover more tasks rather than more people when the product is broad. Set the end state and the pass rule before the first session.

## Ask about value, not only ease

A flow can pass every task and still fail because nobody needs it. After the tasks, ask what they would do next if they decided to use it, who else would have to agree, what would stop them and what they would use instead today. Keep these after the last task so they do not disturb it.

## What a small round can tell you

A handful of people reliably surfaces problems that hit a large share of users. A problem that hits one person in ten may need several times as many sessions to show up. So:

- Never say a round "found most problems". Say what kind it was likely to catch.
- Run small rounds with fixes between them, rather than one large round.
- Tilt problem-finding rounds toward less practiced or less confident people; experts hide what newcomers trip on.
- A round that finds nothing is a statement about that round, not about the design.

These counts are rough starting values; say so.

## Reading a pilot

A pilot overstates success when the designers are present, managers are watching or parts are faked by hand. Say which applied, and plan a check some weeks after those conditions end. Report the pilot as "worked with help", not "works".

## Pair

Invented: a clinic's online refill request, a prototype test.

- Before: "We tested the refill screen with seven people and found most problems. Task: click Request refill, then Confirm." The task names the labels and the claim is about coverage.
- After: "Task: you are nearly out of your blood pressure tablets and have a trip on Friday; get more. Rough screens with real pharmacy wording. Afterwards: what would you do next, who else would you check with? A round this size catches common problems; fix, then run another with newer users."

## Checks

- The plan names the question first and the artifact second.
- Fidelity is stated per dimension, with a reason.
- No task uses the interface's own labels.
- A value or adoption question follows the tasks.
- No claim that a small round found most problems.
- Pilot results say who was present and what was faked.
