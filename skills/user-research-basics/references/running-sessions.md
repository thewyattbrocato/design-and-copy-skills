# Running a session

Load to write a moderator script for an interview or a task-based test, to fix sessions that went badly, or to review how a session was run. Not for the plan, screener or question list themselves (see plan, screener and asking). Starting values; change them when the person or the setting says otherwise. Add only the parts the request needs: a script gets the opening, the answer-back lines and the close; a "what went wrong" review gets the matching moves from the recovery list; do not hand a recovery table to someone who only asked for a guide.

## Before: set up the room

- Default: the person who built or sells the thing does not moderate its test. Change when nobody else is free: say so in the introduction, keep to the scripted neutral prompts and write no reactions into the notes.
- Introduction, a few minutes: what the session is for, that the design is on trial and the person is not, that there are no wrong answers, that they can stop or skip, what is recorded and who is watching. Spend longer on reassurance when the person is anxious or new to research.
- Test sessions: add one think-aloud warm-up on something unrelated to the product (a mug, a kitchen drawer: "tell me what you notice as you look at it"). Skip it for silent timed runs.
- Task cards: one scenario per card, in the person's terms, with none of the interface's labels. Ask the person to read the card aloud and say it back in their own words before starting; a wrong restatement means the card, not the person, needs fixing.
- Write a "when asked" line for each likely question, because improvising is where moderators explain: "What would you try?" "I'd like to see what you'd do; we'll talk after." Put these in the script when a script is requested.
- The team's questions about opinions, preferences or "would you use it" are not asked during tasks. They go after the last task or are dropped.

## During an interview: listening moves

- Prompts of a few words. The moderator's share of talk stays small; if the guide has long, multi-part questions, cut them to one clause.
- Acknowledge with a statement, not a question ("That sounds like a busy morning"), then wait. Repeat the person's own vivid phrase back and let them continue. Use their names for things.
- Do not agree, praise, argue or correct, including when the person is factually wrong. Ask how they came to understand it that way. A deliberately wrong summary offered to provoke a correction is a staged move; do not use it.
- Do not explain, defend or sell the product ("it already does that"). A feature request becomes a question about the last time they needed it and what they did instead. If they need product help, send it in a follow-up message after the session.
- Plan an open "anything else I should understand" near the middle of the session as well as at the end, and wait longer than feels comfortable before speaking again.
- Notice when someone is about to share something they may regret, such as a colleague's name or private health detail, and steer away before they finish it.

## During a test: the moderator's hands are tied

- A participant's question is not answered during a task. Turn it back ("What would you try?"), note the path and how long they spent, and explain at the end. Nothing in the script explains the design mid-task.
- No interviewing while a task runs. The only interjection is the think-aloud reminder, after a long silence.
- When someone asks "am I doing this right?", the answer is that the design is being tested, not them.
- Let the person finish, fail or give up; ask what they expected only afterwards.

## When a session goes sideways

Use only when asked for a script with fallbacks or a fix for sessions that went badly. One move per case:

- Anxious: an easy first task, an offer of a break.
- Silent: repeat the think-aloud reminder, lightly.
- Drifts off topic: steer back, but note whether the detour is the bigger problem.
- Vents: let them finish, say it will be passed on, return to the question.
- Wants help with the product: help once the session is over and say it is not part of the study; book a separate time if it is long.
- Several people join: thank them, ask who does what, accept less depth and note who said what.
- Short answers: ask for one specific recent occasion.
- Turns out not to fit: pay, end politely, set the data aside.

## After: close and write up

- Close with thanks and the payment as promised, whatever happened.
- Write down within the same day: raw notes first (what they did and said, the time and path of each question they asked), then a separate line for what you think it means. Code notes to the person by letter or number.
- Questions the participant asked are data: each one marks a place the design failed to answer for them. List them with the task.
- Send the product help or follow-up you promised.

## Pair

Invented: a library's room-booking page, a test task.

- Before: "Try booking the quiet room. Do you like how the calendar looks? Click the blue button there; that's where you pick the time."
- After: card read aloud and restated ("Book a room for a study group on Thursday afternoon"), then silence. If asked "is this the right button?": "What would you try?" Opinions about the calendar come after the last task.

## Checks

- No line in the script explains the design during a task.
- No opinion, preference or "would you use it" question sits inside a task.
- Each likely participant question has an answer-back line.
- The introduction says the design, not the person, is tested.
- The moderator is not the designer or seller, or says so and stays scripted.
- Recovery moves appear only if the request asked for them.
