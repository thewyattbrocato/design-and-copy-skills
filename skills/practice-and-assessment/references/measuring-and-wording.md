# Measuring and wording

Load for a survey, rating scale or self-rating item, pass criteria for observed practice, a pre/post check, or a write-up of what a score does and does not show. Starting values; change them when the situation says otherwise. A one-question exit check needs none of this beyond the first section.

## What a number can show

- Name the claim first, then the evidence that could support it. A quiz average shows performance on those items today. A satisfaction score shows how people felt. Neither shows that behavior changed or that anything will be remembered next month. Say which claim the data supports and which it does not, in one sentence each.
- Default: report perceived results and performed results side by side ("rated 4.5 of 5; 5 of 12 completed the follow-up task unaided"). A gap between them is the finding. Do not let the rating stand in for the task.
- Ratings given right after a success run higher than ratings given mid-task, and ratings said aloud to a trainer run higher than anonymous ones. Collect sensitive ratings anonymously and out of the instructor's sight.
- Pair each knowledge answer with "how sure are you" when the cost of a confident mistake is high. The cell to read is confident and wrong: those people will act without checking.
- A score only counts if the way of scoring was fixed before anyone answered. Decide it, write it down, then collect.

## Wording an item people answer

Each item can fail for four separate reasons: the person cannot read it, cannot recall the answer, cannot decide, or cannot fit the answer into the options. Write so only the intended reason can produce a wrong answer.

- One idea per item. "Clear and useful" is two questions; split it or pick one.
- Use the words learners use on the job, not the course's labels. Someone who never heard "triage matrix" cannot rate it.
- Name the thing rated and, if it matters, what it is compared with: "the refund practice session you finished today", not "the training".
- Default: ask about the last time or a recent window, never a forecast. "In the last two weeks, how many times did you check the label against the order?" beats "How likely are you to use this?" People invent answers to forecasts. Change to a short case ("what would you do here?") when behavior cannot yet be observed.
- Routine behavior fades in an hour. Ask a frequency only for events notable enough to remember, with a window that fits how often they happen; for the routine, take it from records or watch it.
- Ask unprompted recall before showing a list; a list steers what people remember.
- Check the wording by having three to five people from the group read each item aloud and say it back in their own words. A restatement that differs from your meaning is the defect.
- Do not presume the answer ("what did you find most useful" assumes something was). Include "none" and "don't remember" where they are real.

## Scales and scoring

- Default: five points, odd, only the ends labeled, the positive end always on the same side, one attitude per statement. No reverse-worded statements (people read them wrong), no "should" (it reads as both prediction and duty), no stacked grid of statements. Change the point count when a stakeholder or an established instrument needs another; the count matters little and consistency matters a lot.
- Prefer a direct question with tailored options when one exists: "Did you try the checklist? Yes, it helped / yes, it got in the way / no / don't remember" says more than "The checklist was useful: 1-5".
- To score several statements about one attitude, write about ten at most, worded differently but all in the same direction, and sum them. A score on one invented item is an opinion, not a measure. If a published short instrument fits, use it and score it as published.
- Report the spread beside any average: ten ones and ten fives average to a three that nobody chose. When views split, show both groups and do not call the middle a consensus.
- Avoid ranking items (harder than rating, and it hides how far apart items are), required questions (people answer anything to move on), and sliders (some find them harder). Keep an optional comment box on every scale.
- Order: the one question the decision needs first, grouped by topic, personal or background questions last, with no introduction page. Ask only what will change a decision; cut "nice to know".
- A recommend-to-a-friend score is a popularity number, not a measure of learning or behavior. If leaders ask for one, give it only with a follow-up question about what the person did differently, and say what it cannot show.

## Pass criteria for observed practice

Write these before the first learner starts.

- Define the task's end state and the success rule in terms a stranger could score. Name the help levels and count them separately: unaided, used the job aid, needed a hint, not finished. Report counts per level, not an average of levels.
- Fix the stop rule (a time cap, or the person gives up) and when the clock starts and stops.
- Time counts only for completed attempts, set against a target: a run by someone experienced, the team's own standard, or the last cohort. Report how many finished under the target. A fast failure must not look good, so show success next to time.
- Drop an attempt by a rule stated in advance (interrupted, impossibly fast), drop the whole attempt not just its time, and say how many you dropped.
- Count errors per chance to make one, define each type beforehand, and count one slip once.
- Learning is the same measure across three or four repeats spaced as real use is. Read where the curve flattens and the ratio of first to last (80 seconds falling to 60 is 1.3 times faster), not only the final pass. A first-session score for a skill used daily can mislead.
- Pre/post: use a matched but different set of items for the later check, since the same items measure memory of the items. Default to a delay of at least a day before the post-check; change to same-day only when you say it measures immediate performance.

## Small groups and missing answers

- Default: "8 of 10 finished unaided", not 80%. If a rate is demanded, give the count and a range that holds up at small n (8 of 10 is consistent with a true rate anywhere from about half to nearly everyone). Do not write "significant" or a margin of error for a cohort you did not sample.
- Average attainment hides individuals. Count the people who met every criterion together ("4 of 12 met all three") and list which criterion the rest missed.
- Two cohorts of ten with overlapping ranges cannot be ranked. Say what you can see, such as where each group went wrong, and what would settle it.
- When many skipped a follow-up, the answers describe the people who answered. State "28 of 40 answered", say what the skippers might share (the busiest, the least confident), and do not extend the result to everyone. Do not offer a prize draw as the fix; a short question and a named sender help more.
- Do not report decimals the data cannot support.

## Piloting and reading back

- Default: try the instrument with three to five people from the group, one at a time, thinking aloud, and change the wording; then run it on about a tenth of the cohort, at least 20 people, including a draft of the report. Change to the first step only for a short internal check.
- Reuse an instrument only if its scores held steady for people who did not change; otherwise treat a repeat score as a rough trend. Do not claim it "measures" a skill without observed performance alongside it.
- Report order: the finding as a sentence, the counts with their base, who is missing, and the one or two gaps that matter most, with what to try next. A table beside any chart.
- Keep quotes to illustrate a count, never to replace one. Never invent a baseline, a benchmark or a pass rate to make the number look meaningful: bracket it, such as [current first-attempt pass rate].
