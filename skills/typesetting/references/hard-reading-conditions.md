# Hard reading conditions

Load this when text is small, far away, glanced at, or aimed at children or tired or low-vision readers, or when asked which case, weight or spacing is "more legible". These are tendencies from small studies of sighted adults, not rules: check on the real text.

- **Letter spacing.** Default to the face's own spacing for body text; tightening letters alone tends to slow reading. If you tighten for a look, open the word gaps too.
- **Small paragraphs.** A multi-line paragraph at 12 to 13px (help text, footnotes) keeps about body leading, 1.5; the tighter caption ratio is for one-line captions.
- **Capitals.** Do not move small labels to caps for legibility: capitals read slower beyond a few words.
- **Emphasis.** Bold is for the word that must be found while scanning, not a more legible body weight. Do not set a whole paragraph in bold or italic to stand out.
- **Measure by task.** Lines near 100 characters tend to be skimmed faster but understood less. Keep 50 to 75 for text people must study; go wider only for scan-and-search text.
- **Distance and glance.** Size by how far the reader is, not by the CSS value. Raise size first, then use a medium or bold weight, regular or wide proportions and open spacing; avoid light or condensed cuts.
- **Confusable characters.** For codes and IDs, set the actual string at the smallest planned size and look for merged pairs (I l 1, O 0, e c, a o, h b, n u). If one merges, raise size or spacing before restyling; a new face belongs to typeface-selection.
- **Reader groups.** For children and older, dyslexic or low-vision readers, larger size, ragged-right and looser letter and word spacing have some support; make spacing adjustable where the product allows. Do not claim a "dyslexia-friendly" face helps; evidence for older and low-vision readers is thin, so recommend testing with them.
