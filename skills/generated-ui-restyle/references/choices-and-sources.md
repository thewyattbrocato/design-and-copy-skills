# Choices and sources

Load when writing the decision notes. The notes are a short list: a large choice, then the source it comes from. They are working material for the restyle and for the person reading it afterwards: put them in the answer in four lines or fewer for a small job, never as a section of the page.

A generated screen's choices come from habit. A restyle's choices come from somewhere that can be pointed at, so a reader can see why this gray, this face, this arrangement.

## What counts as a source

- **The page's own material.** Headings, column names, the objects the data is about, the verbs on the buttons, the people the copy addresses. This is the richest source and it is already in front of you.
- **The person's words.** Who uses the screen, how often, in what setting, what they must decide with it.
- **The world those point to.** The things, materials, places and tools around the product, inferred from the first two: a hospital ward at shift change, a freight yard, a court clerk's counter. Keep what can be seen or handled; skip abstractions such as "innovation".
- **A real brand asset.** A color, face or mark the company actually uses, which is a source that outranks the rest (see below).
- **A rule from another skill.** Hierarchy, spacing, type size and contrast choices cite the skill that owns them and need no story about the product.

When the page gives almost nothing, infer from the product name and the few nouns there are, write the guess as the first line of the notes, and proceed.

## Where each choice gets its reason

| Choice | Draw the reason from | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Cast of the grays | A hue the subject's materials really have, kept faint enough that working screens stay near neutral | color-palette |
| Accent | A color the subject has or a status the person must act on; a category cliche is allowed when chosen on purpose | color-palette |
| Face and weights | How the subject should sound read aloud: precise, bookish, sturdy, casual | typeface-selection |
| Shape language (radii, rules, borders) | The objects around the product and how long it is used each day | design-system-builder |
| Density | Who uses it, how often, and how much they scan or compare | interface-behavior |
| Sample data and imagery | The objects, units and quantities the page already names | components and details |
| Lead area of the layout | The one question the screen answers | layout-structure |

## A usable reason rules something out

Write each reason so it excludes at least one alternative: "grays carry a faint green-brown cast because the work is outdoors in daylight; no cool blue-gray". A reason that would justify the same choice for any competitor ("modern, clean, friendly") excludes nothing and is not a source. layout-structure asks the same of a layout concept: two concrete choices it leads to and one it forbids.

## A real brand

If the product has a mark, color or face that the company actually uses, the notes are built around it: the color becomes the accent, the face stays, the mark is left alone. The generator's default violet, a sparkle icon or a gradient square is not a brand unless the person says it is. When in doubt, keep it and ask in one line.

## Worked outline: street-tree work orders

An internal screen for a city's street-tree crew: a sidebar, a list of open work orders, a detail panel with the tree's record.

- Tells: gradient sidebar and gradient-filled page title (cause 1), emoji as the menu icons and in every status (4), a welcome line as the title and an "up 6%" figure with no period (5), rows of different heights (3). Most causes show, so the whole screen is in scope.
- Notes:
  - Grays with a faint green-brown cast, no cool blue-gray: the crew works outdoors among trees and soil (the detail panel's species and trunk fields).
  - Accent: a vest orange reserved for "hazard": the status column names hazards and the crew wears high-visibility vests.
  - One sturdy sans with tabular dates: leads read dates and tag numbers all day and compare them down a column.
  - One row height and urgency as the only colored element per row: the lead's question is "which trees first".
  - Sample data: numbered tree tags, species, cherry-picker and chipper jobs, district names.
- Passes: the title becomes the district and the week and the figure becomes "orders closed, 6% more than last week"; the tokens carry the notes; line icons from one set replace emoji; no hover lift on rows.
- Kept: every column, the detail panel, the menu order, the department's name.
- Rival check: on a parking-enforcement queue the species, tags and hazard orange would need replacing, so the screen is this crew's.

## Worked outline: a secondhand bookshop's landing page

A public page for a small bookshop that buys and sells used books by post: hero, how selling works, this week's arrivals, a newsletter sign-up.

- Tells: a stock centered hero with an announcement pill and a glossy abstract picture (causes 2 and 6), a hype headline (5), three equal icon cards (2), a pulsing sign-up button (7), an unsourced "loved by readers everywhere" line (5).
- Notes:
  - Light paper tint on the ground, no cool white: short-visit marketing page, and the shop sells paper and cloth.
  - Face with a bookish temperament for headings, a plain sans for the rest: the copy talks about titles and editions.
  - Hero shows this week's arrivals drawn in code from titles the page already lists, asymmetric around the headline: the one question is "what can I sell or buy, and how".
  - The three icon cards become one numbered path: the selling steps are a sequence.
  - Prices set as pencil-style figures: the copy mentions pencilled prices on the flyleaf.
- Passes: the headline says what you can sell or buy and how; the unsourced line is kept as the person's content, set plainly, and flagged for a source; the button stays still.
- Kept: every section in its order, the arrivals list, the sign-up field.
- Rival check: a chain bookstore's page would not have the arrivals drawn from titles, the post-in selling path or the pencilled prices.
