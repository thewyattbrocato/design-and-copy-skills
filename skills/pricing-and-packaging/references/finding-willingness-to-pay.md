# Finding what buyers will pay

Load when the user has no evidence of what buyers will pay, wants to run research, or has answers to read. Every example is invented.

## Order of evidence

Use the cheapest source that answers the question, and move down only when the stakes need it.

| Source | What it gives | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| What buyers pay now for the alternative, in money and in hours | A ceiling and a reference | Says nothing about your extra value |
| Lost and won deals, quotes accepted or refused | Real reactions to real prices | Small numbers, and biased toward the sales you chased |
| Conversations with people in the target job | Ranges and the reasons behind them | Politeness inflates "yes" |
| Choosing between real packages at set prices | Which features carry the price | Needs enough respondents to be stable |
| Money: a paid pilot, a deposit, a preorder, a real quote | The strongest signal | Slow, and small samples |
| A live price change in a group that cannot see the other price | Behavior | Public tests are visible and can upset buyers |

Asking gives ranges and reasons; behavior sets the final number. A friendly "I would pay that" from someone who is not spending is a compliment, not data.

## A conversation that moves from value to price

1. What do you do today, and what does it cost you in hours or money?
2. Which of these capabilities matter most, and which would you never use?
3. What price would feel fair, what would feel expensive, and at what price would you not consider it?
4. Would you buy at [a named price]? (A named price, not "would you be interested".)
5. After each answer: why?

Put value before price, since a price means little until the buyer has said what the thing is worth. Say that price is on the agenda; do not present a pricing talk as something else. Spend a good share of the questions on "why".

## Reading the answers

- Look at the spread. Nine answers that cluster near 12 and near 40 average 24, a price almost nobody gave. Two clusters mean two offers, or build for the higher group.
- Sort answers by who gave them (job, size of need), not by how friendly they were.
- A feature almost nobody pays for, and some dislike, is a candidate to cut or sell apart. The one the team loves is often not the one buyers rank first.
- Sample size is the limit. With under about fifty buyers, treat results as ranges and reasons, not percentages; never quote a survey statistic from a handful of calls.

## Write the walk-away rule first

Before research, write the price and volume below which the idea is not worth running. A team that has already built the thing will read a weak result as "maybe". A written line makes a no acceptable.

## Worked example

A mobile knife-sharpening service asks nine customers. Six home cooks say 10 to 15 per knife is fair and 25 is too much; three restaurant kitchens say 35 to 45 is fair, "if you come on a schedule". The average, about 24, fits neither group. The decision: a per-knife price near the cooks' range for walk-up and drop-off, and a weekly route price for kitchens, each tied to the visit that serves it. The next step is a paid trial month with two kitchens to firm up the second number.

## When the user has no research at all

Deliver a provisional range anyway: the alternative's cost, an assumed value gap, and the unit cost as the floor, all marked assumed. Then give the cheapest test that would firm it up this week (five paid pilots, a deposit page, three quotes sent at different prices to different buyers who will not compare them). Do not invent a survey result to fill the hole.
