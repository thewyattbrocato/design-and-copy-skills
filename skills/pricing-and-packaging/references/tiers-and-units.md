# Tiers, units and free offers

Load for plan design, where a feature goes, bundles, the charging unit, a free tier, or a plan mix that looks wrong. Examples are invented.

## From buyers to plans

1. **Group buyers by what they value and will pay**, not by age, country or company size. Two buyers who look alike on paper can want opposite things. Start with three or four groups; each extra one adds product, sales and support cost. You can serve fewer than all of them.
2. **Test the groups.** If someone who sells to them can sort real accounts into the groups, and each group has something it wants that the others do not, the groups are usable. If you cannot describe how to recognize a group, you cannot target it.
3. **Place each feature by who values it.** Three bins: most buyers would pay for it, some would, almost none would and some dislike it. The first bin is standard or goes in the lowest tier that serves the group that needs it. The second is what separates tiers or sells as an add-on. The third gets cut or sold apart, because forcing it into a price makes some buyers walk away.
4. **Fence the plans.** The cheaper plan must truly lack what the next one adds. Ask: could someone in the top group safely pick the cheap plan? If yes, the fence is missing and the top plan is carrying the cost. Removing a feature from the cheap plan takes nerve; the alternative is giving the whole ladder away.
5. **Nest the ladder.** Each higher plan includes everything below it plus something. When plans differ on unrelated features in different directions, buyers cannot compare them and tend to take the cheapest, the dearest, or leave. Options that are not part of the ladder go in a short add-on list with their own prices.
6. **Label every plan with its group** ("for one person who sends invoices", "for a studio with several staff"), then hand names and blurbs to offers-and-value-propositions.

## How many tiers

| Situation | Default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers compare plans side by side | Three | A real middle, a floor and a ceiling |
| Only two groups differ in value | Two | A third would be a plan nobody needs |
| A group buys through a different route (sales call, procurement) | Add a fourth, "talk to us" tier, with what it depends on | The price truly varies |
| No group differs in value or cost to serve | One price | Extra tiers add confusion |
| Many groups, many options | Fewer, clearer plans plus an add-on list | Choice overload |

Keep a bundle small. When buyers must hold more than a handful of benefits or products in mind at once, they stop weighing them.

## Reading the mix after launch

- If more than about half of buyers take the entry plan, check whether it gives away too much. Options: remove a feature, move a limit, or add a tier above that holds what that group would pay for.
- If almost nobody takes the top plan, it adds nothing a group wants, or the middle gives too much.
- If one plan dominates, check whether it is the only sensible one.
Do not quote a target split as a rule; it depends on the segments.

## Bundles

Bundle when buyers value different halves. Example, a ski-rental shop: skier A values skis at 40 and a helmet at 5; skier B values skis at 12 and a helmet at 30. Priced apart, the best prices are 40 for skis and 30 for helmets, selling one of each: 70 in total. A bundle at 42 sells to both: 84. Offer the parts as well as the bundle, so people who want one half are not forced into both, and keep the bundle below the sum of the parts. Do not bundle things people already buy together. Show a "saving" only if the parts are sold separately at those prices.

## What the price is tied to

Tests for a candidate unit; ask all five:
- Does it rise with the value the buyer feels?
- Can the buyer predict their bill?
- Can you measure it without argument?
- Does it stay meaningful as customers or hardware grow? (A price per gigabyte or per processor falls in value as they get cheaper.)
- Can you say it in one line?

| Unit | Fits when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Per seat | Value grows with the number of people using it | One person does the work and many only view: heavy users undercharged, buyers guessing headcount |
| Per outcome or output (per invoice chased, per listing, per report) | The buyer thinks in those terms | Definition disputes: say what counts, with an example |
| Per usage | Cost to you rises with use | Bills buyers cannot forecast; add bands, a floor or a cap |
| Per audience or size band | Value follows reach | Gaming at the band edge |
| Flat | Use is similar across buyers | Light users overpay, heavy users underpay |
| One price plus a yearly fee for updates | Buyers want to own it | You must keep shipping |

Default to the unit customers already expect in the category unless a different one is clearly better and you can explain it in a sentence. A podcast host charging per episode while its customers' value follows monthly listeners could move to listener bands with a soft limit, and show a typical bill.

## Free tiers

A free plan is a cost that must be earned back. Work it through:
- Cost per free user per month, including support, hosting and any usage that costs you money.
- Plausible conversion to paid. It is usually low; use the user's own estimate or label yours assumed.
- Paid revenue from those converts against the free users' cost, per month.
If the converts do not cover it, the free tier is a marketing budget; decide that on purpose, with a limit.

Limits should stop the free plan covering what paid buyers value (volume, number of projects, team size) and should not remove the one thing that makes the product worth trying. A free plan that makes the entry paid plan pointless drains it. Free also fits when network effects dominate, since a thin network has no value, and when a pilot is the only way to prove the product.

## Thresholds

- Business buyers: find the amount a person can spend on a card without asking, the amount a manager signs, and the amount that needs procurement. Ask the user or the buyer; do not state amounts as general facts. Pricing a team plan just under a sign-off line helps only if it still earns, and the next step up is easier once a buyer has crossed one.
- Consumers: find the price points in the category where people stop, and whether endings like .99 matter there. Business buyers often prefer whole numbers.
- Test a threshold before building the page around it.
