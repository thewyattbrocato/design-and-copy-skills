# Level, discounts and changes

Load for a profit table, back-solving volume, discounts and annual plans, weak sales, raising prices, or reviewing a price page. Examples are invented.

## Setting the level

1. **Pick one goal** and name the trade-off: profit, share, cash early, or lifetime value. You cannot maximize all of them. A one-line strategy: the aim, the period, how it sits against the alternative, and "because".
2. **Cost floor.** Unit cost includes support, payment fees, any channel or store share, delivery and the cost of free users. It excludes what it cost to build; that is spent whatever you charge. If buyers' value is below the cost to sell, change the cost or the mechanism (cheaper channel, pay over time), not only the number.
3. **Profit at several prices**, not only revenue. Example, a translation agency, unit cost 0.06 per word (translator, review, payment fees):

| Rate per word | Words a month | Revenue | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.09 | 640,000 | 57,600 | 19,200 |
| 0.11 | 480,000 | 52,800 | 24,000 |
| 0.13 | 320,000 | 41,600 | 22,400 |

Revenue peaks at 0.09; profit peaks at 0.11. Read it as a range, 0.11 to 0.13, and note that thin margins make a wrong price cost more. Use the user's volumes; if none, mark volumes assumed.
4. **Back-solve volume.** A printable planner sells for 12.00 through a marketplace that keeps a fifth, so 9.60 arrives; payment and file costs take 0.60, leaving 9.00. A 22,500 profit goal needs 2,500 sales. Ask whether the audience and a realistic conversion get there; if not, change the price, the cost or the plan.
5. **Launch rules** to write before the first quote: floor price, maximum discount, who approves exceptions, price endings, when and how prices rise, and what you watch.
6. **Low or high at launch.** Default to the value-based price; a launch price set low is hard to raise by much, and a very low price can read as low quality. Change to a low entry price only with a stated reason (network effects, learning) and a plan to raise. Small, steady rises on an improving product are usually tolerated, so the goal and the market decide, not a rule.

## Discounts and sales

- Default: do not discount for everyone. Give a discount for something: a longer term, volume, prepayment, a case study or reference. Set the floor and the approver first. If almost every quote needs an exception, the list price is wrong or the approver is a rubber stamp.
- A sale works when it covers a few items or days and the earlier price was real; a sale on everything means nothing. Do not quote lift figures from other firms; test your own.
- Define the unit tightly in volume and site deals ("one site" means what), since one large buyer can lock in a low price for large future use.
- Show the saving in money, not a percentage alone, and hand the wording to offers-and-value-propositions.

## Annual plans

Work out who takes the annual offer before approving it. A club charges 30 a month; annual is 9 months for 12 (270).
- A member who would have left after 5 months pays 270 instead of 150: gain of 120, and the cash comes early.
- A member who would have stayed the full year pays 270 instead of 360: a loss of 90, and you also lose the monthly chance to raise or lose them.
The offer pays when enough takers are people who would have left early; people who expect to stay choose it because it is cheaper, so assume some self-selection and do not apply the average stay to the whole group. Check the plan's churn after month 12 before calling it a win. Keep monthly available; hand wording to offers-and-value-propositions.

## When sales are weak

Do not cut the price as a reflex: an early cut says the offer is worth less than claimed, gives away margin you rarely win back and invites a spiral. First find which cause fits:
1. Buyers do not understand the value (watch where visitors or trials drop).
2. A rival undercut (look at lost-deal reasons).
3. A new alternative arrived.
4. Something else: reach, timing, onboarding, trust.
Take at least three non-price actions first (fix onboarding, show proof in the buyer's numbers, change who you talk to). If a cut remains, make it targeted and time-limited, and trade it for something (a term, a reference). Hold the price long enough to learn, say a set window decided in advance. Example: a newsletter tool launched at 18 sees trials that never send a first issue: fix that step, offer a three-month rate only to publishers who give a case study, and hold 18 for the window.

## Raising prices

- **Raise or add a tier?** Raise when every buyer gets more than they pay for, losses seldom cite price, demand outruns capacity or the product has improved. Add a tier when only some buyers value the new thing, so a rise would hit people who do not.
- **Steps.** Prefer a modest step with a visible improvement, repeated if signals stay healthy, over one large jump.
- **Existing customers.** Decide on purpose and say so. Default: keep them at their price for a stated period or until renewal, then move them with notice of at least one billing cycle, longer for annual customers. Change to moving everyone at once when the old price loses money or contract terms allow. Never leave them to find out on the invoice.
- **Watch** conversion, cancellations, downgrades and support tickets over a window set in advance, and name what would reverse the change.
- A cost-driven rise needs the cost story told; wording goes to offers-and-value-propositions and fair-persuasion.

## Reviewing a price page

Check in this order and quote the line at fault:
1. Is the unit stated, with a counted example?
2. Does each plan map to a group, with a fence from the plan below?
3. Does the cheapest plan give away what the top group pays for?
4. Is any plan one nobody would sensibly buy, or differing only by quantity?
5. Does the annual price make sense against how long monthly buyers stay?
6. Do discounts have a reason, and is any "was" price a price once charged?
7. Would a buyer who saw another buyer's price think the gap fair (region, format, channel)?
8. Does the price signal match what is delivered?
If it all holds, say so and stop.
