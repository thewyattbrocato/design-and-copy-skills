# Price moves: does a cut, match or rise pay?

Load when someone proposes or questions a cut, discount, promotion, price match, or a rise because costs went up. Examples are invented. The arithmetic only uses figures the user gave; mark any other figure "assumed".

## Margin first

Margin per sale is the price minus the costs each extra sale carries (materials, fees, delivery, support, any channel share). Count only costs this decision changes: those per-sale costs, plus any one-off cost the move itself creates (re-issuing a price list, adding capacity). Money already spent and overhead that stays the same whatever you charge stay out; they decide whether the business survives, not which of two prices earns more.

## A cut or promotion

Default: before backing it, state the extra sales it needs to earn the same profit: the cut divided by the margin left after the cut. A mug sells at 40 and carries 22 of cost, so margin is 18. A 10% cut is 4, leaving 14; sales must rise by 4 / 14, about 29%, just to stand still. Give units as well as the percentage, then say whether the expected response clears it.
- A cut as large as the margin cannot pay back at any volume. Say so in one line.
- If costs change with the price move, use the change in margin, not the change in price.
- Compare with what would sell if nothing changed, not with today, when demand is growing or shrinking.
- "Volume stays the same" is a fair working assumption only for small moves; for a large one, estimate the response or test it on a slice first.

Change to the profit table in level-and-changes.md when the user supplies volumes at several prices.

## A rise

The same check reversed: a rise can lose sales equal to the rise divided by (margin plus rise) before profit falls. Raising the mug by 5 on a margin of 18 can lose about a fifth of sales (5 in 23) and break even. Ask whether buyers would really leave in that number.

## Which cost counts

When capacity is full, a sale's cost includes the sale it pushes out. When capacity sits idle (a half-empty weekday class, an empty room), a lower price for a fenced group can add profit even below the fully loaded cost per unit; name which case holds, and fence the lower price so full-price buyers cannot take it.

## A rival cuts

Default: do not match across the board. Matching pays only if the share of sales you would lose by holding exceeds the rival's cut divided by your current margin (a 3 cut against a margin of 18: one sale in six). Then ask which customers would really leave, since only part of any loss is preventable; whether the rival can and will cut again; and whether something narrower is cheaper: an offer to the price-sensitive group only, a discount limited to the business at risk, or a non-price answer (service, terms, contract length). Change to matching when the share at risk is above the threshold and the rival cannot go lower. A low price is a lasting position only with lower costs, a niche rivals ignore, or margin earned on something sold alongside; otherwise expect the cut to be copied and both sides to earn less.

## A cost-driven rise

What the cost justifies is its change times its share of the price: a cost that is a quarter of the price and rises by a fifth justifies a rise of a twentieth. That is the ceiling for "because our costs rose". Whether to pass on all of it depends on rivals: if every rival faces the same cost, full pass-through is likelier to hold; if only you do, plan to absorb part. Do not raise every item by its own cost increase; put the rises where buyers value the product most and alternatives are weakest. Give a cost reason only when the user supplied the cost figures.

## Restraint

If the request is only for copy or a banner and the move is small next to the margin, add nothing. A cut at or above the margin always earns one line. One short check, not a section.
