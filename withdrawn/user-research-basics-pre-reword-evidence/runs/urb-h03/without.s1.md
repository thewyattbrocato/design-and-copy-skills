Your guide has a good skeleton, but several questions will give you unreliable answers. Here's what I'd change.

## Main problems

**Q2: "Don't you find it stressful keeping track of irregular income?"**
This is a leading question. It tells people the "right" answer, and most will agree to be polite. Ask about what they actually do: *"Walk me through the last time you had to figure out how much money you could spend in a month."* Stress, workarounds, and tools will come up if they're real.

**Q3: "Would you use an app that automatically sets aside money for taxes?"**
Hypothetical questions about future behavior get inflated yeses. It also describes your solution before you understand the problem. Ask about past behavior: *"How do you handle taxes today? Tell me about the last quarterly payment."* Follow up with: *"Has anything gone wrong there? What did you do about it?"*

**Q4: "How much would you pay… $5, $10, $15?"**
Stated willingness to pay is weak data, and the options anchor people at $5–$15. Better signals are what they pay for now (QuickBooks, spreadsheets, an accountant, other apps) and whether they've ever switched tools and why. If you do want price reactions, save them for the end, after showing a concept, and use open-ended phrasing or a Van Westendorp-style set of questions.

**Q5: "What features would you want in the perfect budgeting app?"**
People are poor at designing solutions, and you'll get a wish list that doesn't reflect what they'd use. Replace it with: *"What's the most frustrating part of how you manage money now?"* and *"Have you tried other tools? What made you stop?"*

**Q6: "Our app makes invoicing really easy. How do you feel about that?"**
This is a pitch, not a question, and it invites compliments. Ask: *"How do you invoice clients today? What's annoying about it? What happens when someone pays late?"*

## Smaller fixes

- **Q1:** Add a consent and framing script. Say that there are no wrong answers, that you're not selling anything, and that you're testing ideas, not them. Ask permission to record. Also, 45 minutes is long. Plan for 30–40 minutes of content so you don't run over.
- **Q7:** Good, but make it more specific: *"Is there something I should have asked but didn't?"*
- **Missing: screening and context.** Confirm they're really a freelancer: type of work, how long, income range and variability, number of clients. Without that you can't segment your findings.
- **Missing: warm-up.** Start with *"Tell me about your work and how you got into freelancing."* It's easy to answer and gives you context.
- **Missing: follow-up probes.** Prepare a few and use them constantly: *"Can you tell me more about that?"*, *"What happened next?"*, *"Why was that?"*, and just silence.

## Suggested structure

1. **Intro and consent (3 min):** purpose, recording, no right or wrong answers.
2. **Background (5 min):** their work, clients, how income arrives.
3. **Current money management (10–12 min):** the last time they budgeted, tools used, what's painful, how they handle surprises like a slow month.
4. **Taxes (7–8 min):** how they set money aside, what they've gotten wrong, who helps.
5. **Invoicing and getting paid (5–7 min):** process, late payments, tools.
6. **Optional concept reaction (5 min):** only now show or describe Ledgerleaf and note where they react most. Ask what would stop them from using it.
7. **Wrap-up (2 min):** *"What should I have asked?"* and ask whether they'd be open to a follow-up.

## The principle behind all of this

Ask about **specific past events**, not opinions or hypotheticals. "Tell me about the last time…" gets you facts, and "Would you…?" gets you politeness.
