# Ledgerleaf interview guide (revised)

**Goal:** describe how freelancers handle irregular income and taxes today, and where it breaks down. You'll use this to decide whether to build tax set-aside and invoicing, and which one first.

**1. Open and consent (3 min)**
- Thanks for joining. This will take about 45 minutes. I'm learning about how freelancers manage money, and there are no right or wrong answers.
- Ask for permission to record. Say who else is listening, if anyone. Say they can skip any question or stop at any time.
- Don't name Ledgerleaf or pitch it. Say only that you're researching money management.

**2. Context (5 min)**
- Tell me about your work. What do you do, and how long have you been freelancing?
- Walk me through how money came in over the last few months.

**3. Last-time stories (20 min)**
- Tell me about the last time you had to figure out how much money you could spend. What did you do, step by step?
- Tell me about the last time money was tighter than you expected. What happened? What did you do next?
- Tell me about the last time you dealt with taxes. When did you start, what did you use, and how did you decide how much to pay?
- How did you handle money for that tax payment in the months before? *(Probe: did they set money aside, where, and how did they pick the amount?)*
- Tell me about the last time you sent an invoice. Walk me through it from start to finish. *(Probe: what tools were involved, and how did you know when you were paid?)*
- Tell me about a time a client paid late. What did you do?

**4. Current tools (8 min)**
- Show me, or describe, what you use now to track money. How did you end up with that setup?
- What have you tried and stopped using? What happened?
- What do you do by hand that you wish you didn't have to? Give me the most recent example.

**5. Close (5 min)**
- Is there anything about how you manage money that I didn't ask about?
- Who else should I talk to?
- Thank them and explain the incentive.

## What I changed and why

- **Q2 ("Don't you find it stressful…"):** This leads. It supplies the answer and invites a yes. Ask for the last time money was uncertain and let them say how it felt.
- **Q3 ("Would you use an app that…"):** This asks for a prediction. People say yes to things they'll never use. Ask how they handle taxes now. Someone who already sets money aside, or who got burned at tax time, is a far stronger signal than a "yes."
- **Q4 (price: $5, $10, $15):** Stated willingness to pay is unreliable, and your options anchor the answer. Ask what they pay for now and what they've stopped paying for. Pricing needs a different method, such as a pricing test with real offers.
- **Q5 ("perfect budgeting app"):** This asks what they want, and you'd get a wishlist. The tool questions in section 4 surface the same needs from real behavior.
- **Q6 ("Our app makes invoicing really easy…"):** This is a pitch plus a leading question, and it reveals the sponsor. The invoice story tells you whether invoicing is a real pain.
- **Q1 and Q7:** Both work. I turned Q1 into a proper opening with consent, and I kept Q7 and added a referral ask.

## Notes

- **Assumption:** About 45 minutes, one participant at a time, with no existing interview data.
- **For you to do:** Recruit people who have freelanced for pay recently, and don't use friends or colleagues as the whole sample. Get recording consent. If you wrote the guide, consider having someone else facilitate, or at least pilot it with one person tonight. Plan roughly 5–8 interviews per type of freelancer, and report results as "3 of 7," not percentages.
