# Plan: two weeks, no budget, about 6 designer-hours

**Reframe "validate" with your VP first.** Customer conversations can't tell you whether people will pay for this. People over-claim in interviews. They can tell you whether the problem exists, how people handle it today, and whether the checkout moment is the right place. A fake-door test is the closest thing to a purchase signal you can get cheaply. Agree on what the planning meeting will decide, for example "invest in a partner and build, or park it." Also agree on what result would change that decision.

## Week 1: mine what you already have (you, ~1 day)

Check these before talking to anyone:
- **Support tickets and sitter incident reports.** Look for vet bills, injuries, illness during a stay, and disputes over who pays. Count them as n of N, and note that tickets only come from people who write in.
- **Any existing guarantee or claims history.** If Kestrel already covers anything, how often is it used and what does it cost?
- **Checkout analytics.** Look at average booking value, drop-off at the payment step, and repeat-booking rate. A $15 add-on on a $40 booking is a different proposition from one on a $400 booking.
- **Reviews and sitter chat.** Search for "emergency," "vet," and "sick."

The result is a short list of real incidents and rough frequency. If incidents are rare, that is a finding in itself.

## Weeks 1–2: 5–6 customer conversations, 3 sitter conversations

You run these, 30 minutes each. Recruit by emailing recent bookers, within the last 60 days, ideally a mix of one-time and repeat. Offer a booking credit if finance allows. If not, ask for a favor and say the call is short. For sitters, pick people who have handled a sick or injured dog.

**Questions** ask about the past, not the future:
- "Tell me about the last time your dog needed unexpected vet care. What happened, and what did you do?"
- "How did you pay for it? What did you wish had been different?"
- "Last time you booked a sitter, what were you worried about? What did you do about it?"
- "Do you have insurance for your dog now? How did you pick it?" (Ask this only after the stories.)

Don't ask "Would you buy insurance at checkout?" or "Would you like this?" Don't show the concept until the end. Then watch their reaction, and ask what they'd need to know before trusting it.

Sitters can tell you what goes wrong during stays and whether they'd be asked to handle claims.

**Designer hours:** have them take notes on 3–4 calls. They shouldn't facilitate, since they'd be tempted to sell the idea. Spend the remaining ~2 hours on the next item.

## Week 2: a fake-door test (only if traffic allows)

Have the designer mock an add-on line at checkout with a price range and a "Learn more" link. Clicking leads to "Not available yet, tell us what you'd want covered." Use one metric, set in advance: click-through among people who reach the payment step. Run it for at least one full week. A few days of data or a few hundred visitors is a lead, not a result. Check with engineering that it doesn't hurt conversion, and consider showing it to a small share of traffic. Skip this if checkout volume is too low to read.

## Check before the meeting
Insurance is regulated. You need a licensed insurer or MGA partner, and legal or compliance may constrain what you can show or say. Ask legal one question this week: can we show a fake-door for an insurance product? Even a rough partner quote would help the VP's decision more than interviews.

## What you can bring to the meeting
- Incident evidence from existing data, with counts and their bias.
- "X of 6 customers and Y of 3 sitters described [pattern]," in quotes you actually heard.
- Fake-door click-through, labeled as a lead.
- What's still unknown: price tolerance, partner economics, and claims handling. State plainly that none of this proves people will pay.

**Assumptions:** you have some checkout traffic and a support inbox you can search. If either is missing, drop that step and lean on the conversations.
