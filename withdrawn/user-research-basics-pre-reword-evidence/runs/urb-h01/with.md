**Assumption:** you have a few thousand past bookings, a support inbox, and checkout analytics, and no existing research on insurance.

## Reframe "validate" first
Customer conversations can't tell you whether an add-on will sell, and "would you buy this?" gets you polite yeses. In two weeks with no budget, you can answer three narrower questions:

1. **Is there a real problem?** Do bookers or sitters have moments where a dog is hurt, sick, or lost, or where property is damaged, and nobody knows who pays?
2. **Is insurance the right shape for it?** Or would a free guarantee, a sitter-side policy, or clearer terms work better?
3. **Is there a cheap behavioral signal?** Do people click on it at checkout?

Tell your VP now that you'll bring back evidence on the problem and an early demand signal, not a green light. Ask what result would make them drop the idea. Agree on that before you start, so the findings can change the decision.

## Week 1: use what you already have (about 3 days of your time)
- **Support tickets and chat logs.** Search for injury, vet, emergency, lost, damage, refund, and "who pays." Count them as n of N, and note that only people who write in show up there.
- **Incident and refund data.** How often do incidents happen per booking? What did they cost you or the sitter?
- **Current policy.** Does Kestrel already offer any guarantee or sitter coverage? Insurance that duplicates it is a hard sell.
- **Checkout analytics.** Where do people drop off now? Are they price-sensitive at that step? An add-on there can hurt conversion.
- **Competitor pricing.** Look at what Rover and Wag do for coverage.
- **Feasibility.** Selling insurance is regulated. Find out who the underwriter or partner would be, and whether you need a license. This is probably the biggest risk, and customers can't answer it. Ask legal or finance this week.

## Weeks 1–2: 5–8 short conversations (20–30 minutes)
Recruit people who booked in the last 60 days. Use a short email to recent bookers, and include a few sitters. Offer a Kestrel credit if finance allows it. Split them between people who've had a scare or incident and people who haven't.

Ask about the **last time**, not the future:
- "Tell me about the last time you left your dog with a sitter. What was on your mind beforehand?"
- "Has anything gone wrong, or nearly gone wrong? What happened next? Who paid?"
- "Do you have pet insurance or a vet-bill plan now? How did you pick it?"
- "What did you do at checkout? Was anything unclear or worrying?"

Don't mention the add-on until the very end. If you show it at all, ask how they'd handle the situation today, not whether they'd buy it. Get consent to take notes or record.

The designer should take notes or run the sessions, using their 3 hours a week. Your VP's idea is yours to carry, so someone else should do the asking.

## Week 2: one cheap behavioral test (if traffic allows)
Ask the designer to mock up an honest "Add pet protection — coming soon, get notified" line at checkout, or a post-booking email. Measure clicks or waitlist signups against booking volume. Pick the metric and the run length (whole weeks) before you start, and don't peek. At low traffic this is only a lead. It must not take money or promise coverage that doesn't exist.

## What to bring to the planning meeting
A one-page readout with:
- **What we know:** incident frequency, current gaps, and the regulatory path, each with its source.
- **What we heard:** "3 of 7 recent bookers described X." No percentages from small groups.
- **Signal strength:** a clear label of which findings are solid and which are leads.
- **Still unknown:** price tolerance and the effect on conversion.
- **Recommendation:** build, run a bigger test, or drop it. Say which result would have changed your mind.

## What you'll need to do yourself
- Send the recruiting emails and get consent.
- Run or schedule the sessions.
- Get answers from legal and finance on licensing and the partner.

If the incident data and tickets show the problem is rare, you can stop after week 1. That's a valid result, and it costs you far less than conducting interviews.
