Your coworker is only partly right. The zero-baseline rule applies to bar charts, where bar length encodes the value, so a truncated axis makes differences look like ratios. A line chart encodes value by position, not length, so a non-zero baseline is standard practice. Starting the axis at 20 is reasonable for temperatures between 21.4 and 24.9.

The real risk with your chart is different: a 6-degree axis makes a 3.5-degree swing look dramatic. If the point is that the lake stayed in a fairly narrow band, the chart will overstate the variation. If the point is day-to-day movement, the current scale is fine.

You don't need to redo it from scratch. Some quick fixes:

- **Label the axis clearly** with units, such as "Water temperature (°C)," and make sure the 20 and 26 ticks are visible.
- **Add a caption or annotation** that gives the range, such as "Daily range 21.4–24.9 °C," so readers see the magnitude directly.
- **Consider 18–28 or 20–28** if you want the line to look calmer and to leave room for context. Wider scales are a legitimate choice when you want to show stability.
- **Use a reference line** if there's a meaningful threshold, such as a swimming advisory or a seasonal norm.

If the chart is going to a general audience, I'd add the range annotation and keep 20–26. If your coworker's concern is that readers will read the slope as a dramatic change, widening the axis slightly is the more direct answer to that concern.
