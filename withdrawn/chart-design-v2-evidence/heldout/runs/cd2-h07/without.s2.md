I'd use a 4×3 grid of small multiples, one panel per store. Don't put 12 lines on one chart.

**Why not one chart:** a 20× range (400 to 9,000) flattens the small stores into the floor. Twelve overlapping lines would also be unreadable.

**Layout**
- **Grid:** one small line chart per store, with the same x-axis (weeks 1–52) in every panel. Order panels from largest to smallest by total visitors, so the grid reads as a ranking.
- **Y-axis:** give each panel its own scale, so every store's shape is visible. Put the peak value in each panel's corner (e.g. "peak 9,100") so the size differences aren't hidden. Say "each chart has its own scale" in a small footnote. If size comparison matters more than shape, use a shared log scale instead, but that's harder for a general audience to read.
- **Color:** draw the 10 typical stores in one neutral gray or blue. Give the two exceptions a single accent color each, so they stand out immediately.
- **Annotations:**
  - Mark the shared Christmas peak with a light vertical band around weeks 50–51 in every panel, so the pattern is obvious.
  - Airport: label its July peak ("Summer peak, travel season" or whatever the real reason is).
  - Harbor Mall: add a marker at week 30 with "Renovation begins".
- **Title:** state the takeaway, not the topic. For example: "10 of 12 stores peak the week before Christmas; Airport peaks in July and Harbor Mall dropped after its week-30 renovation."

**Things to check**
- If Harbor Mall is closed after week 30 rather than just reduced, show that period as a dashed or shaded segment so it isn't read as a collapse in demand.
- If you'd rather show the shared Christmas pattern more directly, you can plot each store as a percentage of its own average. All 12 panels can then share one y-axis, and the Airport's different shape stands out even more. The cost is that absolute size disappears, so you'd need the peak labels.
