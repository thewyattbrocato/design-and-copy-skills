**Title: Everyone Wants to Make a Mug First. Here's What a Year of Teaching Beginners Taught Me.**

Last spring we started Saturday classes at Third Fire for adult beginners, with eight people per group. I've been a potter for a long time, but I'd never taught people who had never touched clay. A year in, I've changed my mind about a lot of what I assumed.

**Almost everyone wants to make a mug first**

On the first Saturday I ask everyone what they'd like to make. Almost every time, the answer is a mug. It makes sense. A mug is something you use every day, and it's the object people picture when they imagine owning handmade pottery.

It's also the hardest thing you can ask a beginner to make.

**Why the mug is so hard**

A mug looks simple, but it asks for a lot of skills at once:

- **Even walls.** You need walls that are thin enough to be comfortable and thick enough to survive firing. A beginner's walls tend to be thick at the bottom and thin at the lip, or the reverse.
- **A clean shape.** The cylinder has to stay upright and true while you pull it up. Clay that's slightly off-center at the start gets more off-center as the pot gets taller.
- **A handle.** It has to be pulled or shaped, then attached at the right moment, when the clay is firm enough to hold and soft enough to bond. Timing matters more than strength.
- **A comfortable fit.** The handle has to suit a hand, and the mug has to balance when it's full.

A bowl forgives a lot of mistakes. A mug shows every one of them. Each step depends on the one before it. A wobble in the first ten minutes can lead to a handle that falls off a week later.

**What I'd tell a beginner now**

Telling someone "the mug is hard" can sound like discouragement. What I've learned is that it works better to be honest about it, and to treat the mug as a goal to work toward instead of a first assignment.

Early on, people who make a small cylinder, then a bowl, then a slightly taller cylinder end up with a better mug than people who start on one right away. They also have a better time getting there. When someone's first object is a lopsided mug, they usually decide they're bad at pottery. When their first object is a lopsided bowl, they decide they're a beginner. The second is much more accurate, and it keeps them coming back.

**Other things I got wrong**

*I over-explained.* In the first few classes I gave too much information at the wheel. Beginners can only take in one or two corrections at a time, and the rest is noise. Now I pick the one thing that matters most for each person and leave the others for next time.

*I underestimated how emotional clay is.* People get attached to their first pieces. Then the pieces dry, shrink, crack, or come out of the kiln looking different from what they pictured. I've learned to explain early that the process is slow and that not everything survives it. That's part of the craft, and it isn't a sign that someone did something wrong.

*I thought the wheel would be the hard part.* It is hard. But the harder part is patience: waiting for clay to dry, waiting for the kiln, waiting a week to see a result. Adults aren't used to a hobby that makes them wait that long. Once people accept the pace, they relax and the work gets better.

*Eight is a good number.* It's small enough that I can get to everyone, and big enough that people learn from watching each other. A lot of the best teaching happens when someone sees their neighbor fix a problem and says, "Oh, that's what you're doing."

**What I'm doing differently in year two**

I'm being clearer at the start about what the mug involves and why we get there the way we do. I'm also paying more attention to the pieces that aren't mugs. Some of my favorite work from the first year was a plain, slightly uneven bowl that its maker was proud of.

I've also stopped thinking of my job as teaching people to make pots. It's closer to helping them get comfortable with not being good at something yet. That's harder for adults than it is for kids, and it's the main thing I try to help with now.

If you've thought about trying clay, you don't have to be good at it first. Come on a Saturday, bring a bit of patience, and expect to make a bowl before you make a mug. The mug will come.
