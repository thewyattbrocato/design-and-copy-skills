**Title: Everyone Wants to Make a Mug: What I Learned in My First Year Teaching Beginners**

Last spring we started Saturday classes for adult beginners at Third Fire Pottery. Groups are small, eight people at a time, and I've now watched a lot of first-timers sit down at the wheel. I came in thinking I knew what the job was: teach people to throw. A year later I think the job is a little different. Here's what I've learned.

**Almost everyone wants to make a mug**

On the first Saturday I go around the table and ask what people hope to make. The answer is nearly always a mug. Sometimes it's a mug for a specific person, or the mug they drink from every morning and want to replace with one they made.

I get it. A mug is personal and useful, and you can picture it in your hand. It's also the hardest thing I could hand a beginner.

**Why the mug is so hard**

A mug looks like a simple cylinder with a handle. In practice it asks you to get several things right at once:

- **Even walls.** Too thick and it's heavy and clunky. Too thin and it slumps or cracks. Beginners tend to leave the bottom thick and the rim thin, so the mug feels wrong in the hand even when it looks fine.
- **A clean, compressed base.** Skip this and the bottom cracks in the kiln, often after you've already put in hours of work.
- **A handle that fits the body.** The handle has to be the right size and thickness, and it has to attach well. It also has to dry at the same pace as the cup. If it dries faster, it pulls away.
- **Trimming.** A good foot makes a mug look finished. It also takes a steady hand and a sense of how much clay is left inside the wall, and that sense only comes from practice.
- **Shrinkage.** Clay shrinks as it dries and again in the kiln. Beginners are often surprised that the generous mug they made comes out noticeably smaller.

Each of these is learnable. Doing all of them in your first few weeks, while you're still learning to center clay, is a lot to ask.

**What I do instead**

I don't tell people they can't make a mug. That would be a good way to lose a room. I tell them we'll get there, and that the road goes through other things first.

We start with cylinders and simple bowls. A cylinder is a mug without the handle, and nearly everything that goes wrong in a mug goes wrong in a cylinder first. Once someone can pull up even walls and finish a clean rim, the mug stops being scary. The skills are already in their hands.

Bowls teach the other half. They help people feel how the base is compressed and how the form moves from the bottom up. They're also forgiving. A lopsided bowl is still a bowl, and people tend to love it anyway.

I also encourage students to throw a few of everything instead of one precious piece. A beginner who makes one thing over a whole evening gets attached to it and learns very little. A beginner who makes five learns from every one.

**Centering is the real first lesson**

Centering the clay on the wheel is the hardest thing in the first few weeks, and it has little to do with strength. It's about posture, bracing your arms, and staying steady. People get frustrated, and I tell them that's normal. Every potter I know remembers fighting with clay that wouldn't center. Once it clicks, it clicks, but it takes longer for some people than others.

**What surprised me**

I expected to spend most of my time on technique. I spend a lot of it on expectations. Many adults aren't used to being bad at something in front of other people. They apologize for their pots and call them ugly. I try to change that early, because I've found that people who can laugh at a collapsed pot keep coming back, and people who can't often stop.

I also didn't expect the group to matter so much. With eight people, there's enough room for everyone to get attention and enough energy that someone's small win lifts the whole table.

**What I'd tell a new student**

Your first mug probably won't be your best. It might be heavy, a little crooked, with a handle that's seen better days. Use it anyway. You'll notice what to change on the next one, and that's how people improve.

I'm looking forward to year two. If you've been thinking about trying a class, come sit at the wheel. Bring a mug-shaped goal, and we'll work our way to it.
