# **The Mug Problem: What a Year of Saturday Classes Taught Me**

When we launched Saturday morning classes at Third Fire Pottery last spring, I thought I knew what to expect. I've been throwing for twenty years, taught workshops here and there, helped a handful of friends at the wheel. But teaching a full class of complete beginners, week after week, is something else entirely. A year in, I've learned that the gap between knowing how to make something and knowing how to teach someone to make it is about as wide as the gap between wedging clay and centering it. Wide. Humbling.

The first thing I learned is that almost everyone—I'd say 90%—walks into that first class wanting to make a mug. Not a bowl. Not a simple cylinder. A mug. With a handle. I'm not sure why this surprised me, but it did. I think it's because a mug feels achievable in a way abstract pottery doesn't. You use mugs. You want a mug *you made*. There's something beautifully practical about that impulse.

The second thing I learned is that a mug is possibly the hardest beginner project on the wheel.

This sounds like it shouldn't be true. A mug is just a cylinder with a handle, right? But here's what I didn't fully appreciate before: a mug demands competence in three separate skills—throwing a straight-walled form, pulling a handle that doesn't collapse, and getting the whole thing to feel balanced and functional. More than that, the mug is unforgiving. A wonky decorative bowl can be beautiful. A lopsided mug just feels wrong in your hand. Students *feel* the difference immediately.

So this year, I've learned to say yes to mugs, but not right away. The first four classes, we throw bowls, small ones. Wide, forgiving forms that teach centering and opening without the psychological pressure of "this needs to be perfect." Then we move to cylinders. Then—maybe—we add the handle. I've watched the frustration level drop dramatically. It turns out that students don't actually need to make a mug on week one. What they need is to feel the clay respond to their hands, to understand how the wheel works, to build confidence. The mugs come later, and they're better for it.

I've also learned to stop assuming I know what's going wrong. Early on, if someone's cylinder collapsed or their walls got thick and uneven, I'd jump in with instructions. Now I ask questions first. "What do you think happened?" Most of the time, they already know. They felt it. They knew their hands drifted or they didn't open evenly from the start. They just needed space to notice it themselves. The learning sticks better that way.

The thing that's surprised me most, though, is how honest people become with clay. There's something about the material that doesn't let you hide. You can't fake centering, can't bluff your way through opening, can't willpower a handle into existence if your technique isn't there. In that first hour or two, people get frustrated—at the clay, at themselves, at me. But then something shifts. They start laughing at their failures. They watch someone else's cylinder collapse and feel better. They throw the same form five times in a row until the muscle memory clicks. There's a real humility that sets in, and I've found that's where the growth happens.

I've learned that beginners need permission to make ugly pots. They need to know that the goal isn't a perfect mug by week eight—it's to build a relationship with the material. To learn what clay wants to do, and what they're capable of convincing it to do. Some of my students will come back next session. Some won't. But the ones who keep showing up? They're not the people who threw a perfect bowl on day one. They're the people who threw a terrible one, and came back anyway.

So here's what I'm telling myself as I plan next spring's classes: slow down, ask more questions, and stop worrying about the mugs. The mugs will come. In the meantime, there's plenty of learning happening in all those collapsed cylinders and wobbly bowls.

It's just going to take a little time. Like everything good with clay, it always does.
