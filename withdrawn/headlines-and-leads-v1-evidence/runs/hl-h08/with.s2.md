**Title: Everyone Wants to Make a Mug First. Here's What a Year of Teaching Beginners Taught Me.**

On the first Saturday of every new session, I ask the group what they want to make. Someone says "a bowl" and someone says "a vase." Most of the room says "a mug."

I get it. A mug is the thing you hold every morning. Making your own feels like the whole point of learning pottery. It's also the hardest thing I could hand a beginner.

Our Saturday classes for adult beginners started last spring, with groups of eight. I've now spent a year watching people meet clay for the first time. Here's what I'd tell anyone thinking about joining us.

**A mug is six skills pretending to be one object**

A mug asks you to center clay, open it evenly, pull up straight walls, and keep them an even thickness. Then you have to trim a foot, and pull and attach a handle at the right moment, and the handle has to feel good in a hand. A bowl needs the first few of those. A mug needs all of them, in order, and a mistake early on shows up later.

The handle is where most mugs go wrong. It has to be attached when the body and the handle are at about the same dryness. Too wet and it slumps, too dry and it cracks away in the kiln. Nobody can feel that difference on day one, and that's normal. It takes a lot of repetitions to learn.

**We start with cylinders, and that's on purpose**

For the first few weeks, we make cylinders, bowls, and small cups without handles. It isn't the exciting part, and I know it. But a cylinder is a mug body without the handle, so every one you make is practice for the mug you actually want.

When I explain it that way, people stop feeling like they're being kept from the good stuff. They start seeing that they're building toward it. By the time we get to handles, they already know what even walls feel like under their fingers, and that makes the handle much less frustrating.

**Your first pieces will be heavy, and that's fine**

Almost every beginner's early work is thick and heavy, especially at the base. People are nervous about going too thin and collapsing the piece, so they leave extra clay. I'd rather you leave extra clay than none. Thick pieces survive, and you learn from them.

Trimming is how we deal with it. Once a piece has dried a bit, you turn it over and carve away what you didn't need. Lots of students are surprised that trimming is where a piece starts to look finished. A heavy lump can turn into something light and elegant, and seeing it happen is one of my favorite moments in class.

**Slow down**

The most common beginner problem isn't strength or skill. It's speed. People rush the wheel, and rushing makes clay wobble. If you speed up your hands, the clay speeds up its wobble too.

The best advice I give is "slower, and let the clay finish moving before you do." It sounds too simple, but it fixes a surprising number of problems. Anything I can say to bring the room's pace down by a notch makes the whole class better.

**Expect to lose some pieces**

Clay is unforgiving in ways other crafts aren't. Pieces collapse on the wheel, crack as they dry, or come out of the kiln different from how they went in. I tell every group on the first day that this will happen to them, and that it happens to me too.

I think saying it early helps. A student who expects to lose a piece is more relaxed when it happens, and more willing to try again. The ones who struggle most are the ones who think a failed pot means they're bad at it. It doesn't. It means they're doing pottery.

**Why groups of eight**

I kept classes small on purpose. With eight people, I can get to everyone during a session, and watch hands as well as pots. Pottery is very physical, and a lot of what I teach is "feel this, not that." That's hard to do from across a room.

The group also does a lot of the teaching. People notice what their neighbor figured out and borrow it. Somebody celebrates when a piece centers for the first time, and the whole table feels it.

**So, about that mug**

Yes, you will make one. By the time we get there, you'll have enough practice that it's a real challenge instead of an impossible one. Your first mug probably won't be perfect. The handle might be a little off, or the walls a little thick. But it will be yours, and it will hold coffee, and you'll know exactly how much work went into it.

If you've been thinking about trying it, come make something with us. Bring patience and clothes you don't mind getting muddy. We'll start with a cylinder.
