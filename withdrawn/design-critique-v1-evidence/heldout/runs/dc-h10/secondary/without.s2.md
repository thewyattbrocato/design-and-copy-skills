Based on your description, the main problem is the gold on white. The hairlines and the 32px favicon will make it worse.

**Contrast is the biggest issue.** #c9a227 on white is about 2.4:1. That's below the 3:1 minimum for large text, and thin strokes need more contrast than thick ones, not less. On a shop sign it may read as faint from across the street, and in a cup-sized or phone-sized view the script will wash out.

**The favicon won't work as designed.** At 32px, a hairline stroke is less than a pixel wide, so it either disappears or anti-aliases into a gray smudge. The leaf above the "i" will likely vanish entirely. Script letterforms also rely on their thin joins to be readable, and those are the first thing to go at that size. For the favicon, make a separate simplified mark: a bold monogram like "P" or "PT", a solid leaf, and a heavier stroke weight. Don't just shrink the wordmark.

**Paper cups.** Hairlines are risky in print. Most printers want strokes at least around 0.25–0.5pt, and very thin gold lines often fill in, break up, or print unevenly, especially on kraft or uncoated stock. If the cups are white, the gold will have the same contrast problem. Metallic gold foil is an option, but it needs a spot-color or foil setup and is more expensive.

**Suggestions:**
- Keep the gold as an accent, but darken it for text. A deeper ochre or antique gold in the range of roughly #8a6d1a to #9c7d20 will get you much closer to 4.5:1 while still reading as gold. Or use a deep tea green or dark brown for the wordmark and reserve the gold for the leaf or a rule.
- Thicken the hairlines to a weight that survives reduction. A useful test is to print or view the logo at the smallest real size you'll use. If the script breaks down, thicken it before refining it further.
- Make the leaf larger relative to the wordmark, or give it a solid fill, so it still reads at small sizes.
- Check the logo in grayscale. If it doesn't hold up as a silhouette, the color is doing too much work.

**Quick checks you can do now:** view the logo at 32px on a phone or in a browser tab, step back to arm's length from a printout, and squint at it. If you can't read "Pemberly" in those conditions, the design needs weight or contrast changes.
