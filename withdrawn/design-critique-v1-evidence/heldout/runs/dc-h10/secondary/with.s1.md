Going only on your description, I'd fix three things before this goes on anything.

**1. The gold is too light for white.** I estimate #c9a227 on white at about 2.4:1 contrast. That's weak for a sign or cup, and it's worse for thin strokes, which lose even more contrast as they anti-alias. A darker gold such as #8a6d1a is about 4.9:1 and still reads as gold. Or keep #c9a227 but use it on a dark ground (deep green, charcoal, or espresso brown), where it will look richer and hold up better.

**2. Hairlines won't survive the smaller and rougher uses.**
- **Favicon (32px):** The script will turn into noise, and the leaf will vanish or become a smudge. Make a separate favicon: a bold monogram ("P" or "PT") or a simple leaf-and-letter mark with a heavier stroke. It doesn't need to be the full wordmark.
- **Paper cups:** Printers and cup-coating processes usually thicken or break strokes below roughly 0.5 pt. Ask the printer for a minimum stroke spec. Gold ink on paper also prints as a duller ochre in CMYK, so get a physical proof, not just a screen check.
- **Sign:** Hairlines disappear at distance. Check legibility from the sidewalk, and consider a heavier cut for signage.

**3. The leaf is probably too small to matter.** A tiny detail above the "i" is charming at large sizes and disappears at small ones. If it has to stay, make it bolder, or treat it as an optional accent that the favicon and cups can drop.

**Quick checks you can run yourself:**
- Shrink the logo to 32px and to about 1 inch wide. If it's unreadable, the small-size version needs its own design.
- Convert it to grayscale. If the gold nearly vanishes, the value contrast is too low.
- Ask a print shop for a proof on the actual cup stock before ordering.

**What's working:** A script wordmark with a gold accent suits a tea shop, and the hairline style reads as refined. Keep that feel, but make the strokes sturdier and the gold darker or put it on a dark field.
