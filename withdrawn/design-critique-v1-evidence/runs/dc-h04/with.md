**Ship Option B**, with a few changes.

**Why**

- **Your audience already believes in you.** Repeat donors don't need to be persuaded that reading rooms matter. They need the shortest path to giving, and a feeling that their money does something concrete. B does both. The headline "$50 stocks one shelf for a month" tells them what their gift buys. The stories with numbers back that up.
- **The form is visible right away on a phone.** In A, a full-width photo plus headline pushes the form below the fold on a small screen. Repeat givers will have to scroll past the pitch to reach it. B puts the form first. Because the columns stack on mobile, the form should go above the stories.
- **A has the weaker form.** It has no monthly/one-time toggle. For second- and third-time givers, a monthly option is the biggest revenue opportunity on the page. A also doesn't show what's behind "Give now", such as card fields or a wallet option. That makes the real checkout a mystery, and it may add a step.
- **B's impact is specific.** "Priya, 9: read 14 books this term" is concrete and believable. A's "Every child deserves a reading room" is a sentiment almost any literacy charity could use.
- **The photo is the one thing A does better.** It adds emotional warmth. But supporters have already felt that, so the photo's value is lower for this audience. It's also easy to bring into B in a smaller form.

**What I'd change before launch**

1. **Order for mobile.** Stack it as headline, then amount buttons and the monthly/one-time toggle, then card fields, then the stories. Put one short story directly under the button as reassurance, and the other two below.
2. **Make checkout fast on a phone.** Offer Apple Pay and Google Pay at the top of the form. Typing card numbers on a phone is the biggest source of drop-off. Use large tap targets and numeric keyboards for card and amount fields.
3. **Preselect an amount and a default frequency.** Preselect $50, since it matches the headline. Test whether defaulting to monthly beats one-time, but make sure the toggle is obvious and honest.
4. **Tie the buttons to the headline.** Show what each amount buys, for example $25 = half a shelf, $50 = one shelf, $100 = two shelves. Include a custom amount. Update the button label to match, such as "Give $50 monthly".
5. **Add one small photo.** Use a single image of a child reading, either beside one story or as a thin strip above the headline. It should be small enough that the form stays visible without scrolling. This gets you some of A's warmth without the cost.
6. **Recognize returning donors.** If you can, greet them ("Thanks for giving again") and prefill their details. This is probably worth more than any layout choice for your audience.
7. **Add trust details near the button.** Include the tax-deductible note, EIN, a secure-payment cue, and a line on where the money goes. Keep these short.
8. **Check the stories.** Get consent for names, ages, and numbers, and make sure each figure is accurate. Specific claims lose trust if donors later find they were stretched. If "$50 stocks one shelf" is a simplification, say so in a footnote.

**How to settle the board**

I'm judging from the descriptions and general donation-page patterns, not your data. If the board is still split, run a two-week A/B test on real traffic. Measure completed gifts per visitor and average gift size, and track the monthly-giving rate separately. Only B has a monthly option, so I'd expect it to win on that. If A somehow wins, the likely reason is that the photo drives more emotional response than I'm assuming. In that case, make the photo in B larger rather than switching designs.
