Replace the pie with a **sorted horizontal bar chart**, with the percentage printed at the end of each bar.

**Why the pie fails**
- Eleven slices is far beyond what a pie can show. People judge angle and area poorly, and the bottom five slices (4%, 3%, 2%, 2%, 1%) look almost the same size.
- The rainbow legend makes readers look back and forth between the legend and the slices. Hue also implies the categories are unrelated, and it doesn't tell the board what matters.

**What to build**
1. **Sort by value, largest at top.** Meals at 38% and Shelter at 21% then show up at once as about 60% of spend.
2. **Label each bar directly** with its name on the left and its value on the right (e.g. "Meals 38%"). This removes the legend.
3. **Start the axis at zero**, with no gridlines, frame or 3D, and no gradients.
4. **Use one muted color for all bars.** If there's a point to make, use a single accent color on it. For example, highlight Admin + Fundraising in one color to show overhead is about 5%, which is usually what a board wants to know.
5. **Group the tail if it's still busy.** You could show the top 7 or 8 programs and put the rest in "Other (Outreach, Admin, Fundraising, Events, Other): 12%". With 11 bars, though, a bar chart is still readable, so grouping is optional.
6. **Write a title that states the finding**, such as "Meals and Shelter make up 59% of our spending." Add a sentence like that as text under the chart, so the takeaway doesn't depend on reading the chart.

**Alternative:** a single 100% stacked bar works for a "whole budget" view. With 11 parts, though, the small segments get cramped and labels collide, so I'd use it only after grouping into 4–5 categories (Meals, Shelter, Other programs, Overhead).

**Check the data:** your percentages sum to 100%, so a part-of-whole framing is valid. State the fiscal year and total dollar amount in the subtitle. If boards want dollars, you could put both on the bar label ("Meals $1.9M, 38%").
