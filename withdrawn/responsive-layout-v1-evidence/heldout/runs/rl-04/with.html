Weeknight Cooking

Why your weeknight chili tastes flat

By Marisol Quintero 9 min read

You followed the recipe. The beans are tender, the beef is cooked through, the pot smells vaguely like chili. And yet the first spoonful is dull, like a song played in the next room. Flat chili is the most common complaint I hear from people who cook it on a Tuesday, and it almost never means you need more heat or a secret ingredient. It means something in the order of operations got skipped.

Start with the spices, because that's where most of the damage happens. Chili powder, cumin and oregano dumped into liquid taste like dust in tomato water. Their flavor compounds are fat-soluble, so they need to hit hot oil first. After you brown the meat and soften the onions, push everything aside, add a spoonful of fat and the spices, and stir for thirty to sixty seconds until the whole pot smells toasted and a little almost-too-much. That minute does more than an hour of simmering ever will.

Ingredients

  • 1½ lb ground beef or chuck, chopped
  • 1 large yellow onion, diced
  • 3 tbsp tomato paste
  • 3 tbsp chili powder
  • 2 tsp ground cumin
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika
  • One 28 oz can crushed tomatoes
  • Two 15 oz cans beans, drained
  • 1 tbsp fish sauce or soy sauce
  • 1 lime, for the finish
Serves 6. Salt to taste, in stages.

Next, the tomato paste. Straight from the can it's raw and faintly metallic, and stirring it into a simmering pot doesn't fix that. Give it a couple of minutes in the hot pan with the onions, pressing it against the bottom until it darkens from bright red to brick. It will stick and form a crust. Good, that's flavor. Deglaze with a splash of beer, stock or water and scrape it all back into the pot. This single step adds the savory backbone people assume comes from long cooking.

Flat chili isn't missing an ingredient. It's missing a few minutes of attention at the right moments.

Then there's depth, the quiet savory note that makes you take a second bite. A tablespoon of fish sauce or soy sauce is the cheapest way to get it, and no one will taste fish. Worcestershire, a square of dark chocolate, or a spoonful of the liquid from a jar of pickled jalapeños all work in the same way. Add one of them, not all of them. You're not trying to make the pot taste like something new, just more like itself.

Finally, the part almost everyone forgets: season at the end, and add acid. Chili simmers and reduces, so salt added early can mislead you in both directions. Salt in stages, and taste again right before serving. When it still seems muted, the answer is usually brightness, not more salt. A squeeze of lime, a splash of cider vinegar, or a handful of chopped cilantro lifts everything at once. And if you can, make it a day ahead. Chili that rests overnight tastes rounder, and a flat pot often wakes up on its own.
