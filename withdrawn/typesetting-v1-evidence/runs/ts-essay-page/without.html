Why bridges hum

Every span is a very large instrument, and the wind, the traffic and the river are always playing it.

Words by Staff Writer · 8 min read

Stand on a long bridge at night, when the traffic has thinned, and put your palm flat against the cold rail. Beneath the skin of your hand there is a faint, steady tremor, too regular to be a passing truck and too low to be called a sound. The bridge is humming. It has been humming, in all likelihood, since the day it opened.

The explanation begins with a plain fact of physics: everything that is built has a natural frequency. Strike a tuning fork and it rings at one pitch because its shape and stiffness allow little else. A bridge is the same kind of object at a vastly larger scale. Its deck, cables and towers each prefer to oscillate at particular rates, usually well below a single cycle per second for the whole span, and much higher for a single stay cable or steel hanger.

What keeps the instrument sounding is a supply of small, unplanned pushes. Tires crossing expansion joints deliver a rhythmic tap. Wind curling off the edge of the deck sheds tiny vortices, first on one side and then the other. Even the river contributes, through the pier foundations, as currents tug on the structure below the waterline. None of these forces is large. But a structure that is pushed near its preferred rate will answer with disproportionate enthusiasm.

A bridge is never at rest. It is only ever quiet enough that we forget to listen.

The sound of a cable

Suspension and cable-stayed bridges hum most audibly, because their cables behave like enormous guitar strings. A stay several hundred feet long, held under tension of many tons, will vibrate at a fundamental pitch and a ladder of overtones above it. Engineers who walk the cables with accelerometers can read the tension of each one from its frequency alone. A cable that has slipped, or begun to corrode inside its sheathing, sounds subtly wrong.

The hum is not always benign, and the history of bridge building is partly a history of learning that lesson. Designers now add dampers, helical ridges on cable sheaths and carefully shaped deck edges, all meant to scatter the energy that wind and rain would otherwise feed into a single note. The goal is not silence. It is to make sure the bridge never settles into a rhythm it cannot afford.

So the next time your hand finds that tremor in the rail, take it as a good sign. A humming bridge is a bridge that is moving, flexing and shedding energy as it was designed to. The structures that worry the people who look after them are the ones that have gone completely still.
