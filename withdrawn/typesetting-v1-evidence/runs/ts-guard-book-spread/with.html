Marguerite Vance
Chapter Seven

The Salt Archive

❦

The tide had gone out further than anyone in Port Allard could remember, and it left behind the long grey shelf of the harbour floor, strewn with bottles, rope and the ribs of boats that had been forgotten for a century. Ilse Corran stood at the end of the customs pier with her boots in her hand and watched the gulls walk about on the mud as though they had been granted a deed to it.

She had come back to the town for her aunt's funeral and had stayed, so far, for eleven days. There was the house to be sorted, and the cat to be argued with, and the long afternoons in which nobody called and the telephone, an object of cream-coloured plastic older than Ilse herself, sat in the hall like a patient animal. On the twelfth morning it rang.

"Miss Corran?" The voice was dry and careful, a voice that had spent its life lowering itself into small spaces. "I am the keeper of the town records. I believe you have something of ours."

"I have a great many things," said Ilse. "Most of them are chipped."

"This would be a key. Iron, about the length of a finger, with a salt-bloom on the bow. Your aunt was the last person to carry it."

Ilse looked down the hall to the dresser, where the key had lain among the buttons and spent matches since the day of the funeral. She had assumed it belonged to the garden shed, which had no lock, or to a trunk, which had no lid.

"Where would I bring it?" she asked at last.

"Below the Exchange, at the bottom of the stair. Come at slack water, if you would. The door is only honest then." He rang off before she could ask what he meant, and the receiver purred in her hand like something that had been interrupted in the middle of a thought.

She walked up through the town with the key in her coat pocket, turning it over between finger and thumb. Port Allard kept its secrets in the old manner, by never raising them: shutters closed against a wind that was not blowing, a baker who nodded without looking up, a child on a step who counted her passing under his breath. At the Exchange, a heavy building with a clock that had been wrong since the war, the pavement dropped away into a stairwell she had never noticed, though she had passed it every day of her childhood.

The steps were slick and green. Somewhere beneath them the sea was making the small, domestic noises of a thing going about its business, and the air smelled of cold iron and lamp oil and paper that had been wet and dried a hundred times. At the bottom stood a door, low and black, and in the door a keyhole no larger than a coin.

She hesitated. It was the hesitation of a person who knows, quite clearly, that a certain kind of life lies on the far side of a certain kind of door, and that it will not be possible to return the receipt. Then the water drew back from the lowest step with a long sigh, and the key, as though it had been waiting years for precisely this courtesy, slid home.

The lock turned over with a sound like a held breath let go. Inside, a lamp was already burning.
