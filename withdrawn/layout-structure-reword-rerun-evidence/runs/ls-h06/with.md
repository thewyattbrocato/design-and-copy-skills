Break the grid when the break has a job you can name. If you can't say what the break is for, take it out.

## Good reasons to break it

- **A full-bleed image that is the page.** A photo or illustration that runs to the viewport edges signals "this is the moment." Use it for an opening hero, a chapter break, or a product shot that carries the pitch.
- **A pull quote hanging into the margin.** The quote sits partly outside the text column. It draws the eye and still marks where the text edge is.
- **One odd item in an obedient set.** A single featured card spans two columns. One product has a different background. One testimonial is larger than the others. The contrast only works because everything around it is regular.
- **A figure wider than the text.** A chart, wide screenshot, or code block can run past the reading column while the prose keeps its measure. Wide content gets the space it needs without stretching the lines of text.
- **An expressive piece where the break is the point.** Editorial openers, posters, and portfolio or campaign pages can overlap, rotate, or offset elements. Keep a clear entry point and keep the key facts easy to find.
- **A pacing change.** On a long page, one loud, off-grid section after several calm ones gives the reader a beat to react to.

## How to keep it from looking like a mistake

1. **Break by a lot or not at all.** A 6px overhang looks like a bug. A deliberate break is large enough to be unmistakable, such as a whole column, the full bleed, or a clearly different size.
2. **Keep everything else strict.** The surrounding grid has to be visibly consistent. Without that, the break reads as drift.
3. **Anchor the break to something.** Align the overhanging element to a column edge, the page edge, or another element's edge. The break should still sit on a line, just a different one.
4. **Make it deliberate in kind, not just in size.** Change scale, color, or spacing along with position. A shifted element that is otherwise identical looks misaligned. A larger, differently treated one looks intentional.
5. **Break rarely.** Use one or two breaks per screen at most. If the same break shows up three times, it has become a rule, so build it into the grid.
6. **Keep the entry point and the action clear.** Don't let the break bury the headline or the main button.
7. **Test narrow and long.** Breaks often fail when the viewport shrinks or the text gets longer. Overhangs can clip, collide, or force horizontal scroll. Check the stacked mobile order too. Most breaks should collapse into the normal column there.

The quick test is to ask what the break does that staying on the grid wouldn't. If the answer is "emphasis," "scale," "rhythm," or "room for wide content," keep it. If the answer is "it looked more interesting," drop it or make it bolder until it has a job.
