# Halvorsen Freight call: 60 minutes, Thursday

## Goal
Don't pitch. Leave with (1) a shared one-sentence problem statement, (2) a named metric or two, and (3) a small next step, such as a data pull or a pilot scoped to Joliet. If the group leaves still disagreeing but agrees on what data would settle it, that's a win.

My working hypothesis is that these may be one chain, not three problems. No-shows lead to last-minute coverage, which leads to overtime. Poor forecasts lead to wrong staffing levels, which also leads to overtime or idle labour. Everyone may be looking at a different link. This is a hypothesis, so say it as a question and let them confirm or kill it.

## Before the call
- **Ask Marco for 10 minutes with the shift leads, or at least their names and what they care about.** Even a short message helps: "What's the one thing that makes a shift go badly?"
- **Ask Dana and Marco for one number each.** For example, last quarter's overtime hours or spend at Joliet, and the forecast-vs-actual variance they're unhappy with. Arriving with their numbers lets you speak to the details.
- **Ask Kevin and Ola for nothing yet.** Find out which systems are involved (WMS, timekeeping/UKG-type tools) and what procurement will want, so you can speak to both in the last stretch.
- **Send a short pre-read:** the agenda and the line "We'll spend most of the time on your situation, not our product."

## Agenda

| Time | Segment | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Frame and goal | You |
| 5–20 | Each person's view of the problem | Shift leads first |
| 20–35 | Connect the dots | You, at a shared whiteboard or doc |
| 35–45 | What would "fixed" look like | Dana and Marco |
| 45–55 | What it would take to test | Kevin and Ola |
| 55–60 | Next steps and owners | You |

## What to say

**Opening (0–5)**
> "Thanks, everyone. I've spoken with Marco and Dana separately, and what I took from those conversations is that people see this a bit differently, which is normal for operations like this. I don't want to assume I know which view is right. For the next hour, I'd like to hear how each of you sees it, figure out where those views connect, and decide what's worth testing. I'm not going to demo anything today. By the end, I'd like us to agree on what the problem is and what a small next step looks like."

**Perspectives (5–20)**

Start with the shift leads, because they have the least status in the room and are most likely to be anchored by Marco and Dana if they go later.
> "[Name], you're closest to the floor. Walk me through the last shift that went badly. What happened?"

Follow up with:
- "When someone doesn't show, what do you do in the next hour? Who do you call?"
- "How often does that happen on a normal week?"

Then Marco:
> "Marco, you've told me overtime is the big one. When overtime spikes, what's usually behind it?"

Then Dana:
> "Dana, when you say forecasting accuracy, what decision does a bad forecast lead to? Is it staffing, budget, or customer commitments?"

Don't debate during this segment. Reflect each view back in one sentence, and write it down where everyone can see it.

**Connect the dots (20–35)**
> "Here's something I'm noticing, and tell me if it's wrong. If a forecast is off, you either end up short-staffed or over. If you're short and people also don't show, someone has to cover, and that's often overtime. So overtime might be the cost showing up, with the forecast and attendance as inputs. Does that match what you see, or is overtime driven by something different?"

Then ask the shift leads directly:
> "You said scheduling itself is fine. I believe you. If schedules are built well but attendance breaks them, what would help most on the day?"

This validates their view without conceding that the problem is outside software's scope. Attendance is a planning problem too: buffers, call-off prediction, shift-swap and cover pools.

Then ask the group:
> "If you could only see one number weekly to know whether this is getting better, which would it be?"

**What "fixed" looks like (35–45)**
> "Dana, if this were working in six months, what would you see in the numbers? Marco, what would you see on the floor?"

Push for specifics: "overtime down X%" or "forecast within Y% by shift." If they can't give a number, that's a finding. Say so: "It sounds like we don't have a baseline yet, so step one might be getting one."

**Kevin and Ola (45–55)**
To Kevin:
> "If we were to look at Joliet's data, what would it take? Where does the time and attendance data live, and who would need to approve access?"

To Ola:
> "What does your process look like for something like a limited pilot? What would you need to see, and how long does it usually take?"

Keep it practical, and note any blockers without trying to resolve them live.

**Next steps (55–60)**
> "Here's what I heard: [one-sentence problem statement]. The next step I'd suggest is [for example, a data pull of 3–6 months of schedules, attendance, and overtime at Joliet, so we can see how much of the overtime traces back to no-shows vs. forecast misses]. Who can own that on your side, and can we reconvene in two weeks?"

Send a recap email within a few hours, listing the shared problem statement, the data requested, and the named owners.

## Pitfalls to avoid
- **Don't say "you're all right."** It sounds like a dodge. Say "these may connect," and make them confirm it.
- **Don't correct the shift leads.** If they say scheduling is fine, take it as information.
- **Don't let Dana and Marco run the room.** If they debate, redirect: "Let's park that. What data would settle it?"
- **Don't talk about features.** If asked for a demo, say: "Happy to, but I'd rather show you something built on your data. That's what the pilot would be."
- **Don't claim results you can't back up.** Avoid saying "we typically cut overtime by X%" unless you can cite a comparable customer.

## If it goes sideways
- **If two people argue,** say: "This is useful, because it tells us we need data. What would each of you want to see to be convinced?"
- **If the shift leads stay quiet,** ask them something concrete and low-stakes, like "What's the hardest day of the week?"
- **If nobody will commit to a next step,** ask for the smallest one: "Can Marco and I do a 30-minute working session with a shift lead to look at one week of data?"

If you tell me what the product does well (attendance prediction, forecasting, scheduling, or something else), I can tighten the connect-the-dots section to fit what you can credibly offer.
