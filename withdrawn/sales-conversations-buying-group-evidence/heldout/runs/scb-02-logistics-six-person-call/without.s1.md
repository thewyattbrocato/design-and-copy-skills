# Halvorsen Freight call plan (60 min)

## Strategy
Don't try to win the argument about which problem is the real one. Marco, Dana and the shift leads may each be describing a different link of the same chain:

**People don't show up → shifts run short against plan → overtime to cover → the labour forecast looks wrong after the fact**

That's a hypothesis, not a finding, so treat it that way. Use the call to test it with them, and get agreement on one small next step. Don't demo or talk price. Success is that they leave agreeing on how to find out what the problem is, with you as the one running that.

## Before the call
- **Marco:** Ask him to introduce the shift leads, or tell you what they care about. Ask what he'd be fine with you saying about overtime.
- **Dana:** Ask what a forecast miss costs them, and whether she has numbers by site.
- **Everyone:** Send a 3-line agenda the day before. Ask whoever can to bring rough numbers for call-out/no-show rate, overtime hours, and forecast vs. actual volume. These don't need to be clean.
- **Shift leads:** They're the most likely to be skeptical, because they already think scheduling is fine. Give them the floor first.

## Agenda

| Time | Segment | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Open and frame | Set the tone as listening and diagnosing |
| 5–20 | Round-robin: "the last time labour went wrong" | Get each person's version, shift leads first |
| 20–30 | Playback and test the chain | Show the views connect, and check you're right |
| 30–45 | What we'd need to know | Identify data and who owns it (Kevin, Dana) |
| 45–55 | Proposed next step | One scoped diagnostic, ideally at Joliet |
| 55–60 | Close | Owners, dates, and a follow-up |

## What to say

**Open (0–5)**
> "Thanks for making the time. I've talked with Marco and Dana separately, and I've heard some different takes on where the labour challenges are. I think that's useful, because it probably means this touches more than one part of the operation. I'm not going to demo anything today. I'd like to hear how each of you sees it, tell you what I'm hearing, and see if we can agree on a way to find out what's really going on. If that doesn't look worthwhile by the end, I'll say so."

**Round-robin (5–20)**
Start with the shift leads: "I haven't met you before, and you see the floor more directly than anyone on this call. Can you walk me through the last time a shift went sideways? What happened?"

Then Marco, then Dana, with the same question. Follow-ups:
- "What did you do about it in the moment?"
- "How often does that happen?"
- "What did it cost, in overtime, missed SLAs, or something else?"

Don't correct anyone or mention your product here. Write down their exact words.

For Ola and Kevin, ask lightly near the end of the round: "Ola, Kevin, is there anything about how you'd need to evaluate something like this that we should keep in mind?" That gets their constraints on the table without making them defend a position.

**Playback (20–30)**
> "Here's what I'm hearing. Tell me where I've got it wrong. The shift leads say scheduling is fine, but people not showing up leaves them short. Marco, that's probably what you see as overtime, because someone has to cover. Dana, when that happens, the plan and the actuals drift apart, and that may be part of what looks like a forecasting problem. Do those feel like the same issue from different seats, or am I forcing a connection?"

Then **stop talking** and let them respond. If they push back, such as "no, our forecast is actually off on volume", take that seriously. It means the chain only partly holds, and you should say so.

**Quantify (30–45)**
> "I don't want to guess how much of the overtime is from no-shows versus forecast misses versus something else. Do we have the data to split that out? Kevin, what systems would hold shift attendance, scheduled hours and actual hours? Dana, is volume forecast vs. actual tracked by site?"

Get names and rough timelines. Don't promise integration answers yourself. For anything technical, say "I'd want to confirm that with our team."

**Next step (45–55)**
> "What I'd suggest is a short look at one site, probably Joliet, using a few months of attendance, schedule and overtime data. The goal is to break overtime down by cause. At the end you'd know how much comes from absences, how much from forecast error, and how much from scheduling, whether or not you ever work with us. Would that be useful, or is there a better place to start?"

Offering it with "whether or not you ever work with us" lowers the barrier. Only say it if you can actually deliver it that way.

**Procurement (Ola)**
If Ola asks about pricing or process, say: "Happy to cover that. I'd rather not quote anything until we know which problem we're solving. What does your process look like for something like a pilot or assessment?"

**Close (55–60)**
> "To confirm: [Kevin] will check what data exists, [Dana] will pull forecast vs. actual by site, and [Marco] will check with the shift leads on attendance records. I'll send a summary today with these owners. Can we reconnect in [1–2 weeks] to look at what we've got?"

## Things to watch for
- **Dana and Marco dominating.** Return to the shift leads: "Before we move on, did that match what you see?"
- **Shift leads staying quiet or defensive.** Don't push. Ask about specific incidents rather than opinions, which feels less like blame.
- **Someone calling absenteeism a "management issue" and not a software issue.** Acknowledge it. You can say, "That might be true, and the data will show it. Visibility into patterns is where most teams start."
- **Overclaiming.** Don't say your software fixes no-shows unless it concretely does something about them, such as shift-swap, backfill, or attendance-aware planning. Say what it does and doesn't do.

## Right after the call
Send the written summary the same day with the owners, dates and data requests. In a group that disagrees, the summary is what makes the agreed next step stick.

If you tell me which features of your product map to absenteeism, forecasting and overtime, I can tighten the playback and the next-step pitch around what you can really deliver.
