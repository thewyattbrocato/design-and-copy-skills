Your manager's advice is half right. Priya is still your best route in, but a third "just checking in" email won't work. Her silence probably isn't disinterest. She loved it, but now she has to sell it internally to three people, and that's work she hasn't had time or a reason to do. Your job is to make that work easier, and to stop depending on her alone.

**1. Send a different kind of email, not another nudge.**
Skip "any update?" and give her something she can forward. A one-page internal brief works well:
- The "saves a day a week per coordinator" quote turned into a number: coordinators × hours × loaded cost, against the $38k.
- Three short sections, one each for finance, IT, and nursing, covering what each of them will care about. That means ROI and payment terms for finance, security, SSO and integrations for IT, and fill rates, fairness and staff satisfaction for nursing.

Then ask a specific question: "Who's the right person in finance/IT/nursing, and would a 20-minute call with each be useful, or would you rather I send this to them directly?"

**2. Ask about the process, not the decision.**
Silence often means you don't know how they buy. Ask her:
- Is there a vendor review or security questionnaire for IT? Those can take weeks, and you can start now.
- Is there a budget cycle or a threshold above which finance needs a formal process?
- Is this competing with other priorities this quarter?

If she answers, you get a real timeline. If she doesn't, that tells you something too.

**3. Go around her, politely.**
You have two follow-ups and no response, so a different channel is fair:
- Send a short LinkedIn message or call her desk. Many people ignore email but answer a call.
- Offer to do the legwork: "Happy to fill out your security questionnaire in advance so IT isn't a bottleneck."
- Ask if the head of nursing would want a peer reference from another hospital group. A reference call from a nursing leader at a similar customer often moves a clinical stakeholder faster than anything you can say.

**4. Send a break-up email if that doesn't work.**
Wait about a week, then send something honest and low-pressure:

> Priya, I don't want to keep filling your inbox. It sounds like the timing may not be right, which is completely fine. I'll close this out on my end for now, but if it comes back up (budget cycle, new scheduling pain, etc.) I'm happy to pick it back up. If there's anything I could send your team that would make this easier to move forward, just say so.

These get replies more often than you'd expect, because they remove the pressure and give her an easy way to say "actually, here's what's happening."

**5. Be honest about the deal.**
Three weeks of silence with a four-hospital group, three required signers, and one champion is a stalled deal. Don't forecast it as likely. Keep it in the pipeline at a low probability until you've spoken to someone besides Priya.

**What to tell your manager:** "I'm continuing with Priya, but with a business case she can forward and a request for introductions to the other three signers. Chasing her alone hasn't worked, so I'm changing the approach." That's still consistent with "keep chasing Priya," and it shows you're managing the risk.
