## Why chasing hasn't worked

Priya said yes to the product. She hasn't said she can get three other people to move, and you haven't tested that. Your last three emails all asked her to respond to your material, so replying costs her something and gains her nothing. A third "just checking in" will likely get the same silence.

The open question is probably not whether she likes it. It's whether she can turn a good demo into a case that finance, IT and nursing each accept. Each of them is held to a different concern, and she may be dreading that work.

## The move

Don't chase. Change the ask: offer to do the hard part with her. Send one short email, then call.

> Priya, you told me Rostrly would save your coordinators a day a week. That's the line I'd lead with, but finance, IT and nursing will each ask something different, and I don't want you carrying that alone.
>
> My suggestion: 20 minutes where you tell me who the decision-maker is in each of those three, and what you think they'll worry about. I'll turn that into a one-page case with your numbers that you can edit and forward. If it's easier, I can brief any of them directly.
>
> Does Thursday at 2 or Friday at 10 work? And if the timing is wrong and this should wait until [month], tell me and I'll close it out cleanly for now.

Both exits are in the email. A reply of "not now, try in January" is better for you than silence, because you can forecast it honestly.

## What to do on that call

1. **Find out where it stalled.** Ask: "Where did this land when you took it back to the team?" If she hasn't raised it with anyone, that's your answer.
2. **Get a name and one worry per role.** For example: finance wants the cost against what Westmoor spends on coordinator time now. IT wants security and integration. Nursing wants to know it won't make rostering harder for the floor.
3. **Size it in her numbers.** Ask how many coordinators, how many hours a week, and what that time currently costs or displaces. Without that, "saves a day a week" is a feeling, not a case. Don't offer a figure of your own.
4. **Test whether she's the ally.** Ask her to do one small piece of real work, like getting an IT contact's name or the coordinator headcount, by a set date. If she does it, she's driving. If not, she's a friendly contact and not a route to a signature.
5. **Recommend a smaller first step if she's nervous.** Examples are one hospital, or a short first term, but only if you can actually offer them `[check what you can offer]`. Say what Rostrly won't do, too.

Don't re-list the cost of waiting, invent a quarter-end deadline, or discount. She already wants it, and pressure makes choosing wrong feel riskier to her.

## If she goes quiet again

Wait about a week, then send one two-line message naming both exits: "Shall we keep going on the case for finance/IT/nursing, or close this out and talk again in [month]?" After that, rank this deal low in your forecast and don't count it as late-stage. Progress is something she did, like naming a signer or booking IT. A pricing PDF you sent doesn't count.

## For your manager

"Keep chasing Priya" is half right. Priya is still the right contact, but you've sent three requests and she's replied to none. Tell them you're changing the ask, you're trying to get a second stakeholder named, and you'll call it parked if there's no reply by a set date.
