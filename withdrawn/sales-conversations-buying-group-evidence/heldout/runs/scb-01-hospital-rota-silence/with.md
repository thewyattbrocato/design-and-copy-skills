**What I'm working from**
- You told me: Priya is an ops lead and a real fan. She said it would save her coordinators a day a week. Finance, IT and the head of nursing all have to sign. Recap and pricing went out the next day, then two emails, and you've heard nothing for about 3 weeks.

**Why more chasing won't work**
Your manager is half right: stay in touch with Priya. But a third email asking for her reply is the same move that has failed twice. She has already said yes to you. What's missing is agreement from three people you haven't met. Nobody has told finance what the cost is, IT what the risk is, or nursing what changes for their staff. A recap and a price sheet addressed to Priya don't help her with any of them.

**The moves, in order**

1. **Change the ask, and give her two honest exits.** The goal is a short call where she tells you where it stands. Offering to close it out also makes a reply easier. Something like:

   > Priya, I don't want to keep nudging you if the timing isn't right. When we spoke you said Rostrly could save your coordinators a day a week, and I'd guess the hard part now is getting finance, IT and nursing comfortable. Where did it land when you took it to them? If it's stalled, I can make that part easier. Should we spend 15 minutes on Thursday at [time] working out what each of them needs to see, or would you rather I close this out and we pick it up in [month]?

   Any extra pressure would hurt here: no deadline, no "pricing expires", and no discount. She already knows what waiting costs.

2. **On that call, find out what the group has agreed.** Ask:
   - Which of the three has she actually spoken to, and what did they say? (This tells you who is for or against it.)
   - Who signs, and in what order? (This gets you names.)
   - Does nursing see the same problem she does? (This tests whether the group agrees there is a problem at all.)
   - What is each of them measured on? (This gives you each role's figure.)

   Treat the call as a test of intent. A good ally will pull a figure, name a reviewer, or get the right people into a room. A warm contact who does none of that is a route to a signature, not a champion. If that's what Priya turns out to be, rank the deal lower in your forecast and say so.

3. **Give her something she can forward.** Don't send another pitch. Build a one-page case in her numbers: coordinators × hours saved × cost per hour, plus one line per role, with `[to learn]` wherever you don't have the figure. It should cover:
   - finance: the cost against what coordinators cost today
   - IT: your security and integration answers
   - nursing: what changes for the nurses, such as swaps and fairness
   
   Don't use any figure beyond what Priya gave you. Ask her to correct the numbers; that also tests whether she's engaged.

4. **Ask to meet the others, and be specific about it.** "Could I do 20 minutes with [finance person] to answer their questions directly? You wouldn't need to be the go-between." If any of them are hostile, you'll want to hear that early. Talk to the blocker about their specific concern, and don't go over their head.

5. **Make the first commitment small and reversible.** If the group is afraid of choosing wrong across 4 hospitals, suggest a single-site start with a success test and a clean exit. I'm only suggesting this if you actually offer a pilot or short first term; I don't know your terms. Make sure the scope and the exit are real before you say it.

**If Priya doesn't reply to this one either:** send one last short note giving her the two exits again, then stop. Silence after that is an answer. Mark the deal as stalled in your forecast rather than late-stage.

Two things to check before you send anything: whether you can offer a single-site pilot or short first term, and whether Priya is allowed to share your pricing internally. If she can't, that may be part of why nothing has moved.
