**Treat Rachel's objection as legitimate, and answer it before she raises it. Don't try to win her over in front of the committee.**

Her stated reasons, "switching is a hassle" and "DocuVault's been fine," are about risk and effort. They aren't about product quality. If you attack them head-on, she'll feel cornered in front of her peers. If you ignore them, she'll raise them herself, at the moment that does the most damage.

**Before Thursday**
- **Talk to Rachel one-on-one first.** Ask what would make a switch low-risk for her, not whether she's on board. Her answer tells you what to emphasize, and it keeps her from being surprised in the room.
- **Get the ops managers to bring the pain.** Their concrete, first-hand problems with DocuVault (workarounds, delays, missed renewals) will carry more weight than anything you say. Ask them to share specific examples. Don't coach them into a debate with Rachel.
- **Learn what the CFO and legal care about.** The CFO will weigh the $85k against the switching cost and the status quo. Legal will want data migration, audit trail and compliance covered. Both are swing votes, and Rachel's view may matter less to them than you think.

**In the meeting**
1. **Open with the problem, not your product.** Start with what the ops managers experience today and what it costs in time or risk. This frames the decision as "is the status quo good enough?" instead of "is switching worth the hassle?"
2. **Make the switching plan a main section, not an appendix.** Cover timeline, who does the work, data migration, parallel running, what you handle versus what Calderon handles, and a named implementation lead. This is the most direct answer to Rachel's concern, and it shows you take it seriously.
3. **Name the hassle yourself.** Say something like: "Switching has a real cost, so here's exactly what it involves and how we keep it off your team's plate." That treats her concern as reasonable and takes the sting out of it.
4. **Give her a role in the evaluation.** Procurement can own the contract terms, the exit clauses, the SLAs and the reference checks. This turns her into someone who shapes the deal, not a blocker.
5. **Reduce the risk of the decision.** Options include a phased rollout, a pilot with one region, a termination-for-convenience clause, or a price lock. A smaller commitment is easier to say yes to.
6. **Compare against DocuVault's real renewal terms.** Ask beforehand whether they know what DocuVault will charge at renewal. "Fine" often means "I haven't looked at the price increase."

**If she pushes back live**
- Don't rebut. Acknowledge, then ask: "What would you need to see to feel comfortable with the transition?" Then answer that specifically, or commit to following up.
- Let the ops managers respond to "DocuVault's been fine" from their own experience. It lands better from them than from you.
- Don't ask the committee for a verdict on the spot if the room isn't there. Ask for an agreed next step, such as a reference call, a pilot scope or a follow-up with procurement and legal.

**Avoid**
- Singling her out, or making the pitch feel like a contest between her and the ops managers.
- Disparaging DocuVault. It makes the switch look more like a risk, and she may have chosen it.
- Overselling features. Her objection isn't about features.
