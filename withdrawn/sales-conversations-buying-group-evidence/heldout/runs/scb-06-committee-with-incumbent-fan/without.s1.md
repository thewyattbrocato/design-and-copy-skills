**Treat Rachel as the person who owns the risk of switching.** Her objections ("it's a hassle," "DocuVault's been fine") are about migration pain and the risk to her, not about features. If you spend the meeting proving DocuVault is bad, she'll defend it. If you show her that switching is manageable, she has less to defend.

**Before Thursday**
- **Talk to Rachel first.** A 15-minute call is enough. Ask what a bad switch would look like for her: data migration, downtime, retraining, contract lock-in. Then you're answering her real concerns in the room, and she isn't hearing them cold in front of her peers.
- **Get the ops managers to name specific, concrete pain with DocuVault.** Examples are time lost on a task, a missed renewal, or a workaround they use. "It's fine" loses to a specific cost and wins against a vague "we want something better."
- **Know who the CFO and legal counsel lean toward.** They're the swing votes. The CFO cares about total cost and risk. Legal cares about audit trails, compliance, and contract terms. Prepare one clear point for each.

**In the meeting**
1. **Open with the committee's problem, not your product.** Have the ops managers describe the pain in their own words. That keeps the case coming from inside Calderon and keeps you from looking like you're attacking Rachel's preferred option.
2. **Put the switching plan up front, not at the end.** Give a migration timeline, who does the work (ideally you), what happens to existing contracts and data, and a named implementation contact. Rachel's biggest objection is hassle, so make that the strongest part of your pitch.
3. **Acknowledge the renewal option out loud.** Say something like: "Renewing is a legitimate choice, so here's what staying costs versus switching." Compare the two on cost, risk, and what the ops team gets. That shows Rachel you aren't dodging her view, and it makes her position one option on the table.
4. **Give her ways to lower the risk.** Options include a phased rollout, a pilot with one region, parallel running during the transition, a termination clause, or price protection. Each one answers "what if it goes wrong?"
5. **Ask her for her expertise.** For example: "Rachel, what would you need to see in the contract and onboarding terms to be comfortable?" That moves her from blocker to evaluator, and her answer tells you what's left to resolve.

**Things to avoid**
- Don't call out her emails or her preference for DocuVault.
- Don't let the ops managers turn it into "procurement vs. operations." If it starts going that way, steer back to cost and risk.
- Don't read her silence as agreement. If she's quiet, ask directly near the end what questions or concerns she still has.

**Close**
Propose a concrete next step that doesn't require a full decision on the spot. Examples are a reference call with a similar customer, a scoped pilot, or a written migration plan by a set date. Rachel can agree to that without feeling she's signed off on the switch.
