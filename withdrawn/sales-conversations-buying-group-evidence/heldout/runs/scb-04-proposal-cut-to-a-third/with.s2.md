## What happened

1. **Four people agreed, but not on one problem.** Each one-to-one was a separate pitch, and each person said yes to their own version. The ops director cares about uptime and service, maintenance about faults, the CFO about cost, IT about integration and support load. When they compared notes, the only thing they could all sign was a smaller, reversible step. That's a pilot.
2. **Your questions measured liking, not commitment.** "Does this make sense?" and "do you like it?" get a polite yes. Nobody was asked what could stop this, who else needed to agree, or what would have to be true for 600 vehicles to be the right first step. A quiet influencer likely had a concern you never heard. The IT head and the CFO are the most likely, but that's a guess.
3. **The proposal went out before the gate was met.** You had felt problems, but I see no named signer, no agreed success test and no written buyer commitment (data, people, time). Without those, a 600-vehicle proposal gave the group nothing to agree to except your plan.
4. **The pilot is probably fear of choosing wrong.** Rolling out 600 vehicles is hard to undo, and a pilot costs the approvers nothing visible. That's a risk-management decision, not necessarily a rejection of the product.

## What to do differently

- After the one-to-ones, write **one problem statement every role would sign**, with the figure each role is held to beside it. Show each person what the others need. Then bring them together for a short session on the problem and the kind of fix, not on your product.
- Ask each person early: "Who could stop this, and why?" and "What would have to be true for you to sign off on all 600?" Assume a quiet influencer has an objection until you've heard it.
- **Find the real ally.** Don't pick the friendliest contact. Pick the one who pushes back, does real work for you (pulls a figure, names the reviewer) and can get the right people into one room.
- **Track progress by what the buyer did**, such as criteria written down, signer named or reviewer meeting booked. "They loved the demo" doesn't count.
- Propose the pilot yourself, with a success test, before they have to ask for one. A pilot they invent has no terms. One you design does.

## What to do with this deal now

Take the 200, but don't let it become an open-ended trial. Don't discount in reply to the pilot decision. If anything is traded, trade scope or term, and get something back.

1. **Learn why 200.** Ask the ops director, or whoever championed it: "What did the group need to see before going to 600?" and "Who raised the pilot, and what was the worry?" Their answer is your success test.
2. **Make it a real pilot, in writing, before you start:**
   - a success test in numbers Brightline helped choose `[to learn]`
   - dates with checkpoints, and a stop point if the first measure misses
   - named owners on both sides, and what each will do (IT access, depot access, data)
   - the price for the 200 and the pilot end date
3. **Choose the 200 deliberately.** Pick vehicles or depots where the problem is felt most and the result is easiest to measure, not the easiest to install.
4. **Settle the expansion terms now.** Put the decision date and the price for the other 400 in the pilot agreement, so the group isn't re-deciding the commercials later. Expansion is opt-in: nothing is bought without a written yes. If a straight-line scale-up of your $290k proposal for 200 vehicles is what you'd quote, confirm it, because your pricing may not be linear.
5. **Don't use pressure.** No "the 600 price expires" and no deadline you made up. If they stall after the pilot, use the stuck-buyer moves: one recommendation with its reason and a flip condition, and a small reversible next step.
