# Brightline postmortem

**Goal for what's left of this deal:** turn the 200-vehicle pilot into a written, dated path to the rest of the fleet.

## What I can't know from here

Here is what you told me: four separate one-to-ones, four yeses, a joint proposal for 600 vehicles (~290k over 3 years), and an approval for 200 "as a pilot" with no warning. I don't know who made the decision, what was said internally, or whether anyone had a reservation they kept from you. The causes below are likely, not confirmed. The `[to learn]` items at the end are how you'd confirm them.

## What probably happened

**1. You had four separate yeses, not one shared decision.** Each person agreed to a version of the product that fit their own job. Ops likes uptime, maintenance likes diagnostics, the CFO likes the numbers, and IT likes that it integrates. Nobody said "no", but nobody had to commit to the same problem or the same scope. When they compared notes, the group had never agreed on what problem 600 vehicles would solve. The easy compromise was to start smaller. This is the most common way a deal shrinks after good one-on-ones.

**2. "Love it, makes sense" is approval of an idea, not a commitment.** Those words cost the buyer nothing. The buyer actions that count are things like a success measure written down, a named signer, a reviewer's slot booked, or data pulled. Did any of the four do real work for you in that month? If not, you were hearing politeness, and you read it as progress.

**3. The proposal arrived before anyone had tested the full-fleet claim.** A 600-vehicle, 3-year commitment is a big risk to carry personally. Someone in that group (often the CFO or IT) probably felt that choosing wrong would cost them and a pilot wouldn't. A pilot lets them say yes to you without owning the downside.

**4. You never found out who could stop it.** If you didn't ask "who could block this, and what would their reason be?", then a quiet reservation stayed quiet. The person who killed the full scope may be someone you never spoke to, or someone you did who simply didn't say it.

**5. The proposal was joint, but the agreement wasn't.** Sending one document to four people who hadn't met around it let each of them read the part they cared about. It also let any one of them push back without it being a visible disagreement.

## What to do differently next time

- **After the one-on-ones, write one problem statement every role would sign.** Put each role's own number beside it, such as vehicle availability for ops, cost per repair for maintenance, and payback for the CFO. Show each person what the others need, then get them in one room to agree it before any proposal exists.
- **Ask early who could stop this and why.** Assume a quiet influencer has an objection until you've heard it.
- **Judge progress by what the buyer did.** "Criteria written down," "signer named" and "data pulled" are progress. "They loved the demo" isn't.
- **Test intent with a step that costs them something**, such as pulling fleet data, introducing the signer, or booking the finance review. If nobody will do one, the yeses are soft.
- **Find your ally by behaviour, not title.** The ally is the person who pushes back with questions, does real work for you, and can get the right people into one meeting.
- **Raise the pilot yourself, early.** If a phased start is a likely outcome, offer it on your terms: success test, dates, and the price and path to the full fleet. Don't wait for them to impose it.

## What to do with the deal now

**Don't just take the 200, and don't push for the rest yet.** The pilot is real revenue and a real way in, but a vague pilot usually stays a pilot. Take it, and shape it so expansion is something they've already agreed to.

Before you sign, try to get these in writing:

1. **A success test in numbers the buyer helped choose** (`[to learn]`: what do they track today for availability, repair cost or similar?).
2. **A named signer and decision date for the remaining ~400 vehicles**, tied to the success test.
3. **Dates and checkpoints**, with a stop point if the first measure fails.
4. **A named owner on each side** and what each will do.
5. **Price for the expansion, agreed now.** I can't tell you the right figure. Pro-rata on your proposal, 200 of 600 vehicles would be roughly a third of the ~290k, but check that against your own pricing. If you quoted volume pricing, say plainly what the 200 price is and what the full-fleet price would be. Don't discount the pilot to win goodwill. If anything is conceded, trade it for something, such as the expansion commitment or a reference.

**First conversation:** ask your strongest contact, "Where did this land after it went back to the team, and what would the pilot need to show for the rest of the fleet to make sense?" Then stop talking. The answer will tell you who decided, what the reservation was, and what the success test should be. Ask it plainly, not as a challenge, and don't re-argue the 600.

**Choose your pilot vehicles on purpose.** If you can influence which 200 are in scope, pick a set that will actually test what the doubters doubt, such as a mix of routes or depots, not only the easy ones.

**Don't use pressure.** No "the price goes up" deadlines, and no repeating what waiting costs them. They've already said they want to change, and more fear will make a wrong choice feel riskier.

**Next step:** a call with your ally, plus whoever signs, to set the success test and the expansion decision date. Name who, what and when, put it on both calendars, and confirm it in writing the same day.
