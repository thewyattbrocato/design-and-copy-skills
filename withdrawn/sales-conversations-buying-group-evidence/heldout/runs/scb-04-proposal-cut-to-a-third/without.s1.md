# Postmortem: Brightline Transit

I wasn't in the room, so some of this is inference. Check it against what you know, and the debrief below should confirm or correct it.

## What probably happened

**1. You collected opinions, not commitments.** "I love it" and "makes sense" cost the speaker nothing. Four people said them, but none of them said "I'll take this to the exec team and back it for 600," or "here's what would block it." A month of uniformly positive feedback should have been a warning. Real buying processes surface objections, and silence usually means they're being held back or saved for a meeting you're not in.

**2. Each stakeholder was liking a different thing.** Ops likes uptime and dispatch, maintenance likes diagnostics, the CFO likes ROI, and IT likes (or tolerates) the integration. Each yes was to their slice. Nobody had to reconcile them, and a joint proposal for 600 vehicles is where that reconciliation finally happens, without you present.

**3. The CFO and IT head were probably the ones who piloted it.** A 290k, 3-year commitment across the whole fleet is a risk to manage. "Prove it on a third of the fleet first" is the standard way finance and IT cut that risk. It's probably not a rejection. It also suggests you never found out what proof the CFO and IT needed, or what could go wrong for them: integration with existing systems, installation downtime, driver or union pushback, data security, or contract length.

**4. The proposal went out cold.** If the first time they saw the full scope, price, and term was in the written proposal, you lost the chance to hear "that's too big a step" while you could still shape it.

**5. You probably had no map of the actual decision.** Who signs, who can veto, whether there's a board or procurement step, and what the approval threshold is. Without that, the proposal went into a process you couldn't see.

## What to do differently

- **Treat unanimous enthusiasm as a yellow flag.** Ask each person directly: "What would make you hesitate on a fleet-wide rollout?" and "If this goes wrong, what does it look like?"
- **Get commitments, not compliments.** Ask for something small that costs them: an intro to the signer, access to data for a business case, or a date for a joint meeting.
- **Run a multi-stakeholder session before the proposal.** Put all four in one room (or call) and let the disagreements appear while you're present.
- **Map the decision.** Ask early: "Walk me through how a purchase this size gets approved here. Who else weighs in, and what do they typically ask?"
- **Pre-wire the proposal.** Nothing in it should be a surprise. Walk the CFO through the numbers and IT through the rollout plan before sending.
- **Offer a phased option yourself.** A proposal with "Phase 1: 200, Phase 2: 400, with price locked" lets you frame the pilot on your terms rather than receive it as a counter.
- **Write a mutual close plan** with dates, owners, and what each step requires.

## What to do with the deal now

Don't just take the 200 as written. A pilot approval is a counteroffer, and you still have leverage before you sign.

1. **Find out who made the call and why.** Ask your champion (or whoever you trust most there) for a debrief: "What drove the pilot decision, and what would need to be true for the rest to follow?" Try to talk to the CFO and IT head specifically.
2. **Turn the pilot into a staged contract, not a test.** Push for:
   - Written success criteria agreed up front (uptime, fuel or maintenance savings, install time, adoption).
   - A pre-agreed conversion path: if criteria are met, the remaining 400 vehicles proceed at the quoted price, with the price locked for a defined window.
   - A fixed timeline (for example, review at 90 or 120 days) so it doesn't drift.
3. **Protect your pricing.** Don't give a big discount on the 200 to "win" the pilot. If they want pilot pricing, trade it for the conversion commitment.
4. **Choose the 200 deliberately.** Propose a representative mix (routes, depots, vehicle ages) that will show the value clearly, rather than letting them pick the easiest or hardest vehicles.
5. **Plan the pilot as an expansion campaign.** Schedule checkpoints with all four stakeholders, share results in the CFO's terms, and make sure the person who'd sign for the other 400 sees the results.
6. **Know your walk-away.** If the pilot is unpaid, open-ended, or has no conversion terms, push back. Taking 200 is fine. Taking 200 with no path to the rest is a smaller deal that may never grow.
