## What probably happened

I wasn't in the room, so these are the likeliest causes, not confirmed facts.

1. **You had four separate yeses, not one shared decision.** You sold each person a version tuned to their role. Each said yes alone. Then they compared notes, and the group settled on the amount of risk they could all accept. For a group that was 200 vehicles as a pilot.
2. **"Love it, makes sense" isn't a commitment.** None of the four yeses came with something the person did: a figure pulled, a criterion written down, a named signer, a meeting they set up. Nothing in your account suggests anyone was asked to do any of that. Without it, enthusiasm is polite agreement.
3. **Nobody agreed what proof was needed for 600.** The pilot is the group's answer to a question you never asked them. Someone, probably finance or IT, had a worry that never came up in your one-to-ones. People often don't share that kind of worry with the seller when everything seems to be going well.
4. **The joint proposal was your first group conversation.** The first time all four were in one conversation about the deal was when they read your document. By then the disagreements were already set.

You also don't know who actually signed. That is the gap to close first.

## What to do differently next time

- **After the one-to-ones, write one problem statement** that each role would sign, with the figure each is held to beside it (ops: availability, maintenance: [to learn], CFO: [to learn], IT: [to learn]). Show every person what the others need.
- **Convene the four before you write a proposal.** Ask each of them beforehand if you can raise the differences you've heard. Keep the session on the problem and the kind of fix, not your product. End with an agreed statement and an owner and date for each open item.
- **Ask early who could stop this and why.** Assume the quiet one has an objection until you've heard it.
- **Measure progress by what the buyer did.** "Four people said yes" doesn't count. "Criteria written down, signer named, reviewer slot booked" does. If you had asked "how do I know this is real?" after month one, the gap would have shown.
- **Find the person who drives change.** The test is whether they do real work you ask for, such as pulling a figure or getting the others into one room. Among your four, you don't seem to know who that was.
- **Raise the pilot yourself.** If a phased rollout is a realistic outcome, put it in the proposal with success criteria and a priced expansion. Then the group isn't improvising one.

## What to do with the deal now

Take the 200, but don't treat it as a smaller version of the same deal. As it stands, the pilot probably has no agreed success test, so a good result may still lead to "let's keep watching".

1. **Debrief your closest contact this week.** Ask: "Who decided on 200, and what would they need to see to go to 600?" You're looking for the real objection and the real signer.
2. **Get a written expansion path before or at kickoff.** It needs:
   - success measures in numbers they helped choose
   - checkpoint dates
   - a named owner on each side
   - a decision date for the rest of the fleet
   - the per-vehicle price for the other 400, held for a stated period
3. **Get the four in one room to agree the success test.** This is the group conversation you skipped. It's also where the hidden worry surfaces.
4. **Choose the 200 so the result is credible.** Aim for a representative mix of depots or routes, not the easiest ones, so the result can't be dismissed as unrepresentative.
5. **Don't discount to win the 400 now, and don't make the pilot convert automatically.** Expansion should be opt-in, with the price and decision date written down. A held price is a term, not a concession, and it's fair.

If your contact can't name a signer or a success test, treat the 400 as unqualified. Run the 200 well, but don't forecast the rest.
