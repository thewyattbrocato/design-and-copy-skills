## What probably happened

I wasn't in the room, so this is inference from the pattern. It looks like a **consensus-by-interview** failure.

1. **Four separate yeses don't add up to a decision.** You heard "makes sense" from each person alone, and none of them had to defend it to the others or commit anything. Agreeing is free in a one-to-one. The real decision happened later, in a room you weren't in, and probably came down to the CFO's risk question: "Why commit $290k over 3 years across 600 buses before we've seen it work on ours?" A pilot is the standard answer to that question.

2. **You collected enthusiasm but not commitments.** "Love it" is a feeling. A commitment is a named budget, a decision date, success criteria, or an agreed next step. If none of the four ever said "here's what we'd need to see to roll this out fleet-wide," they hadn't thought it through, and the approval process did that thinking for them.

3. **Nobody owned the decision, and you didn't find out who did.** Four stakeholders with no clear economic buyer usually means a committee. Committees default to the lowest-regret option, which is a smaller scope with an exit.

4. **The proposal led with the full 600-vehicle commitment.** You never tested whether they'd be comfortable with that scope, so the smaller option was theirs to invent.

5. **You got no early warning because you never asked for it.** People rarely volunteer "this will get cut down." Questions like "What would make this hard to approve?" or "What would the CFO push back on?" surface it. Without them, you only hear agreement.

## What to do differently next time

- **Get everyone in one meeting before the proposal.** Hold a joint session where they discuss it with each other. Disagreement shows up there, while you can still address it.
- **Ask who decides and how.** For example: "Walk me through what happens after we submit. Who signs, and what do they look at?" Then talk to that person directly, especially if it's the CFO.
- **Ask for the objection.** "If this came back reduced or delayed, what's the most likely reason?" Ask every stakeholder.
- **Build a mutual action plan** with dates, owners, and success criteria. Prospects who won't engage with one aren't as committed as they sound.
- **Offer the phased option yourself.** Put a pilot-to-rollout structure in the proposal, with pricing and conversion terms you control. Then the pilot is your framing, not their counter.
- **Treat unanimous warmth as a yellow flag.** Real buying processes have friction. A month of pure agreement usually means you haven't hit the hard questions yet.

## What to do with this deal now

Don't just take the 200. Use the pilot approval to shape what comes next.

1. **Find out what "pilot" means.** Is it a 3-year contract on 200 vehicles, or a short trial? What are the price and term? Is there any conversion path to the other 400?
2. **Get a debrief with the real decision-maker.** Ask something like: "What drove the decision to start with 200, and what would you need to see to extend to the full fleet?" Frame it as wanting to make the pilot succeed. The answer is your roadmap.
3. **Negotiate the expansion into the contract.** Ask for:
   - Written success criteria, such as fuel savings, maintenance cost reduction, or downtime
   - A pre-agreed evaluation date
   - Price protection on the remaining 400 vehicles for 9–12 months
   - A named executive sponsor
4. **Don't give up discount without getting something.** If the 200-vehicle price is lower than your proposal's per-unit rate, trade the concession for the expansion terms above. If they want volume pricing, they should commit to volume.
5. **Choose the 200 vehicles deliberately.** Aim for a representative mix, including routes and vehicle types where the value is easiest to measure. Make sure the data the CFO cares about is tracked from day one.
6. **Set up a regular check-in with all four stakeholders**, plus the CFO, with a short results report. This is also where you build the joint consensus you lacked the first time.
