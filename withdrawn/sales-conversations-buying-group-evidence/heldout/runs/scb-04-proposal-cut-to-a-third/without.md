# Postmortem: Brightline Transit

I only have your summary, so the causes below are inferences. A debrief with Brightline (see the end) would confirm them.

## What probably happened

**1. You collected four separate yeses, and none of them was a decision.**
"Love it, makes sense" is a statement about the product. It isn't a commitment to spend $290k. Each person was agreeing in a one-to-one, where agreeing is free. The real decision was made later, in a room you weren't in, by people weighing risk, budget and operational disruption against each other.

**2. Nobody owned the 600-vehicle decision.**
Ops, maintenance, finance and IT each had a stake, but you never identified who held the budget and could say "all 600." In deals like this, the CFO or a steering group usually decides, and their default for a big 3-year commitment from a new vendor is to cut the scope and run a pilot. That is the standard way a committee lowers its risk. The pilot was probably the outcome the group could agree on, whatever each person told you privately.

**3. Nobody warned you because you never made it safe or necessary to.**
Friendly stakeholders avoid delivering bad news. You also didn't seem to ask questions that surface objections, such as "What would make you hesitate to roll out to all 600 at once?" or "Who else needs to be convinced, and what will they worry about?" Without those questions, the friendly feedback told you little.

**4. The proposal went out before the room was aligned.**
A joint proposal sent to four people who had never discussed it together lets the first real conversation about it happen without you. You should have heard the objections before the proposal existed.

**5. There was no mutual plan.**
It sounds like there were no agreed steps with dates, such as security review, procurement, a pilot discussion or a decision meeting. Without them you couldn't see the process, so the outcome surprised you.

## What to do differently next time

- **Map the buying group in the first two weeks.** Ask: "Who signs? Who can veto? How was the last fleet-wide purchase decided?" Identify the economic buyer and meet them early.
- **Treat enthusiasm as a signal to probe, not a win.** Ask each person, "If this went to the exec team tomorrow, what's the first objection?" and "What would make you recommend a smaller start?"
- **Get the stakeholders into one room before the proposal.** Ideally you'd run a joint session where they surface disagreements while you're present.
- **Offer the phased option yourself.** If you propose the structure (for example 200 now, with pre-priced expansion to 600 on defined results), you keep control of the terms. Otherwise they impose a pilot with no expansion path.
- **Write a mutual action plan** covering the decision date, who is involved, and what has to be true for a yes.
- **Qualify on commitment.** Ask for a small next step, such as data access, a technical validation or an intro to the CFO. Willingness to spend effort tells you more than praise does.

## What to do with the deal now

Don't just take the 200. Take it, but renegotiate its terms so it can become 600.

1. **Run a debrief call** with whoever you trust most there, and ask who decided, what the concerns were, and what would have to be true to go to 600. Do this before you sign anything.
2. **Define pilot success criteria in writing.** Agree on specific, measurable outcomes (for example, fuel use, maintenance cost, downtime or safety events) and a review date. Otherwise "pilot" is open-ended.
3. **Pre-price the expansion.** Lock the 600-vehicle per-unit price, or an option on the remaining 400, valid for a set window after the pilot review. Don't let the price of the extra 400 reset to a new negotiation.
4. **Protect your price.** Don't discount deeply just because it's smaller. If you give something, trade it for something such as a committed expansion decision date, a reference or case study, or a 3-year term on the 200.
5. **Make the 200 the best possible test.** Choose vehicles and routes that are representative but likely to show clear results, and plan onboarding carefully. Pilots often fail through poor rollout rather than poor product.
6. **Get the executive sponsor into the pilot.** Set up a mid-pilot check-in with the CFO or ops director, so the expansion decision is built on results they've seen.
7. **Check your own economics.** Pro rata, 200 vehicles is roughly $97k over 3 years, but confirm that the pricing, hardware and support costs still make sense at that volume.

If they refuse any expansion terms, that is useful information too. It tells you the pilot is a real evaluation and not a formality, and you can plan around that.
