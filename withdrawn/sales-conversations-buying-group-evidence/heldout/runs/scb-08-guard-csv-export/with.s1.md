**Answer yes, plainly, then ask what the CSV is for.** A CSV question is usually a stand-in for a workflow, such as feeding a finance spreadsheet, a BI tool, or a report for their boss. The yes is only the start of the answer.

**Say something like:**

> "Yes. Any report, one click, top right. [Show it if you're on screen. It takes ten seconds.] Out of curiosity, what would you do with the file? Which report, who gets it, and how often?"

Then stop talking and let them answer. Those three questions give you:
- **Which report:** what they care about most.
- **Who gets it:** the stakeholders you haven't met yet.
- **How often:** whether this is a one-off export or a recurring job. A recurring job might need scheduled delivery or an integration, and that is a different feature.

**Why this works better than a longer answer:**
- Don't oversell it. A feature list in reply to a yes/no question makes a small point sound like a worry.
- If the answer shows a gap, say so on the call. For example, if they need it emailed every Monday and you only have manual export, tell them that and say what you'd do instead. Don't let "yes, CSV" imply more than it covers.
- If they only wanted to confirm the basics, you've lost nothing. Move on to their next question.

**Before the follow-up, check two things:**
1. Whether "any report" is literally true. Check for row limits, reports that export differently, and anything that breaks in Excel, such as date formats or encoding. If there are exceptions, you'll want to know before they ask.
2. Whether you know which report they'd export first. If you can open that one in the demo, the yes lands harder than a generic claim.

I don't know their stage. If the call has no named signer or sized problem yet, the CSV answer shouldn't lead into a demo or trial offer. Use it as one more discovery thread.
