**Answer it in one sentence, then ask what the export is for.**

> "Yes. Any report exports to CSV with one click, top right. What would you be doing with the data once it's out?"

Keep the first part short. They've already asked once, so a second ask probably means one of two things:
- They didn't register the first answer.
- The real question is something else, such as getting data into Excel, a BI tool, or something finance or audit needs. CSV is just how they phrased it.

Your follow-up question finds out which. Their answer is what you want to hear. It tells you the workflow the export serves, who consumes it, and how often. Those are the buyer's own numbers: reports per week, people involved, and the manual step this replaces. Write them down as `[to learn]` until they say them.

**If they ask again on the follow-up**, don't repeat the pitch. Show it. If you're on a screen, click the button on a report that looks like theirs, and open the file. Seeing it work settles it faster than saying "yes" a second time.

**If the answer turns out to be more than CSV**, such as a scheduled export, an API, a specific format, or a direct connector, say plainly what you do and don't do today. Don't stretch "one click" to cover something it doesn't. If there's a gap, say what you'd do about it.

**Don't:**
- Turn a yes/no feature question into a feature tour.
- Offer a trial or a demo just because they asked. They haven't yet shown a problem they feel or a route to a signer.

Close the follow-up the way you'd close any call. Name one next step with an owner and a date, and confirm it in writing the same day.
