**Short answer: (b), with a cheap version of (a) running alongside. Don't sink two weeks into a full custom proposal yet.**

**Why**

Six weeks of silent security review on a $210k data platform deal is a real risk signal, not noise. Security review is usually a gate, not a formality. A data platform touches their data, so it will get scrutiny. The only information you have saying "they're just slow" comes from Elena, and she's in marketing. She doesn't own that process and likely can't see into it. Her reassurance is an opinion, not evidence.

A custom proposal is the expensive thing to be wrong about. If security blocks you, or never really started, you've burned two weeks and likely a chunk of your AE's pipeline credibility on a deal that was never gated by the proposal.

**What to do**

1. **Find out the actual state of the review, this week.** Ask Elena for a direct intro to the security reviewer, framed as helping them: "Happy to get on a 30-min call to answer questions and cut their time." Then ask specific questions:
   - Who owns the review, and what's the status (not started, questionnaire out, pen test, legal/DPA)?
   - Is anything outstanding from us? (Often it's a questionnaire or SOC 2 report sitting in someone's inbox.)
   - What's the typical turnaround, and what happens after approval?
2. **Proactively send the security package**: SOC 2 / ISO report, completed standard questionnaire (CAIQ/SIG), architecture and data-flow diagram, subprocessor list, DPA, pen test summary. Offer to fill in their own form.
3. **Ask about the buying process**, not the product: Who signs? Is there a budget approved for $210k? Is there a procurement or legal step? What's the target go-live date and what's driving it?
4. **Meanwhile, do a lightweight proposal**: a 2-3 page scoped summary, pricing, and a mutual close plan with dates. That's maybe a day or two of work, and it keeps momentum without betting two weeks.
5. **Set a trigger for the full proposal**: security contact engaged and a timeline confirmed, plus economic buyer identified. Then build it.

If Elena resists the intro to security, that's informative in itself.

**Champion vs. friendly**

Taking every call and sharing gossip is access, not advocacy. A real champion has **power or influence, and acts for you when you're not in the room**. Test it with asks that cost her something:

- **Introduces you to the economic buyer and security** (not just offers to "pass along info").
- **Can describe the buying process specifically**: steps, owners, dates, budget source. Vague answers like "they're just slow" are a flag.
- **Has a personal stake** in the outcome: a project or goal of hers depends on this. Can she articulate why it matters to her and to Kintaro in numbers?
- **Shares bad news and coaches you**: tells you who's skeptical, what competitors are in play, what could kill it. Friendly people only give good news.
- **Takes action**: pushes the review internally, books meetings, forwards your materials, sells for you.
- **Has credibility internally**: ask others, or check whether things she says will happen actually happen.

Score her on those. If she says yes to the intro and the mutual close plan within a week and follows through, she's a champion. If she deflects, stays vague, or can't get you to security or the budget owner, she's a friendly contact, and you should multi-thread (find the data/IT owner who'd actually use the platform, the security lead, the economic buyer) rather than rely on her.

One more thing worth noting: a marketing VP sponsoring a data platform is plausible, but check who actually owns the budget and who the technical stakeholders are. If data or IT leadership hasn't been in a single call, that's a gap on its own.

**Bottom line for your AE**: the question isn't whether Elena is nice, it's whether anyone with authority has committed to a date. Get that answer first, send the lightweight proposal now, and save the full build for when the deal has earned it.
