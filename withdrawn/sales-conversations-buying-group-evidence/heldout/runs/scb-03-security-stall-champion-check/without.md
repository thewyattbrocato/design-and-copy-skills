**Why not build the proposal now**

Six weeks of security review on a $210k data platform deal is a signal, not noise. Your platform will probably touch their customer or transaction data, so security is a gate, not a formality. If the review fails or stalls, two weeks of proposal work is wasted. The proposal also doesn't unblock anything, because nobody at Kintaro is waiting on it.

"They're just slow" is the one explanation that needs no action from you. Elena is VP of marketing, so she isn't on the security team and probably can't see inside their review. She's relaying her guess, not facts.

**What to do instead (this week)**

1. **Find out the actual state of the review.** Ask Elena to introduce you to the security reviewer, or ask her for the reviewer's name. Then ask them directly: What's the process? What have you received from us? What's outstanding? Who else signs off? What would a clean outcome look like, and by when?
2. **Check whether the ball is in your court.** Often "reviewing" means a questionnaire, SOC 2 report, pen-test summary, or DPA is waiting on the vendor. Audit what you've sent and what they've asked for.
3. **Map the rest of the buying process.** Who owns budget? Does legal, procurement, or IT sign off? Is there a date the deal has to close by, and why?
4. **Offer a lightweight proposal.** Send a 2–3 page scoped summary with pricing and timeline. Hold the full custom build until you have a security timeline and a named economic buyer. If Elena pushes for the full version, ask her what decision it feeds and when.

**Is Elena a champion or just friendly?**

Taking every call and sharing internal gossip shows she likes you. It doesn't show she'll do anything for you. A real champion does these things:

- **Has power or influence.** They can explain how decisions get made and who else is involved. They have a track record of getting things done internally.
- **Has a stake in the outcome.** They have a business problem your product solves and can state it in their own words, with a number attached.
- **Takes action on your behalf.** They make intros to the security lead, the budget owner, and the executive sponsor. They push back internally when things stall.
- **Shares bad news.** They tell you about competitors, objections, and risks, not just reassurance.
- **Coaches you.** They help you prepare for the people who aren't on your side.

**Quick tests, all low-risk:**

- Ask for an intro to the security reviewer and the budget owner. If she hesitates or deflects, she's friendly, not a champion.
- Ask, "What would make this deal die?" A champion gives you a real answer. A friendly contact says "nothing, we're good."
- Ask her to commit to a specific step, like getting the security reviewer on a call this week. Watch whether it happens.
- Ask who else cares about this and who might oppose it. If she can't name anyone, she may not have the visibility to be your champion.

If she fails these tests, you don't lose her. You just stop treating her as your path to close and find a second route in, such as the security team, IT or data leadership, or a budget holder.

**Talking to your AE:** frame it as protecting their time, not doubting Elena. Something like: "I'd rather spend those two weeks on a proposal we know has a path to signature. Let's get the security timeline and a second contact this week, then build the full version." That gives them a concrete plan and a short deadline rather than a debate over whether Elena is a champion.
