**What I know and what I don't**
- You've told me: the deal is $210k ARR, security has been "reviewing" for six weeks, Elena is the VP of marketing, and she says they're slow. She takes every call and shares internal news.
- I don't know:
  - Whether security has sent you anything, such as a questionnaire, a SOC 2 request or a DPA.
  - Who signs and whether budget is approved.
  - Whether Elena has ever been in a room with security or the signer.
  - How big the problem is in Kintaro's own numbers: `[to learn]`.

**Why not build the proposal now**
- Security is the likeliest place for the deal to die, and you haven't tested it. Six weeks with no visible progress is not normal. Active reviews generate requests. If you've had none, the review may not have started.
- "They're just slow" is Elena's guess, not a fact. She's a marketing VP describing another department's process.
- A proposal is expensive to build and cheap to ignore. You'd put two weeks into a document with no confirmed signer, no confirmed route through security, and no commitment from the buyer.

**What to do this week**
1. Ask Elena for specifics: "Who on security owns this? What stage is it at? What have they asked for that we haven't delivered?" If she can't answer, that's your first finding.
2. Ask her for a 30-minute call with the security reviewer. Offer to bring your security lead and your documents (SOC 2, architecture, DPA). Send those documents now, whether or not they've been requested.
3. Ask who signs a $210k data platform purchase and what the approval steps are. That is often not marketing alone, and it may involve IT, procurement or the CIO.
4. Tell the AE to write a one-page outline: scope, price range, and what Kintaro is buying. It's enough to keep momentum and to test whether Elena can take it upward.

**Set the end date now.** For example: by next Wednesday you have either a named security contact and a review timeline, or you know you don't. Agree the owner and date with the AE today.

**What would change my answer:** If Elena names the signer, confirms budget, and security gives a completion date, then build the proposal. Scope it to what the signer needs to see.

**How to tell a champion from a friendly contact**
A friendly contact gives you information. A champion spends their own influence on your behalf. Test with small asks that cost her something:

| Test | Champion | Friendly |
|---|---|---|
| Intro to security or the signer | Arranges it, or says exactly why not | Deflects ("they're slow, don't worry") |
| Bad news | Tells you what's blocking the deal | Only reassures |
| Decision process | Describes it in detail: names, steps, dates | Vague |
| Her own stake | Has a problem she's personally measured on and can state in numbers | Likes the product |
| Action between calls | Does things: forwards documents, books meetings, briefs others | Takes your calls |
| Access to power | Has the signer's ear, or is the signer | Can't say |

Elena's pattern so far fits friendly, though it isn't proof. Taking every call and sharing news is what a friendly contact does. Nothing you've described shows her acting on your behalf. Her answer to the intro request will tell you which she is. If she brings in security within a week, she's a real champion. If she keeps saying "don't worry," treat her as a friendly contact and find another route in, such as the security team directly or a second stakeholder.

**Two things to check**
- Ask your AE what they've actually seen Elena do between calls.
- Confirm whether your own security documents have been sent. If they haven't, the six weeks may partly be on you.
