**Use B.**

**Why it works:** Council members hear dozens of speakers, and most open with a name and an agenda. B gets attention in the first sentence because it's a specific child on a specific street. It also makes the ask concrete before you've said anything abstract. And "She's fine" is a good beat: it lets the room breathe and keeps the story from sounding like you're manipulating them.

**Why not the others:**
- **A** spends your first 10 to 15 seconds on housekeeping. The council has your name on the sign-in sheet and the agenda. "Walk you through" also promises a long presentation, and you only have three minutes. Introduce yourself *after* the hook.
- **C** is true but generic. Any advocate in any city could say it, so it's easy to tune out. The claims also invite "but does that apply here?" before you've earned their attention. Save the evidence for the middle.

**A suggested shape for the three minutes:**
1. **B** as your opener.
2. Quick intro: "I'm Dana Ortega with Cedar Hill Neighbors for Safe Streets, and I'm asking you to approve a protected bike lane on Alder between 4th and 12th."
3. One or two facts, such as C's evidence, ideally with local crash data if you have it.
4. Repeat the ask at the end, with a specific action: "Please vote yes on Tuesday."

**One check:** make sure the incident is accurate and that the family is comfortable with you sharing it, if you know them. If any detail is shaky, such as the van, the month, or the route to school, fix it before you say it. A council member who can poke a hole in your opening will use it to dismiss the rest.
