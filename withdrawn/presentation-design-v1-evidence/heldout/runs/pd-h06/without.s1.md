Use **B**.

**Why B works:**
- **It makes the problem concrete.** A specific child on a specific street is something council members can picture. Three minutes is short, and a story gets attention faster than a statistic.
- **It ties the story to the ask.** The last line says the street she was crossing is the one you want changed tonight, so the story leads straight into your request.
- **The "She's fine" is a good choice.** It keeps the story from sounding like manipulation and lets you stay calm. The stakes are clear without you pushing for sympathy.
- **It sets you apart.** Other speakers will probably open with their name and organization. A story at the start stands out.

**Why not the others:**
- **A** is a polite, forgettable opening. It spends your first 15 seconds on introductions and a "walk you through" preview. Council members get that kind of opening all the time, and your name and affiliation are already on the sign-in sheet or the clerk's announcement.
- **C** is a generic claim with no person or place in it. It's easy to nod at and forget. It also makes claims about injuries and business foot traffic that someone could challenge, and you'd have to back them up in a very short time.

**Two practical notes:**
1. **Introduce yourself right after the hook.** Something like "I'm Dana Ortega with Cedar Hill Neighbors for Safe Streets, and I'm asking you to approve a protected bike lane on Alder between 4th and 12th." That gets A's information in after you've got their attention.
2. **Check the facts on the story.** Make sure the details are accurate (the October date, the delivery van, the school route), and that the family is fine with you sharing it, or that it's from a public report. If anyone can verify it, the story is stronger.

You can also use C's evidence later in the talk, after the story, as support for the ask.
