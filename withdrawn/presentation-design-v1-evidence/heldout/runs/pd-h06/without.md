Use **B**.

**Why B works:**
- **It puts a face on the problem.** Council members hear dozens of speakers. A specific child on a specific street is what they'll remember on Wednesday, while "a proposal for a bike lane" blurs into the rest.
- **It makes the ask clear before you've asked.** The last line ties the story directly to tonight's decision, so the council knows what you want from the first 20 seconds.
- **"She's fine" is a smart choice.** It keeps the story from feeling manipulative and lets you make the point calmly: this was a near miss, and you're asking them to act before there's a worse outcome.

**Why not the others:**
- **A** spends your first 15 seconds on introductions and a preview. With only 3 minutes, that's wasted attention. It also sounds like every other speaker. Your name and group can go in a one-line mention right after the hook, or on the sign-in sheet.
- **C** is generic and abstract. Anyone could say it about any street, and it opens with a claim they've probably heard before. Use that evidence in the middle of your talk, where it backs up the story. It shouldn't be the opener.

**Suggested structure after B:**
1. Hook (B), about 15 seconds.
2. "I'm Dana Ortega with Cedar Hill Neighbors for Safe Streets, and I'm asking you to approve a protected bike lane on Alder between 4th and 12th." Say your ask plainly.
3. Two or three facts: the evidence from other cities (your C material), and any local crash data.
4. Repeat the ask at the end.

One thing to check: make sure the incident is accurate and, if you can, that the family is OK with you sharing it. If it's from a news report or crash data, you can say so. If it's something you know personally, a quick okay from the parent will keep the story from becoming a distraction.
