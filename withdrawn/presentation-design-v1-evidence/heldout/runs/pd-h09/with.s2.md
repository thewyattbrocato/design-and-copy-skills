# How to decide: slide, spoken, or handout

Ask three questions about each fact, in this order.

1. **Does the room need to see it to follow or remember the claim?** If yes, it goes on a slide. That usually means one number, one picture, or one claim.
2. **Is it explanation, context, or a story?** If yes, say it out loud. A slide can't carry the "why" as well as you can.
3. **Will someone want to look it up, check it, or act on it later?** If yes, it goes in the handout. That covers tables, lists, definitions, and anything you'd never read aloud.

Each fact gets one home. If it's on the slide, don't also say it word for word, and don't repeat it in the handout.

## Applied to your audit

I'm assuming you have about 10 minutes and want the plant managers to **approve three changes**. That gives roughly five content slides. Adjust if your slot is different.

**Takeaway:** *Most of our near-misses come from two spots and one time window, and three cheap changes address them.*

| Your material | Home | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The takeaway and the ask (approve the three changes) | Slide | It's the point of the talk, so it should stay on screen. |
| 38 near-misses, 23 at dock 6 | Slide (one bar or one big number) | The concentration is the finding, and the room needs to see it. |
| 9 at the freezer door | Slide (same chart) | It's one bar in the same picture. |
| 6 scattered | Chart label only | It matters mainly because it shows the problem is concentrated. |
| 2–4 pm peak | Slide (one claim or simple time chart) | It sets up the staggered-handover fix. |
| The three recommendations | Slide (one line per location) | This is what you're asking them to approve. |
| Why 2–4 pm: handover overlaps with peak inbound trucks | Say it | It's a cause-and-effect explanation. |
| What a dock 6 near-miss looks like | Say it | A concrete story makes the number land. |
| "Reviewed 6 weeks of logs and footage" | Say it, one sentence | It gives you credibility without needing a slide. |
| How you defined a near-miss | One spoken sentence, full method in the handout | They'll ask, so have a short answer ready. |
| Full incident log | Handout | It's a reference table. |
| List of camera angles reviewed | Handout | Nobody needs it live. |
| Counting method in detail | Handout, plus a backup slide after the end | Keep it ready in case someone challenges the count. |

## Resulting deck (about 5 slides)

1. **"Three changes will cut our forklift near-misses where they cluster"**: the takeaway and the ask. Say the 6-week review and the 38 total out loud.
2. **"61% of near-misses happen at one crossing: dock 6"**: a chart of 23 / 9 / 6. Compute the percentage yourself: 23 of 38 is about 60%.
3. **"Most happen between 2 and 4 pm"**: one visual. Explain the handover and inbound-truck overlap by voice.
4. **"Each hotspot has a targeted fix"**: dock 6 gets a one-way rule and stop line, the freezer door gets mirrors, and handover gets staggered.
5. **"Decision needed: approve the three changes"**: add an owner and a start date. Leave this up during questions.

Put the log, the camera list, and the counting method in a one- or two-page handout, and hand it out after the talk. Otherwise people will read it while you're speaking.

## Quick test

For any item, ask whether the room would be lost without it in front of them. If not, it doesn't belong on a slide. Then ask whether anyone will need it after you stop talking. If so, it belongs in the handout.
