The test is to ask three questions about each item, in this order.

1. **Does the room need to see it to follow the argument?** If so, it goes on a slide. That means a number, a comparison, a picture or the decision itself.
2. **Is it reasoning, context or a story?** Say it out loud. The slide shows the "what", and your voice supplies the "why" and "how".
3. **Would someone look it up or check it later, and you won't mention it?** Put it in the handout. Anything you'd skip during the talk shouldn't be on screen.

Here is how that sorts your material, assuming a 10-minute slot (about 5 content slides).

**On slides**

| Slide | Claim title | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Three changes would remove most forklift near-misses" | The three recommendations and the decision you want |
| 2 | "23 of 38 near-misses happened at one crossing: dock 6" | Bar chart: dock 6 (23), freezer door (9), other (6) |
| 3 | "Most happen between 2 and 4 pm, when handover meets peak inbound trucks" | Near-misses by hour, with the overlap shaded |
| 4 | "Fix the two hot spots and the overlap" | Dock 6: one-way rule and stop line. Freezer door: mirrors. Handover: staggered. |
| 5 | Ask: who approves, who owns each fix, by when | Owners and dates |

Slides 1 and 5 can be one slide if you want a shorter deck. Slide 3 needs an actual count for "most" in the 2–4 pm window. I don't have that number, so don't write "most" on the slide without it.

**Say out loud**
- The scope: six weeks of logs and footage, 38 near-misses. Say it in one sentence at the start, not on a slide.
- Your definition of a near-miss, in one sentence. The full method goes in the handout.
- Why 2–4 pm is risky: two shifts overlapping while trucks peak. This is the explanation the chart can't give.
- Why these fixes fit these spots. If one footage clip really stuck with you, a 20-second description will land better than any bullet.
- The 6 scattered incidents. They're just the "other" bar, so mention them in passing.

**In the handout**
- The full incident log.
- The list of camera angles you reviewed.
- The full counting method.
- The recommendations with a little more detail, if you want them to leave with something.

**Backup slide, not in the main deck**
Plant managers will likely ask "how did you decide what counted?" or "did you miss incidents the cameras don't cover?" Park a one-slide answer to each after your last slide. If nobody asks, you never show them.

Two things are easy to get wrong here:
- **Don't put the method or the log on a slide** because it feels like proof. The 23/9/6 split and the time pattern carry the argument. The method answers "can I trust this?", which is a handout and Q&A question.
- **Don't repeat things across the three places.** Each fact should live in one. The slide has the count, the handout has the log behind it, and you say why it matters.
