Use one question for each piece: **would the room lose the thread without it?** Then **would someone need to look it up later?** Whatever is left is what you say out loud.

**Slide: what the room must see to follow and decide.** Put only the claim, the evidence that carries it, and the ask.
- Headline: 38 near-misses in 6 weeks, and 23 of them (60%) are at dock 6.
- One chart of counts by location, with dock 6 visually dominant. A chart is better than a list here because the point is concentration.
- Time pattern: "Most occur 2–4 pm, when second-shift handover overlaps the inbound truck peak."
- Recommendations as a three-row table: location or pattern, fix, decision needed. Keep each cell short.
- One line defining "near-miss," because the numbers can't be read without it. Put it in small type under the chart.

**Say out loud: the reasoning and the judgment calls.** Slides can't hold these, and they're where managers will push back.
- Why the 2–4 pm overlap is a plausible cause, and that you're inferring it, not proving it. Say "consistent with" rather than "caused by."
- Why the 6 scattered events still matter, and why you didn't group them with dock 6.
- Why these three fixes and not others, and what each one costs in disruption.
- Limits of camera review: what angles you couldn't see, and whether any events may have gone unrecorded.
- One concrete clip or incident description. Managers remember a specific moment far better than a percentage.

**Handout: reference material they'll want to check or revisit.** Keep it out of the room so it doesn't compete with you.
- The full 38-row incident log, with date, time, location, and description.
- The camera angle list and review dates.
- Your near-miss counting method in full, including edge cases and how you resolved them.
- A breakdown by location and by hour, including the 6 scattered events, so anyone can recompute your figures.
- Data sources, review period, and limitations.

A quick test for anything you're unsure about: if it's a number someone might challenge, it belongs on the handout with the method behind it, and you state the headline version out loud. If it's a judgment, say it. If it's a lookup, hand it out.

One check before you finalize: confirm the 2–4 pm figure against the log's timestamps, not your memory of it. If "most" means a specific count, put the count on the slide. Managers will ask.
