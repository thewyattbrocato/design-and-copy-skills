# Slide, spoken, or handout

Ask three questions about each piece of content, in this order:

1. **Does the audience need to see it to get it?** This applies to shapes, comparisons, and a few key numbers. Put it on a slide.
2. **Is it the reasoning, story, or "so what"?** Say it out loud. Text on a slide competes with your voice, and people read instead of listening.
3. **Will someone want to look it up, check it, or act on it later?** Put it in the handout.

Something can pass more than one test. Put the headline on the slide and the detail in the handout.

## Applied to your audit

**On slides** (the things to see):
- **The headline:** "38 near-misses in 6 weeks."
- **The location split:** 23 at dock 6 (about 60%), 9 at the freezer door (about 24%), 6 scattered (about 16%). A single bar chart works well, and the dock 6 bar should be the one that stands out.
- **The time pattern:** a chart of near-misses by hour, with 2–4 pm visibly spiking. This beats putting the sentence on a slide, because the spike is the evidence.
- **The recommendations:** three short lines, each matched to a finding.
  - Dock 6 gets a one-way rule and a stop line.
  - The freezer door gets mirrors.
  - The 2–4 pm window gets a staggered handover.
- **Optionally:** a still from the footage of the dock 6 crossing. Plant managers will react to a real image more than to a number.

**Said out loud** (the reasoning):
- **Why 2–4 pm:** the second shift handover overlaps with the peak of inbound trucks. The chart shows the spike, and you explain the cause.
- **One concrete near-miss at dock 6:** a short story that makes the number real.
- **Why these fixes:** the tradeoffs, such as what staggering the handover costs in throughput, and what you're asking them to decide.
- **Method in one sentence:** "We reviewed six weeks of logs and camera footage." That's enough to establish credibility without a methods slide.

**In the handout** (the reference material):
- **The full incident log.** Nobody can read it live, but someone will want to check their own area.
- **The list of camera angles reviewed.**
- **Your near-miss counting method.** Put a one-line definition in a slide footnote or say it aloud, since managers will ask "what counts?". The full criteria go in the handout.
- **Optional:** the recommendations with proposed owners and dates, so the handout doubles as an action sheet.

## Rules of thumb

- **One idea per slide.** If you need a second sentence to explain the slide, say it instead.
- **Slides shouldn't repeat your script.** If you read them aloud, the audience is bored by the second line.
- **Hand out the handout at the end**, or tell people it's coming. Otherwise they read it during your talk.
- **Keep the raw material in your back pocket.** If someone asks "how did you count that?", you can point to the handout instead of building a slide for it.

A rough shape for this talk is five or six slides: headline, location split, time pattern, recommendations, and the ask. Everything else supports those.
