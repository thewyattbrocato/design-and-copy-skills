**No, I wouldn't buy it.** The pitch is the kind that has gotten overlay vendors into trouble.

**Why not**
- **A toolbar can't make a site compliant.** Most accessibility problems are in the underlying page: missing or useless alt text, poor color contrast, unlabeled form fields, broken keyboard navigation, and checkout steps a screen reader can't complete. An overlay added on top can't reliably fix those, and sometimes it interferes with the assistive tools people already use.
- **Regulators have acted on this exact claim.** In January 2025 the FTC fined accessiBe $1 million for deceptive claims that its product could make sites compliant. Disability advocacy groups have long opposed overlays, and plenty of stores running them have still been sued.
- **"Legal protection included" needs scrutiny.** Ask for the contract language. It's usually limited, conditional, or just a promise to help you respond to a claim. It almost certainly isn't a guarantee that you won't be sued or a promise to cover your losses. Plaintiffs' lawyers look at whether the site works, not whether it has a widget.
- **"Trusted by thousands of stores" doesn't tell you much.** It says nothing about whether those stores are compliant or have been sued.

**What to do instead**

Sixty product pages is manageable for you and the freelancer.
1. **Check your theme or template.** On a hosted platform, much of the markup comes from the theme. Use a current one that advertises accessibility support, and check the platform's own accessibility documentation.
2. **Run free scans.** WAVE, axe DevTools, and Lighthouse will catch the obvious problems. Scans only find some of the issues, but they're a good start.
3. **Test by hand.** Go through a purchase using only the keyboard (Tab, Enter, Esc). Then try a screen reader (VoiceOver on a Mac is built in) on one product page and the checkout.
4. **Fix the content you control.** Write real alt text for product images, such as "Matte black 29-inch mountain bike, side view," not "IMG_2231." Make sure text has enough contrast, and give links and buttons meaningful names. Add captions or transcripts to any videos.
5. **Publish an accessibility statement** with a contact method for people who hit problems, and respond when they do.
6. **Consider a one-time audit.** A year of this toolbar is about $588. A review by an accessibility specialist of your homepage, a product page, the cart, and checkout would likely cost a similar amount. It would give you a concrete fix list for you and the freelancer, which is worth far more than a widget.

**Caveats**
- I'm not a lawyer. If you've received a demand letter, or you sell into the EU (where the European Accessibility Act has applied since June 2025), talk to one.
- No approach gives zero lawsuit risk. A documented, good-faith effort to fix real problems is the strongest position you can reasonably get.
- If the rep sends the contract terms, I'm happy to look through the "legal protection" language with you.
