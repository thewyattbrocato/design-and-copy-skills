Overall it's honest and readable, and the fixed-appointment offer is the best part. But it has a few problems.

**1. The news is buried.** The window change doesn't show up until paragraph 4. Customers who need to plan around it will skim the history first. Lead with something like: "Starting June 3, our delivery windows go from two hours to four. Here's why, and what you can do if that doesn't work for your dock." Then give the context.

**2. Check the date.** If you publish Friday, Oct 16, June 3 is either already past or about 7½ months away. Either way, include the year and make sure it's the date you mean. "This spring" and "last winter" also read oddly if the change is that far off.

**3. The statistic is the weakest point.** A four-hour window will lower missed deliveries almost by definition, because the target got twice as easy to hit. Sharp customers will notice. Say how you counted a miss, and ideally add something they can't dismiss that way, such as average arrival time or on-time rate against the original two-hour window. Also, the pilot covered 3 routes, so say how many stops that was.

**4. The idling benefit is yours, not theirs.** Drivers waiting at gates helps your costs, not your customers' dock schedules. It's fine to mention, but don't offer it as a reason they should be happy.

**5. The apology is vague.** "Some of that we deserved" sounds grudging. Name what went wrong, for example "we missed X% of windows in January–February," and own it plainly. You already have the weather, retirements and migration details, so use them.

**6. The tone is a bit PR-ish.** The "founder bought two used box trucks" opening is charming but has little to do with the announcement. "Thank you for your continued trust as we continue to grow" is boilerplate, and it repeats "grow." End on the practical point instead: the phone number or email for requesting a fixed appointment.

**7. The appointment offer needs specifics.** Include how to request one, any lead time, and what "existing accounts" means for new customers. Also, if the answer to a dock that can't handle four hours is a fixed appointment, say whether that changes the reliability numbers.

I'd restructure it in this order:
1. The change and the date.
2. Why: reliability matters most, and the pilot results with the miss definition.
3. What you're not changing: the 30-minute text.
4. The option if it doesn't work: fixed appointments, with contact details.
5. A short, specific acknowledgment of last winter.
