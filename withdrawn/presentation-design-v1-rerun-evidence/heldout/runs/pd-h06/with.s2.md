Use **B**.

**Why it works:** Three minutes is short, and council members hear a lot of speakers who open with credentials and general claims. B puts a specific child on a specific street in the first ten seconds. It also ends by tying that incident directly to the decision they're making tonight. The "She's fine" line matters. It keeps the story from feeling manipulative, and it shows the danger as a near miss that could have gone worse, not a tragedy that already happened.

**Why not the others:**
- **A** spends your most attention-rich 15 seconds on a greeting and your name. Those don't persuade anyone, and the clerk and the meeting agenda already record who you are. It's also a "walk you through" opener, which tells the council a long presentation is coming.
- **C** is generic. Council members have heard "cities across the country" claims many times, and they can wave them off as not applying to their town. It also asserts without showing anything.

**How to use B well:**
1. Fit your name and affiliation right after it, briefly: "I'm Dana Ortega with Cedar Hill Neighbors for Safe Streets, and I'm asking you to approve a protected bike lane on Alder between 4th and 12th." That gets A's useful content in without leading with it.
2. Put C's evidence in the middle, but make it local if you can: crash data for Alder, or a comparable nearby city that built one.
3. Close with a clear ask, such as "Please vote yes on the Alder Street bike lane tonight."

**One check:** make sure the incident is accurate and that you have the family's okay if they could be identified. If either is shaky, B loses its force, and you'd be better off with a version using Alder's crash statistics in its place.
