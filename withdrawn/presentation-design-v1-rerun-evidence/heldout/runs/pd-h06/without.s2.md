Use **B**.

**Why B works:**
- **It's concrete and human.** A specific child, a specific street, a specific month. Council members hear a lot of abstract arguments, and a story sticks where a statistic doesn't.
- **It makes the problem local.** The danger is on Alder Street, not "cities across the country." That's hard to wave off.
- **It ends on the ask.** "The street she was crossing is the one I'm asking you to change tonight" tells them what you want in the first 15 seconds. With only 3 minutes, that matters.
- **"She's fine" is a smart touch.** It keeps the story from feeling manipulative and makes it a near miss, which is a stronger case for acting before someone gets seriously hurt.

**Why not A:** It spends your best seconds on introductions and says "walk you through," which promises a long presentation. You can give your name and affiliation in one line right after the hook, or the clerk may already have it from the sign-in sheet.

**Why not C:** It's the weakest opening. It's generic, it makes claims with no source, and it's what every advocate says. It's better used as supporting evidence in the middle, ideally with a local or comparable-city number.

**Suggested structure:**
1. B (hook and ask)
2. One line: "I'm Dana Ortega with Cedar Hill Neighbors for Safe Streets."
3. Two or three facts: the crash history on Alder, plus one line from C backed by a real source
4. A specific ask: "Approve the protected lane between 4th and 12th."

One check: make sure the incident details are accurate and, if possible, that the family is fine with you sharing it. If you can't use that story, a similarly concrete local detail is the next best opening.
