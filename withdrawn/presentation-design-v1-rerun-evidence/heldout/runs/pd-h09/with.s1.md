**The test:** ask what the room needs to see to follow and remember the argument, what only needs your voice, and what someone would look up later.

| Where | What goes there | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Slide** | The claim, plus the one number or picture that proves it | If they miss it, they lose the argument |
| **Spoken** | Reasoning, context, stories, transitions | It's a "because" or a "here's what that looked like" |
| **Handout** | Reference material and proof of rigor | Someone might verify it, but nobody needs it in the moment |

Your takeaway is something a manager could disagree with: *"Two locations and one time window account for nearly all our near-misses, and three low-cost changes address them."* Everything on a slide has to support that sentence.

**Applied to your material**

- **Slides (about 4–5 for a 10-minute slot):**
  1. **"32 of 38 near-misses happened at two spots."** Show a simple bar chart of dock 6 (23), freezer door (9) and other (6). Put "6 weeks of logs and camera footage" in small text as the source line.
  2. **"Most happen from 2 to 4 pm, when handover overlaps the inbound peak."** Show one chart of near-misses by hour. I don't have your hourly counts, so use your real ones.
  3. **"Three fixes, each matched to a cause."** Dock 6 gets a one-way rule and a stop line. The freezer door gets mirrors. Handover gets staggered. Keep it to a few words per line.
  4. **The ask:** approve the three changes, with an owner and a start date. I've left those as placeholders for you to fill in. Leave this slide up during questions.
- **Say out loud:**
  - How you reviewed the footage.
  - Your one-sentence definition of a near-miss.
  - Why the handover overlap creates risk, such as tired drivers, unfamiliar traffic and trucks backing in.
  - One or two vivid moments from the footage, if you can show them without blaming individuals.
  - Why each fix fits its location.
- **Handout:**
  - The full incident log.
  - The list of camera angles.
  - The complete counting method.

**The method is the tricky one.** Managers will ask "how did you decide something was a near-miss?" Give them one spoken sentence, keep the full definition in the handout, and make a backup slide after your ask in case someone pushes on it. Do the same for the other likely hard question, such as the cost or disruption of staggering handover.

**Things to avoid**
- Don't put the incident log or the camera list on a slide. They're tables, and nobody can read them while listening to you.
- Don't open with an agenda or with your method. Open with the need, then the takeaway, then the evidence. Most of your audience cares about the 84% (32 of 38) before they care how you counted.
- Don't copy your handout onto slides. Each fact should live in one place.
