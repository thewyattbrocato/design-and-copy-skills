# How to decide what goes where

Ask three questions about each item, in this order:

1. **Slide:** Does the room need to see it to follow the claim? That means the claim itself, a number, or a picture. A slide holds a headline and one visual, under about 30 words.
2. **Say it out loud:** Is it reasoning, context, or a story? The "why" and the transitions work better in your voice than in bullets.
3. **Handout:** Would someone look it up or check it later rather than listen to it? Logs, lists, and methods go here. Tables and reference material never go on a live slide.

If an item fails all three, cut it.

## Your example

**Takeaway:** Three targeted changes will remove most of the near-misses, so I'm asking the plant managers to approve them.

I'm assuming a ~10-minute slot, which means about 4 content slides.

| Slide | Title (the claim) | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "38 near-misses in 6 weeks, and 23 were at one crossing: dock 6" | A plant sketch with dock 6 highlighted, or a simple bar chart of 23 / 9 / 6 |
| 2 | "Most happen 2–4 pm, when second-shift handover meets peak inbound trucks" | One chart of near-misses by hour |
| 3 | "Three changes, one for each hotspot" | Three short lines: one-way rule and stop line at dock 6, mirrors at the freezer door, staggered handover |
| 4 | "Approve all three by [date]; [owner] leads" | The ask, which stays up during questions |

Check the titles by reading them in order. They should give the whole argument and the ask without anything else.

## What you say out loud

- **The review in one sentence.** Say something like "We went through six weeks of logs and camera footage." That is enough for the room to trust the work.
- **Why the overlap causes it.** Explain how handover and truck arrivals put more people and vehicles at the crossing at the same moment. This is the part that persuades, and it needs your voice.
- **One concrete moment from the footage at dock 6.** Pick the clearest clip or incident. A single vivid case makes 23 feel real. Use one you actually have.
- **The 6 scattered ones.** Say "no pattern, so no fix proposed," and move on. They don't earn a slide.
- **The freezer door's 9.** It's covered on slide 1 or 3. Say what mirrors fix there, which is probably blind-corner visibility. Confirm that against the footage first.

## What goes in the handout

- The full incident log
- The list of camera angles reviewed
- The method for counting a near-miss

Optionally, add a one-page summary of the three recommendations with owners and dates.

## One exception: the method

Plant managers will probably ask, "What counts as a near-miss?" Say the definition in one sentence during slide 1. Keep a **backup slide** after the end with the counting rule, and only show it if someone challenges the count. The same goes for the camera coverage: if someone asks "did you see the whole floor?", a backup slide beats a scramble.

## Quick test for any item

- Cut the speech: could a stranger still tell what the slide shows and why? If not, the slide is too thin or too vague.
- If you won't mention it, it shouldn't be on screen.
- Each fact should live in one place. If the log is in the handout, don't also show a table of it on a slide.

Two things would sharpen this: how many minutes you have, and whether you have hour-by-hour counts for slide 2. I haven't invented any numbers beyond what you gave me, so the ask slide's owner and date are placeholders for you to fill in.
