**Ship B.**

**Why**

The page's job is to get a visitor to request a quote. B is the only layout where that job is the focal point.

- **B has one clear entry point and one action.** The headline says what Parcelo is and why it's different. The form and its single button sit directly under it. A stranger can say what to do within five seconds.
- **The guarantee is your differentiator.** "Delivered by 6pm or free" tells people what Parcelo is, who it's for and why to trust it. A and C leave that to the visitor to work out.
- **A buries the action.** A tiny "Get a quote" link under a huge photo makes the image the boss and the conversion the afterthought. The rider photo gives you mood, but the logo and a text link don't explain the offer. This is the "illustration louder than the headline" failure.
- **C has no focal point.** Four equal tiles with no headline means four peers and no ranking. Quote, Track, Business and Careers serve different audiences, and only Quote is the conversion you're launching for. Track and Careers are mostly for people who already know you. C also never says what Parcelo does.
- **B's muted photo is correct.** It's supporting context. It sits behind the headline and form in the hierarchy.

**What I'd tweak**

1. **Make the guarantee credible.** "Free" invites the question "what's the catch?" Add a short line under the headline or button, such as "Order by 2pm, any address in [city]. Full refund if we're late." Keep it small and readable, not faded, so it clears contrast. Make sure the real terms back it up.
2. **Keep the form short.** Pickup address, drop-off address and one button are enough. Fields beyond that cost conversions. Ask for package size and contact details on the next step.
3. **Name the button for the outcome.** Use "Get my price" or "See price and book" instead of "Submit". Use one solid primary style, and keep every other control on the page quieter.
4. **Put the logo and nav at meta level.** Keep the logo modest. Put Track, Business and Careers in a quiet nav or a footer-weight row. That keeps C's useful destinations without making them compete with the quote. "Track" matters most because existing customers will look for it, so give it a clear spot in the header.
5. **Use a real rider photo with a scrim.** Keep it muted and use it to show a person and a parcel. Put the headline and form on a quiet patch so text never sits on a busy area of the image. Keep the photo's value contrast and saturation below the headline and button.
6. **Align everything to one left edge.** The headline, supporting line and form fields should share it. Don't center the headline over a left-aligned form.
7. **Test it.** Squint at the page and check that the form and button are what's left. Then A/B test the headline against a version that states the speed ("Same-day delivery, quoted in 10 seconds"). The "or free" framing may win on trust or lose if it sounds risky.
