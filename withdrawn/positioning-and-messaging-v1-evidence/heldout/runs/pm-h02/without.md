The problem is probably that "helps bookstores run their events" describes who it's for, not what it does. People nod because they can't picture anything. A good line names the concrete job, like "sell tickets," "handle RSVPs," or "get the word out."

I don't know which of those you do, so here are drafts with a guess filled in. Swap in the real verbs.

**Out loud**

- "You know how indie bookstores host readings and book clubs, but it's all done with spreadsheets, email threads, and a Facebook post? We replace that with one place to [list the event, take RSVPs, and sell the author's books]."
- Shorter: "We help indie bookstores fill their events. [Readings, signings, book clubs.]"
- Or lead with the pain: "Every indie bookstore runs events, and almost none of them have a tool for it. We built one."

The first one works best at a meetup. It gives the listener a picture, and it's easy to follow with "what do you mean?"

**For the site**

> **Events, handled.**
> [Product name] gives independent bookstores one place to plan, promote, and run readings, signings, and book clubs, so staff spend less time on logistics and more time with readers.

A few tips for either version:
- Name one specific thing the product does, like RSVPs, reminders, or author coordination. Concrete verbs are what make people understand it.
- "Independent bookstores" is already a good, vivid audience. Keep it.
- Skip "platform," "solution," and "streamline." They're the words people nod at.

2. What did they do before, and what's the most annoying part of it?
3. When people do get it, what do they say? That reaction is often the best phrasing.
