## Voice and Tone Guide for Marrow & Co

**Plain-spoken, not jargony.** You explain financial concepts as if you're talking to someone at the bar, not an accounting classroom. Your clients should understand what numbers mean and why they matter—no footnotes, no buzzwords. This means simplicity, but not oversimplification; your clients are smart, and your words should respect that.
- Say: "Your food cost is running at 32% this month, up from 28% last month. That usually means either prices went up or there's waste in prep."
- Don't say: "Optimize your cost-of-goods-sold metrics via supply chain analytics."

**Peer-level, not corporate.** You've been in your clients' shoes. You're not a distant firm dispensing wisdom from above; you're someone who's closed a restaurant at 11 PM and knows what stress looks like. This keeps you from talking *at* people and talking *with* them instead. The boundary: you're still the professional they hired, not their drinking buddy.
- Say: "We handle the bookkeeping so you can focus on what you're good at."
- Don't say: "At Marrow & Co, we provide best-in-class financial solutions tailored to the unique needs of your hospitality business."

**Direct and practical.** You tell people what things mean and why it matters to them. "Your rent is 15% of revenue" is more useful than "Your occupancy ratio is suboptimal." You skip the fluff and get to the point. The boundary: directness doesn't mean coldness—you're practical, not terse.
- Say: "That $5k jump in labor costs last month came from the extra staffing you added for the holiday. Here's what it cost per shift."
- Don't say: "Comprehensive analytics provide actionable insights for informed decision-making."

**Reassuring without being patronizing.** Your clients sometimes feel dumb about numbers. Your job is to make them feel smart. You do that by normalizing confusion ("this is a question every restaurant owner asks") and meeting people where they are, never implying they should have known better. The boundary: warmth, not cheerfulness; confidence, not condescension.
- Say: "You might be wondering why your P&L doesn't match what you think you made—here's why."
- Don't say: "Don't worry, it's super easy to understand!"

---

## How this plays out across surfaces

**In explanations and reports**: Direct first (the number), plain second (what it means), human third (why it matters to them). Numbers always come with context.

**In conversations about problems**: Name the situation, then the solution. "You're spending 40% on labor; most places run 28–32%. Let's figure out why."

**In pitches and first contact**: Lead with who you are (ex-restaurant managers) and why that matters (you get their world). Avoid "best-in-class" and "dedicated team."

## The two pieces rewritten

**Before:** "At Marrow & Co, we provide best-in-class financial solutions tailored to the unique needs of your hospitality business. Our dedicated team of experts is committed to delivering exceptional results."

**After:** "Marrow & Co handles bookkeeping and monthly reporting for restaurants. All five of us managed restaurants for years, so we speak your language—not accounting-speak. Every month you'll get numbers that make sense and actually show you what's happening in your business."

**Before:** "Contact us today to learn how we can help take your restaurant to the next level!"

**After:** "Ready to stop guessing about your numbers? Tell us about your restaurant and what's keeping you up at night. We'll talk through whether we're the right fit."
