## Marrow & Co Voice & Tone Guide

**Who we sound like:** A former restaurant manager who now runs a small bookkeeping firm—someone who's been in the weeds, understands your problems, and talks like a peer, not a consultant.

**Core principles:**

**1. Speak like you've managed a kitchen**
Use clear, direct language. No "solutions," "leverage," or "optimize." Say what you mean. "We'll break down your monthly numbers so you actually understand where your money's going" beats "tailored financial solutions." You talk to restaurant owners the way you'd talk to a line cook—assuming they're smart and busy, not stupid or lazy.

**2. Skip the corporate theater**
You're five people. Act like it. Don't claim "best-in-class" or promise restaurants will "reach the next level"—that's what every agency says. Instead, tell them what you'll actually do and why it matters for a restaurant specifically. ("You'll know which menu items are dragging your margins down" is better than "exceptional results.")

**3. Make it about their reality, not your ego**
Your clients are stressed. They're juggling food costs, labor, cash flow, and whether they can take a day off. Talk to that. Acknowledge the mess. Show you get it because you've lived it. "Your bookkeeper disappeared, your QuickBooks is a disaster, and you're too tired to figure it out" opens more doors than "We provide comprehensive financial management."

**4. Be honest and plain**
Jargon makes people feel dumb. You hate that. So don't do it. "Cash flow" is fine; "liquidity positioning" isn't. If something is complicated, say so—and say you'll walk them through it step by step. Confidence is showing you can explain a balance sheet in plain English, not hiding behind it.

**5. Show you're ex-restaurant, not reformed**
You're not lecturing from the outside. You managed restaurants. You know the difference between busy and broken, between an unlucky month and a structural problem. Let that show. Small references to the world they live in (inventory nightmares, surprise tax bills, the difference between revenue and profit) remind them you speak their language.

**Tone in practice:**
- Direct, not flowery
- Conversational, not corporate
- Honest, not overselling
- Practical, not abstract
- Warm, not slick
