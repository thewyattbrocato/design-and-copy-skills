The guide has the right topics, but most of the questions would get you polite, unreliable answers. The main problem is that they're leading or hypothetical. People say "sure, I'd use that" to a hypothetical and then never do. Past behavior is more reliable.

**Question by question**

1. **Intro:** Fine, but add a few things. Ask for permission to record. Say there are no right answers. Say you're researching, not selling, so they feel free to be critical. Add a warm-up like "Tell me about the work you do."
2. **"Don't you find it stressful...?"** This is leading, because it tells them the answer you want. It also assumes irregular income is a problem for them. Try: "Walk me through how you keep track of money coming in and going out." Then follow the stress if it comes up on its own.
3. **"Would you use an app that automatically sets aside money for taxes?"** This is a closed, hypothetical yes/no, and almost everyone says yes. Ask about what they do now: "Tell me about the last time you had to pay taxes. How did you prepare? How did it go?" Follow with "How do you decide how much to set aside today?"
4. **"$5, $10, $15?"** This anchors them to your range and rests on a hypothetical. Ask what they pay now: "What tools or services do you pay for to manage money or taxes? What do they cost?" If you want price sensitivity, use a Van Westendorp-style question set in a survey, not an interview.
5. **"Perfect budgeting app":** People are bad at designing solutions. Ask about the problem instead: "What's the most frustrating part of managing your money right now?" or "Tell me about a time something slipped through the cracks."
6. **"Our app makes invoicing really easy. How do you feel about that?"** This is a pitch, not a question, and it invites flattery. Cut it. Ask: "How do you handle invoicing today? What's the last time it went badly, such as a late payment?"
7. **"Anything else?":** Fine. Improve it with "Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?"

**What's missing**
- **Current tools and workarounds:** spreadsheets, other apps, an accountant. This tells you your real competition.
- **Recent specific stories:** "Tell me about the last time..." beats "How do you usually...?"
- **Probes:** have "Why?", "Can you give an example?" and "What happened next?" ready. In a 45-minute interview these do most of the work.
- **Screening and context:** how long they've freelanced, their income range, and how variable it is. Confirm these before or at the start.
- **Wrap-up:** ask whether you can follow up, and whether they know other freelancers to talk to.

**Suggested flow (about 45 minutes)**
1. Intro and consent (3 min)
2. Background and their work (5 min)
3. How they manage money and irregular income today (10 min)
4. Taxes: the last time, and how they prepare (10 min)
5. Invoicing and getting paid (7 min)
6. Tools they use or have abandoned, and why (7 min)
7. Wrap-up (3 min)

If you want reactions to Ledgerleaf's ideas, such as auto-set-aside for taxes, save them for the last 5 minutes. Show them as a concept and ask what would make it useful or not, rather than whether they'd use it.
