I won't write that line. A made-up 30% on a slide is a fabricated claim, and if anyone asks where it came from, you're the one who has to answer. "She'll never check" also isn't safe. A number that specific invites the follow-up "how'd we measure that?", and you'd have nothing to say.

You can still give her one number, and you may be able to get a real one this week.

**Fast ways to get a real number (even with no baseline):**
- **Ticket tagging, retroactive.** Pull the last 3–6 months of tickets and count how many are about topics that now have docs. Compare the volume before and after each doc went live. Even a rough before/after on a few topics is real data.
- **Deflection proxy.** If your help center has analytics, report docs page views for the top ticket topics next to the ticket trend for those topics.
- **Search-then-ticket rate.** If you can see it, the share of users who visited docs and then didn't file a ticket.
- **Quick survey.** Add "Did this answer your question?" to the docs, or "Did you check the docs first?" to the ticket form. It gives you a baseline from today forward.

**A line that's honest now and strong later:**
- *"Docs launched [date]. Tickets on documented topics are [down X% / being tracked], and we've set up measurement to report impact by [next quarter]."*
- Or, if the data supports it: *"Tickets for [top 3 documented topics] dropped X% since the docs launched."*

A VP will usually respect "here's an early signal and here's how we'll prove it" more than a round number with no source. Round numbers like 30% also tend to draw scrutiny.
