## Series: "What Your Books Know"

**The angle:** Every accountant posts tips. You'd be doing something different: explaining what a dental practice's numbers say about the practice, and the gap between what owners think is happening and what the books show. You never need a client story, because the material is the *mechanics*, which are the same in every practice. Use clearly labeled hypothetical numbers.

**The six parts** (monthly, so about half a year of email):

1. **Profit isn't the money in your account.** Why a profitable year can feel broke.
2. **Production isn't collections.** The number on the software dashboard vs. the number at the bank, and where the difference goes.
3. **Overhead percentages, and when to ignore them.** How to use benchmarks without being run by them.
4. **The year you bought something.** What equipment, a buildout, or a loan does to profit, cash, and the tax return, and why those three tell different stories.
5. **What an associate really costs (and earns).** Looking at it as a line in the books, not a gut feeling.
6. **Books a stranger could read.** What a lender, buyer, or partner sees when they look at your financials, and what that does to the practice's value.

Each part has one idea, one hypothetical example, and one question the reader can ask their own books. That keeps the series consistent and easy to write.

## Part One: Profit isn't the money in your account

**Subject line (email):** Why a profitable year can still feel broke

**LinkedIn version:**

> Here's a conversation I've had in some form many times:
>
> "My P&L says I made $300K. Why is there no money in the account?"
>
> Nobody did anything wrong. The P&L and the bank account are answering two different questions.
>
> The P&L asks: *did the practice earn more than it spent?*
> The bank account asks: *how much cash is here right now?*
>
> A few things sit in the gap:
>
> **Loan principal.** Your equipment or practice loan payment has two parts. The interest shows up as an expense. The principal doesn't show up on the P&L at all, but that cash is gone.
>
> **Equipment purchases.** A $90K purchase leaves your account on day one. The P&L only sees a slice of it each year.
>
> **Owner draws and tax payments.** Neither is a practice expense, but both move real money.
>
> **Timing.** Insurance money you've earned but haven't received yet counts as income before it counts as cash.
>
> So a practice can be profitable and still tight on cash. That's usually just arithmetic, not a problem with the practice.
>
> The question worth asking your books: *"Where did the profit go?"* If nobody can answer in five minutes, that's the thing to fix first.
>
> Next month: why the production number on your dashboard and the deposits at your bank don't match.

**Email version (add this under the same opening):**

Hi [first name],

This is the first in a six-part series on what your practice's books can tell you, beyond what goes on the tax return. Each month I'll take one idea and explain it plainly.

Let's start with the most common confusion I see.

**Profit and cash are two different numbers.**

Here's a simple, made-up example. A practice's P&L shows $300,000 of profit for the year. Here's where that money could have gone:

- Principal payments on the practice and equipment loans: **$85,000**
- A new piece of equipment bought outright: **$40,000**
- Quarterly estimated tax payments: **$75,000**
- Insurance payments earned in December but not received until January: **$30,000**

That's $230,000 accounted for before the owner has taken a dollar home. None of it is wasteful, and most of it is smart. But if you only look at the P&L, it feels like $300,000 should be sitting there.

**What to do with this:** Once a year, ask for a simple "profit to cash" walk. It starts at net profit and subtracts the items above until it lands at the change in your bank balance. If your bookkeeper can't produce it, it's worth asking why.

**One question for your own books:** *Where did last year's profit actually go?*

Next month: why the production number on your software and the deposits at your bank never quite match.
