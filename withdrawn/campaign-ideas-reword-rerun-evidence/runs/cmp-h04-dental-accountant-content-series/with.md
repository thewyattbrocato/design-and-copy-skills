**The idea:** *The Numbers Exam.* You explain a practice's finances in the language dentists already think in: exam, findings, diagnosis, treatment plan. Each part takes one number that looks fine on the surface and shows what it's hiding. No tax tips, and no client stories are needed, because the dentist's own books are the case.

**Why it fits you:** [assumed: check against your own clients] Dentists are trained to diagnose, but nobody taught them to read their own financials. You've spent eight years reading them, so you're the specialist they refer their numbers to.

## The six parts

1. **Production, collections, profit.** Three numbers that sound alike and aren't.
2. **The overhead number.** What's normal, what's a symptom, and why one percentage can't tell you which.
3. **Pay yourself.** Salary, distributions and profit are different things, and mixing them up causes problems later.
4. **The equipment decision.** How to look at a big purchase before the sales rep makes it sound like a bargain.
5. **The associate question.** What adding a doctor or hygienist does to the books, beyond the production number.
6. **The annual exam.** A one-page checklist that pulls parts 1 to 5 together, with an invitation to book a review.

## Part 1 (LinkedIn post)

> Every dental practice I work with tracks production. It's on the screen at the morning huddle.
>
> Production is not money. It's the fees you charged. Collections is what actually came in. Profit is what's left after everything goes out.
>
> Here's an illustration with round numbers, not a client:
>
> Production: $1,000,000
> Collections: $850,000
> Profit before owner pay: $250,000
>
> Same practice, same year, three very different numbers. The gap between the first two is write-offs, insurance adjustments and unpaid balances. The gap between the last two is everything it costs to open the doors.
>
> A busy schedule can hide a thin margin. A strong production number says the chairs are full. It doesn't say the practice is healthy.
>
> If you only know one of these numbers, it's usually the first one, and it's the least useful.
>
> Which of the three do you check most often?
>
> *This is part 1 of The Numbers Exam, a series on reading your practice's finances the way you read an X-ray. Next month: the overhead number.*
