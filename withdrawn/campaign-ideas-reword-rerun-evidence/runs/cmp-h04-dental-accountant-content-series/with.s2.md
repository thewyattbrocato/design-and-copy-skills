**The idea: "Ten-minute books."** Each part asks one question about the practice that the books can answer, and has nothing to do with taxes. The reader answers it from their own reports in ten minutes. You aren't using client stories. The reader's numbers are the case study.

**Why it works for you:** Dentists are trained to diagnose, and nobody has shown them how to read their own financials the same way. Eight years of doing this work gives you the authority, so you don't need to share anyone's data. Other accountants post what to deduct. You teach people how to read their own numbers.

**The six parts**
1. What an hour of chair time costs you (written below)
2. Production vs. collections: where the gap goes
3. What you earn vs. what you take home vs. what the practice keeps
4. Supplies and lab: which costs creep up without anyone deciding to spend more
5. Does each provider cover their own cost?
6. Next year's big purchases: what cash you'll have, and when

**Three forms, each of which works on its own**
- **LinkedIn post:** The question plus the arithmetic, in about 150 words.
- **Monthly email:** The full walkthrough with a worked example. It ends with "Reply with your number and I'll tell you what I'd look at next."
- **One-page worksheet:** The same question as a fill-in. It's the lead magnet, and it also gives you a reason to start conversations.

## Part 1: What does an hour of your chair cost you?

**Email version**

**Subject: The number most dentists have never calculated**

Most practice owners can tell me last month's production. Far fewer can tell me what it costs to have the doors open for one hour.

Here's how to find out. It takes about ten minutes, and you only need your P&L.

1. **Add up your monthly overhead.** Include rent, staff, supplies, lab, software, insurance, loan payments and everything else. Leave out your own pay.
2. **Count the clinical hours you're open in a month.** Use the hours you are open to patients, not the hours you're personally in the chair.
3. **Divide the first number by the second.**

Here's an example with made-up round numbers. Say overhead is $60,000 a month and you're open 160 hours. That works out to $375 an hour. Before you earn anything, every hour on the schedule has to bring in $375 just to cover the doors being open.

What the number is useful for:

- **Judging a slow day.** A cancellation costs you the production, and the $375 keeps running anyway.
- **Judging short, low-fee appointments.** Compare what a block of time produces to what an hour costs. Treat this as a yardstick, because rent doesn't go away when a slot is empty.
- **Judging a new hire, a new lease or a longer day.** Each one changes the number, and now you can see by how much.

[confirm: In my experience, most owners are surprised by this number the first time. It's usually higher than they guessed.]

**LinkedIn version**

Ask a dentist what they produced last month and they'll know.

Ask what it costs to keep the doors open for one hour and you'll often get a pause.

Here's the ten-minute version:

Monthly overhead (everything except your own pay) ÷ clinical hours open = your hourly cost.

Made-up example: $60,000 ÷ 160 hours = $375 an hour.

Every hour on your schedule has to bring in that much before the practice earns anything. A cancellation or a short low-fee appointment looks different once you have the number. (It's a yardstick, not a loss on every slot, because most of the cost is there whether the chair is full or not.)
