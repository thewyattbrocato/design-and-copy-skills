# Talking points: design system investment

## The one-sentence version
"We're asking for about a quarter of two people's time so that every change to the website and app ships faster, looks consistent, and costs less to maintain across all three squads."

## Open with the problem, not the solution
- We have a website, an app, and three squads building for both. Without shared foundations, each squad solves the same problems separately: how a recipe card looks, how checkout errors read, how a "skip this week" button behaves.
- Customers feel this as inconsistency. Something works one way in the app, another on the web, and another in a different part of the same app. For a subscription business, trust and ease of use drive retention.
- The cost compounds. Every new feature adds more one-off versions to maintain, and the problem gets more expensive each quarter we wait.

## What each person cares about

**COO (speed, predictability, operations)**
- Fewer rebuilds of things we've already built. Squads start from working pieces instead of from scratch.
- Fewer "why is this different here?" cycles between design, engineering, and QA.
- Seasonal and promo launches, like holiday menus or new plans, get easier to turn around.
- Less dependence on whoever happens to remember how something was built.

**Head of sales (conversion, partnerships, brand)**
- A consistent, polished experience from landing page through checkout supports conversion and the brand promise.
- Faster page and campaign changes. When sales wants a new offer or landing variation, there's a reliable kit to build it from.
- Accessibility and quality fixes made once apply everywhere, which cuts the risk of a broken or confusing step in the funnel.

**Finance (cost, risk, return)**
- This is a modest, bounded ask: roughly 0.5 FTE-equivalent for one quarter, with a defined review at the end.
- It's meant to lower the cost of future work, not add new headcount.
- Maintaining many near-duplicate versions of the same thing is a hidden recurring cost, and this reduces it.

## Be upfront about the numbers
Don't hide that you don't have hard data yet. Saying so builds credibility:

- "I don't have a precise measure of the time we lose today. That's part of what I'd like to fix."
- "What I can tell you is where it shows up: [pick 2-3 real examples, such as the same component rebuilt by multiple squads, design-to-code mismatches caught late, or inconsistent states across web and app]."
- "In the first few weeks, we'll baseline it, so by the end of the quarter you'll have real numbers and not just my estimate."

## Make it low-risk with a measurable plan
Propose the measurement as part of the ask:

1. **Weeks 1-2: baseline.** Audit how many distinct versions of the most common UI pieces exist (buttons, forms, recipe cards, etc.). Ask each squad lead for a rough estimate of hours per sprint spent on UI rework and design/dev back-and-forth.
2. **Weeks 3-10: build and adopt.** Focus on the highest-traffic flows first, like menu browsing, checkout, and account/subscription management. Skip the full catalog.
3. **Weeks 11-12: report back.** Share changes in time to build comparable features, number of UI variants, and design-related bugs.

Suggested success measures: time to ship a comparable UI change before vs. after, count of duplicate or inconsistent UI patterns, and design-related bug or QA rework counts.

## Anticipate the pushback
- **"Why not just keep shipping features?"** "We would be, and faster. The two people stay on product work for most of their time. This investment is what makes the next several quarters of feature work cheaper."
- **"Can you prove ROI?"** "Not yet, and I won't pretend otherwise. I'm proposing a quarter specifically to produce evidence, with checkpoints so you can stop it if it isn't paying off."
- **"What do we give up?"** Have a specific answer ready, such as which roadmap items slip or shrink. Leaders trust asks that name the tradeoff.
- **"Is this a one-time thing?"** Be honest: it needs light ongoing maintenance after the quarter, but much less than the initial build. Give a rough figure if you have one.

## Closing line
"I'm asking for a bounded, measurable bet. If it works, every squad ships faster and our customers get a more consistent experience. If the numbers don't show that after a quarter, we stop."

## Before the meeting
- Pick two or three concrete, recent examples from your own squads. A real story like "we built the same quantity selector three times" will land better than any general argument.
- Decide what roadmap work gives way, so you can answer that directly.
- Ask each squad lead for a quick gut-check estimate of lost time. Even a rough "a few hours per person per sprint" turns into a usable range, as long as you label it as an estimate.
