I wrote a notice you can send today, but it doesn't use "has been fully resolved." You haven't closed the hole, so that statement is false. People would act on it by skipping a password change, and they'd be worse off if the attacker still has access. Regulators and plaintiffs' lawyers also tend to focus on exactly that kind of claim. Breach-notification rules (state laws, GDPR if you have EU users) generally require an accurate description of what happened. A statement you'd have to retract in days would do far more damage to trust than a calm, honest one.

The notice below keeps the tone calm, which is what your CEO wants. It tells people the one thing that protects them, which is to change their password.

---

**Email**

**Subject:** Security incident affecting your Pinecone Notes account

Hi,

We're writing to tell you about a security incident that affected your Pinecone Notes account.

**What happened**
On [date], we discovered that an unauthorized party had accessed an old database and taken data for about 38,000 accounts, including yours.

**What was taken**
Your email address and your hashed (scrambled) password. [Confirm: your notes and payment information were not in this database.]

**What we're doing**
We're investigating how the access happened, and that work is still under way. We haven't yet confirmed that the entry point is closed, so we'll send an update by [date] with what we've learned. [Add any steps you've actually taken, such as resetting passwords, bringing in a forensics firm, or notifying regulators.]

**What you should do**
1. Change your Pinecone Notes password now: [link].
2. If you use the same password on any other site, change it there too.
3. Be cautious with emails that claim to be from us and ask for your password or personal details. We will never ask for them.

Hashed passwords are much harder to use than plain ones, but strong protection isn't guaranteed, especially for simple or reused passwords. [Only if true: We have not seen evidence that any accounts have been misused so far, but our investigation is ongoing.]

We're sorry this happened. Questions can go to [contact], and we'll keep you updated at [status page].

[Name], Pinecone Notes

---

**In-app banner**

> **Security incident: please change your password.** An unauthorized party took email addresses and hashed passwords from an old database, including yours. Our investigation is ongoing. [Change password] [What happened]

**For your CEO**

- **Opening line:** "Your security is our top priority" is fine as a closing line after the facts. As the opener of a breach notice, it tends to read as boilerplate and annoy people. Leading with what happened comes across as more trustworthy.
- **"No evidence of misuse":** Include it only if you've actually looked, for example at logins and credential-stuffing attempts. Keep "so far" and "ongoing" in the wording. If you haven't checked, leave it out.
- **If the hole closes before you send:** Once it's confirmed closed, you can say "we have closed the access point we identified." That's specific and true. Don't claim the whole issue is resolved while the investigation is open.
- **Legal review:** Have counsel check the notice before it goes out. Notification deadlines may already be running, since you found out yesterday.
