I wouldn't send the notice as your CEO described it. Two of the three phrases are untrue today, and a false statement in a breach notice is what turns a bad week into a lasting problem.

- **"Fully resolved"** is false. You haven't found the entry point or closed it. If the attacker returns, or someone finds out the hole was open when you wrote this, the notice becomes evidence that you misled users and regulators.
- **"No evidence your data has been misused"** is technically defensible only if you've looked and found nothing. Right now you haven't had time to look. Users will read it as "nothing bad has happened," and you can't say that.
- **"Your security is our top priority"** isn't false, but it reads as boilerplate when it opens a breach notice. It lands better after the facts.

A calm tone and an honest notice don't conflict. Specific, plain notices with clear next steps usually calm people more than reassurance does. Here is a version you can send today:

---

**Subject: Important security notice about your Pinecone Notes account**

We're writing to tell you about a security incident that affected your Pinecone Notes account.

**What happened**
On [date], we discovered that an unauthorized party accessed an older database and copied account information. Your account was among about 38,000 affected.

**What was taken**
The email address and hashed password for your account. Passwords were stored in hashed form, not as plain text. We have no indication so far that the contents of your notes were accessed, but our investigation is ongoing.

**What we don't know yet**
We're still working out how the unauthorized party got in, and we haven't yet confirmed that the access is closed off. We'll update you by [date] or sooner if we learn something important.

**What we're doing**
- Investigating the cause with [internal team / outside forensic firm].
- [Resetting passwords for affected accounts / requiring a password change at next login.]
- Notifying [regulators, as required].

**What you should do**
1. Change your Pinecone Notes password now: [link].
2. If you use that password anywhere else, change it there too.
3. Turn on two-factor authentication: [link].
4. Be careful with emails that claim to be from us and ask for your password or personal details. We will never ask for your password by email.

**Questions**
Contact us at [security@ address]. Updates will be posted at [status page URL].

We're sorry this happened, and we'll keep you informed as we learn more.

[Name, title]
Pinecone Notes

---

Before you send it:

1. **Fill in the bracketed items you can actually back up.** Only promise a password reset or 2FA if it will be live when the email goes out.
2. **Check your legal deadlines today.** Depending on where your users live, GDPR (72 hours to the regulator), state breach laws, or others may apply. Have counsel review the notice, since they'll likely want to adjust the wording.
3. **Take this to your CEO.** You can tell her the notice is built to keep people calm, but she'd be signing a "fully resolved" claim that her own team knows isn't true. If she wants a stronger line, "we've taken immediate steps to protect your account" works if it's accurate. Once you've closed the hole and confirmed it, a follow-up saying so will carry real weight.
