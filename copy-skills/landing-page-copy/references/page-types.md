# Page types

Starting section plans. Reorder by what the reader already knows and where they arrive from, and drop any section the facts cannot fill. Every plan ends with the ask and keeps price and conditions before it.

## Home

The page serves several readers, so the hero names the category and the main audience, then routes the rest.

1. Hero: what it is, who for, one action.
2. The outcome in concrete terms, or the problem if readers feel it.
3. How it works, as a short sequence.
4. Proof for the most common doubt.
5. Routes for other readers (who else it fits, where to go).
6. Price or "from" price and the main condition, or a link to them.
7. The ask.

## Campaign landing page

One reader, one action, no site navigation to wander off. The hero repeats the promise of the ad or email in its own words. Order: hero, outcome, how it works, proof, terms, objections, ask. Short unless the decision is costly.

## Long-form sales

Used for expensive, new or risky decisions. Length comes from answering every real doubt, not from padding. Each section has one job and a heading that states it.

1. Hero with the promise and who it is for.
2. The situation the reader is in and what it costs them now.
3. The outcome, in concrete scenes.
4. How it works.
5. Proof after each claim, with someone like the reader.
6. What is included, price, terms, effort and migration.
7. Objections, each answered with a fact. "Not for you if".
8. The guarantee with its condition.
9. The ask, repeated at each decision point.

## Product and feature

Lead with what the feature lets the reader do, not what it is. Show it working (a short demonstration, steps, or an annotated example) before describing options. Connect it to the job it replaces. End on the action that lets them try it.

## Pricing

The reader is nearly decided and is checking cost and catch. Lead with plans and what each includes, named for the situation each fits, not for the audience's size. State period, renewal, fees, limits and cancellation beside the price. Answer the money objections in a short FAQ, then the ask. No urgency without a real cause.

## About

The reader asks "can I trust these people with this?". Say what you do and for whom first, then why you do it, then the proof that you do it well. A founding story belongs here only if it explains why the product is the way it is. Close on an action, not a mission statement.

## Case study

One customer, told as a story with their words. Order: who they are and what they needed, what they tried before, what changed, the result with how it was measured, what it took to get there, an ask aimed at readers in the same position. Use only details and quotes the user supplied; otherwise leave placeholders.
