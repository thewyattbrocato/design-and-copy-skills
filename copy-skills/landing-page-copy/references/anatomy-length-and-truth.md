# Anatomy, length and truth

Load when a page must be cut or sized, when the hero opens on a feature, when a real limit has to be stated, or when the user asks how much page they need.

## Size the page by the decision

Length follows five things: price, risk, how new the thing is, how emotional the choice is, and whether the page asks for a purchase or only a next step. A cheap, familiar, one-step ask earns a short page with benefit, one proof, terms and the action. A costly, new or two-step ask earns a long one. Say which you assumed in one line. Detail the page cannot hold goes one click away, linked beside its claim.

## Cut by ranked removal

When a page is over a cap or has too many sections, name the cuts in the order you would make them, with the reason, and a skip list of what stays whatever the length.

> Cut first: the second restatement of the benefit. Then: the mission block. Then: the secondary demo. Never cut: price and its main condition, the one proof that answers the biggest doubt, the action.

Two checks expose a section that does not earn its place: delete it and see whether the argument still runs from heading to heading; read only the headings and see whether the case still holds.

## Hero check

Cover everything below the hero. A stranger should still say what it is, who it is for and what to do. If the hero needs the section under it to make sense, rewrite the hero, not the section.

## Open on the buyer's situation, then the feature

Take the situation only from supplied material (quotes, tickets, the brief). If none was given, open on the outcome the facts support and leave [add: a customer sentence about the problem].

> Feature-first: "Automated invoice reminders with custom schedules."
> Situation-first: "Stop chasing late payers by hand. Reminders go out on the schedule you set." (only if lateness was in the facts)

## Say the catch beside the objection

When a real limit exists (no mobile app yet, a setup fee, a waiting list), state it in the section that answers that worry, with the supplied reason it is acceptable, and add a "not for you if" line. Never hide it in the footer or invent one to seem candid.

## Product and benefit lines

Put the facts a buyer decides on first, in the same order for every item, one idea per sentence. A feature bullet is the feature, a dash, then what it does for the reader. Captions say something the picture does not.

## Navigation and links

Minimal navigation suits a page that asks for one action from paid or emailed traffic; the cost is that a cautious reader cannot find policies, contact or accessibility information. Keep those in the footer and state the trade-off.
