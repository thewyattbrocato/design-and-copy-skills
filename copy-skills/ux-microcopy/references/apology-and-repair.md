# Apology and repair

Load when the company did something wrong and a message must own it: an outage that cost people work, a wrong charge, a leaked or misused record, a wrongful ban, an error in something already sent, or an earlier apology that did not land. Examples are invented; adapt the pattern, not the words. Incident order and templates are in hard-moments-and-naming.md; this file covers what the apology itself says.

## First decide what the message is

- **Apology**: we did something wrong. Name it and own it.
- **Sympathy**: something bad happened that was not our doing (a storm closed the depot). Say it is unfortunate and what happens next; do not confess to it.
- **Limit**: we will keep doing this and regret the inconvenience (a fee, a cutoff time). State the rule and its reason; do not dress it as contrition.

Two tests. Would the sentence still be true if we had done nothing wrong? Then it is sympathy or a limit. Would we do the same thing again tomorrow? Then it is a limit. A request that says "apologize" for something that is a limit gets the limit stated plainly, with the reason, and the apology only for any part that was actually wrong (a confusing notice, a late warning).

## What an apology says

1. **Who, what, to whom, and what it cost**, in the active voice: "We charged 212 customers twice on 3 June. Each of you was out the second amount for up to four days."
2. **No "if", no "may have", no apology for how it was taken.** "If this caused any trouble" and "we're sorry you felt that way" apologize for the reaction. Qualify what is unknown ("we are still checking whether older invoices were affected"), never our responsibility for what is known.
3. **Full known scope in the first message**: who, how many, which data or money, since when, until when. Do not widen the admission message by message, and do not shrink it with the share of people who were fine.
4. **Repair without being asked**, sized to the harm: the refund, the restored data, the fee they paid because of us, the time it cost them. A credit or discount sits beside the acknowledgment, never replaces it, and is never tied to staying, reviewing or staying quiet.
5. **What changes**, only as far as the user has said. Never write that a failure cannot happen again or that nothing was exposed unless the user stated it; use a bracket ("[what we changed]").
6. **Ask nothing of the hurt person**: not patience, understanding, forgiveness or feedback. Do not thank them for patience before they have said how bad it was. Give a reply route that reaches a person.

Short is fine. A small slip takes one line and the fix: "We sent the old link in this morning's email. This one is right: [link]. Sorry for the mix-up."

## Explaining

Explain only when the explanation is true and helps the person judge their own risk: was it aimed at them, could it happen again, was any of it their doing (usually not, and say so). A cause appears only if the user supplied it. If the honest reason sounds like an excuse, leave it out; owning it plainly beats a weak reason.

## Incidents about security, privacy or money

Answer four questions, each from given facts or as a visible bracket: what was exposed or charged, whether it was aimed at this person, what they should do now, and what has changed. Put "what to do now" early and make it one or two concrete steps.

## Who is addressed, and when

- Address the people harmed first and directly. Partners, press and regulators come after, in their own messages.
- **Plain failure, clearly ours, many people saw it**: acknowledge at once, completely and plainly.
- **Personal harm** (someone's data misused, a wrongful ban, public shaming): a short acknowledgment now, a full account on a stated date, and a way to talk to a person. An instant, polished apology reads as wanting it over with.
- **Small accidental slip**: one line and the fix.
- Never apologize in advance ("sorry for any delays" before anything has gone wrong).

## When an apology was rejected

Do not resend it louder or longer. Read what the person said: the wrong offence named, repair too small, no chance to be heard, too soon. Reply once, shorter than the first: name what you missed, say what you will do about it, offer a person to talk to. Do not argue the person out of their view, and do not repeat the first apology's wording.

## Voice

Plain and sober by default. When the user asks for a specific voice (a game narrator, a mascot), keep the voice in word choice and keep the facts plain: what happened, what it cost, what we are doing. Do not flatten a voice the user asked for, and do not let it carry the facts.

## Before and after

- Before: "Please accept our regrets for the disruption." After: "Backups for [N] accounts did not run between [start] and [end]. If you changed files in that time, those changes are not in the backup. We ran a full backup at [time]. [Open your backup status]"
- Before: "We're sorry you feel your data was misused." After: "We shared your email address with [partner] on [date] without asking you. We have asked them to delete it and will confirm when they do. To talk to a person, reply to this email."
- Before (limit dressed as guilt): "We're so sorry, but we can't ship after 2 p.m." After: "Orders placed after 2 p.m. ship the next day."

## Quick checks

- The message is an apology, sympathy or a limit, and reads as that one.
- It names who did what to whom, with the cost, in the active voice, with no "if".
- Full known scope is in the first message; unknowns are marked as unknown with a next-update time.
- Repair is offered, not conditional, and a credit sits beside the acknowledgment.
- Nothing asks the hurt person for patience, feedback or forgiveness; a person can be reached.
- No assurance is written that the user did not give.
