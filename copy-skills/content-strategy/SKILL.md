# Content strategy

Content strategy settles four calls for a body of content: what to publish, what to cut, who answers for each piece and what makes it stale. The usual failure is a marketing plan: pillars, a thirty-item calendar, "publish more", with no owner, no capacity check and nothing stopped. Most content trouble is a decision nobody was allowed to make, not a sentence that needs polish. Answer the question asked, at the size the team really is.

## Do not produce

- An editorial calendar or topic list with no reader, job, owner or capacity behind it.
- A traffic, subscriber, revenue, budget, headcount, cost, ROI, error-rate or "N% of pages are stale" figure the user did not give. Build the case from supplied numbers, or name the measurement that would produce one.
- A process that needs people the team lacks, a committee of everyone, or an invented job title. Say "whoever does X today".
- "Publish more" or "match the competitor" as strategy, or deleting pages by traffic alone.
- A wireframe, heading outline or draft copy when a brief was asked for. A 60-page style guide.

## When not to use

- How a page reads: landing-page-copy, headlines-and-leads, editing-and-cutting. Judging existing pages: website-copy-audit. A page-level verdict goes there; this skill picks what to look at and acts on what is found.
- The positioning itself: positioning-and-messaging. Take its output as the main message; with none, state a one-line working message as an assumption. Interface strings: ux-microcopy; here, only collect requirements and name an owner.
- Whether a claim is true: honest-claims. User research: ask for their evidence; invent none.
- Explicit instructions, an existing house style and legal or contract duties win; required content stays even if nobody reads it.

## Missing facts

Do not stall. State each assumption in a line, deliver the work, and mark where an answer would change it. Ask once, as a short list with a default each, only when the session is interactive and nothing workable was supplied. Never invent a figure, a stakeholder quote, a role or a tool. Anything that hangs on a missing number gets a visible placeholder such as `[pages in scope]` or an instruction to measure it.

Scale to the team. One person or a handful: one page (a sentence of direction, a named owner, a short review rule, a monthly look). Larger groups add roles, tags and a log only where a real delay or inconsistency shows.

## The four calls

1. **What is stuck?** Name the decision in one line: publish, cut, own or fix. "Our patient leaflets contradict each other" is a fix-and-own problem; "should the changelog stay?" is a cut problem. The line picks the depth of everything below.
2. **What do we know?** Work from the list the user has: an export, a spreadsheet, pasted titles. With none, the first deliverable is the short list of columns to pull. Say what was not seen; never describe pages that were not supplied. See [evidence-and-cuts.md](references/evidence-and-cuts.md).
3. **Who answers for each item?** One named person per item or group, with the right to say no. "Everyone", "whoever is asked" and "unknown" all mean nobody. See [owners-and-review.md](references/owners-and-review.md).
4. **What makes it stale?** A trigger per kind of content (a price, a policy, a release), a last-and-next review date, and a rule for retiring. See [freshness-and-briefs.md](references/freshness-and-briefs.md).

Before adding a blog, video series or forum, ask who writes, who edits, how often and for how long. Improving what exists usually beats adding; when a programme must start from nothing, use the programme reference. When wishes outrun hours, rank them and cut at the line the hours set.

A small request skips calls: "brief one page" needs only the brief; "who should own this" needs only call 3.

## Output

Deliver the plan, table, brief or list itself first, then stop. Match the format asked; when a count or set is requested, give exactly that. At most two one-line notes (an assumption that changes the plan; something to measure) unless more are asked for. Honor a length cap and count against the text instead of asserting it.

Prefer a next step over a program: what to do this week, who does it, what it produces. If the user's plan already works, say so and change at most a word. Write in the user's language, plainly, without hype.

## Common failures

- A strategy that lists outputs ("blog, video, forum") → give a direction first, and what will not be done.
- A list of content with no owner column or stable IDs → add both, so findings can point at items.
- Usage data read as a verdict → low traffic may mean nobody can find it; "last edited" says nothing about whether it is right.
- A style guide that is a grammar manual → start from the questions that keep recurring.
- A fix that adds a review layer → find the real delay first.

## Quick checks

- The stuck decision is stated, and the answer addresses it.
- Every proposed item has an owner (or "needed: owner") and fits the stated capacity.
- No figure appears that the user did not give; gaps are placeholders or things to measure.
- At least one thing is cut, merged, paused or retired, or the answer says why nothing should be.
- Counts, caps and the two-note limit are verified.
