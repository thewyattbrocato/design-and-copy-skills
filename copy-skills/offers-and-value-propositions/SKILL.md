# Offers and value propositions

A buyer decides on three things: what changes for them and how soon, what it costs in total, and what happens if it does not work. Copy can state these clearly and honestly; it cannot rescue a thin offer. Put the terms beside the decision, in plain words, at the same weight as the benefit.

## Do not produce

- "All-in-one", "everything you need", or "unlimited" with the real limit left out.
- "Satisfaction guaranteed" with no condition, remedy or way to claim.
- Bonus "values", "was" prices or "you save" figures for things never sold or charged at that price.
- "Most popular" or "best value" with no data or reason behind it.
- "Cancel anytime" when cancelling costs something or needs notice.
- Trial copy that hides the first charge, or an intro price with no renewal price.
- Feature bullets where an outcome belongs; tiers that differ only by quantity.
- A discount with no reason, or a price drop inside one conversation for the same thing.

## When not to use

- Who it is for, category, competitors: positioning-and-messaging, if installed. If audience and category are both missing, state your assumption and write on.
- Whether a default, decoy or cancel flow steers unfairly: fair-persuasion. This skill writes honest tiers; it does not run that audit.
- Whether a claim is true and backed: honest-claims. Table construction: data-tables. Checkout fields: form-design. Whole pages: landing-page-copy. Headlines alone: headlines-and-leads.
- Setting the price, margins, contract and legal wording. You may say "this price needs a stated reason".

If a neighbor is not installed, use ordinary judgment. An explicit instruction, a style guide or a published plan beats every default here.

## If the text already works

If a value proposition or terms line already names who, the result and the cost, say so and change at most a word. Rewrite only what a reader would notice: a missing term, a vague promise, a line a rival could sign. Do not rewrite for taste.

## Method

1. **Result first.** Name the buyer, the result they want in their words, and a realistic time to the first result. Phrase it as a milestone ("first invoice sent"), not a guaranteed money or health outcome. Features follow as proof; lead with specifications only when buyers compare them (hardware, technical, procurement), most decisive first.
2. **Value proposition.** One sentence: who, the result, and how it differs from what they do now, including a spreadsheet, a person or nothing. One supporting line, using only proof the user gave. Swap test: if a rival could sign it unchanged, add the specific difference.
3. **Shorten the path before enlarging the claim.** Faster, fewer steps, less effort. Make one believable promise, not a stack; the smallest one that still compels, because a delivered promise earns referrals and a bigger one earns refunds.
4. **Terms at the decision.** Price with its period, and the yearly total beside any monthly figure. What is and is not included, and any real cap behind "unlimited". For a trial or free plan: whether a card is needed, then the date and amount of the first charge, or that there is none. Renewal price. How to cancel. Refund path. One plain line near the button plus a link to the full terms.
5. **Guarantees.** Shape: "if X has not happened by Y, we do Z", with a condition the buyer can meet and a remedy the business will honor. Use the policy the user stated. If none was given, write none and leave [refund policy]; never invent one. If the business cannot honor a guarantee, state the plain refund policy instead of a weaker-sounding promise.
6. **Deals.** Every discount has its true reason (annual billing, a dated launch, a season), shown as the same arithmetic both ways. A regular price may appear only if it was actually charged. Prefer added value to a lower core price. Per-day framing sits beside the full total. A genuine annual saving or dated price change should be stated plainly and confidently, not hedged.
7. **Bonuses.** Real and deliverable only; describe what each does. Give a money value only if it is or was sold at that price.
8. **Tiers.** Two to four, sharing a core, each higher one adding something a real buyer needs. Name them plainly and say in the blurb who or which situation each suits; state what lower tiers lack. No tier exists to make another look cheap. Recommend one with a reason. Three or fewer when buyers compare; for complex configurations hand the table to data-tables, if installed, and keep blurbs short.
9. **Price visibility.** Show the price where the decision is. "Contact sales" only when pricing is truly custom, and say what it depends on. A pricing-page reader is already interested: lead with price, inclusions and terms, then answer worries (migration, contract length, data export, what if it fails) with facts the user gave.
10. **"Free"** says what is free, for how long and what happens after.

## Missing facts

Never invent a price, discount, trial length, bonus value, guarantee, plan, count, deadline, stock limit, review, rating or customer. This holds even when the user insists or no fact sheet came with the request. Write the sentence you can stand behind or leave a visible placeholder such as [first charge date] or [add a real customer quote], and still deliver the copy. Ask one question only when the session is interactive, the answer changes the wording and asking is cheap; otherwise deliver with placeholders. Add no extra term, even a mild one like "no contract" or "nothing hidden", unless the user gave it. A result the user measured is not a result the product caused: say "we think" or state the measurement. Ad and consumer rules on trials, auto-renewal and price comparisons vary by place; tell the user to check where they sell.

## Output

Deliver the requested copy first, with nothing before it. When a count or set is asked for (three taglines, two blurbs under a limit), give exactly that and count characters rather than asserting them. Honor any stated length cap. Add at most two one-line notes, only where they change what the user does, such as an inference of yours or a placeholder to fill. For a critique, quote each weak line, say why in a clause, and give the fix. For "why is this a problem", answer in the space asked.

## Quick checks

- The value proposition names who, the result and the difference.
- Every price has its period and total; every trial states its first charge.
- Every guarantee has a condition and a remedy; every discount has a reason.
- No figure, badge or policy appears that the user did not give.
- A buyer could choose a plan from this copy alone.
