# Proposals and decision support

Load for the words that recommend an option, propose a scope, answer "that costs more than we expected", or offer a way to lower the buyer's risk. Brackets come from the user or stay visible. A reply to a price reaction is a short message, not a proposal.

## Recommend, with what would change it
Default: when a buyer is choosing among options, or has stalled on a choice, name one and give the reason in a sentence, drawn from what they said. Add the condition that would make another option better. Keep the full list one step away rather than hiding it.
- "Start with [option]: you said [their reason]. If [condition], [other option] fits better, and you can [switch path, if the user stated one]."
- Asking a stuck buyer for more needs adds load. Narrow the choice instead.
- Change when the buyer already has a clear, informed preference, when the brief says nothing about their situation (describe who each option suits and stop), or when the seller gains from one option in a way the buyer cannot see (say so).

## Options in a proposal
Default: send a proposal after the buyer has said they want to go ahead. Before that, answer the question asked. If only one route exists, confirm the agreed terms in a short order summary instead of inventing alternatives.
- Default three real packages when budget or scope is unclear, two when one decision is open, with the middle one suggested and its reason stated. The top option is a stretch someone might truly buy, not a prop.
- Each option stands alone and says what it leaves out. Show unit breaks as arithmetic.
- Change when the user supplied one package: write that one, and do not invent the others.

## Safety nets the business can honestly offer
Match the net to the worry. Fear of choosing wrong gets a plan-change window. Fear it will not deliver gets a small pilot. Fear of lock-in gets a short first term or a dated exit. Fear of the effort gets named support.
- Offer only nets the user said exist. If the business cannot give money back or a long window, say what it does give, plainly, and stop there.
- A pilot names what it covers, who owns what on each side, the one result that counts as success (a measure the buyer helped choose), checkpoint dates and an end date. Say what happens at the end: it moves to paid only if the buyer says yes in writing, and otherwise it stops.
- Carving out the riskiest piece with an opt-out is a valid net; say what it covers.

## A believable range, not the maximum
Default: state the expected result as the typical case from figures the user gave, and build any business case on the low end of them. A best-case figure may follow, labelled as best.
- With a single measured result, present it as one result with its scope and date, not as what to expect.
- With no figures, write the arithmetic in brackets ([hours saved] x [rate] = [value]) and supply no numbers.

## Start smaller
When the budget or readiness is below the largest package, suggest a smaller first step. Say what it covers, what it leaves for later and what the next step would be. If the large package is not right for them yet, say so.

## Answering "this costs more than we expected"
Default: restate the goal the buyer gave, state the price once as a single figure with what it includes and replaces, ask how it compares with what they had in mind, and stop. Do not open with a concession.
- Offer a reshaped scope before a lower price: what drops, what stays, the new figure, with the arithmetic. A smaller scope at a smaller price is a different offer. The same scope for less is not.
- Every concession gets something back with a stated reason: a longer term, payment up front, a smaller scope, a reference the buyer agrees to give. Where a shorter term or instalments cost the seller more, show that as a visible difference in the total.
- A one-off exception for a real circumstance is written down with its reason and a date to review it, and is not quoted as the standard price.
- Change when the user wants the price held: restate the value in the buyer's terms, name what the price covers, and say the figure stays.

## Naming a price early
Default: when asked before value is built, give a range and name what moves it ("from [low] for [scope], up to [high] when [driver]"). Do not give only the ceiling, and do not defer a direct question without saying when the answer comes. Change when pricing is truly custom: say what it depends on and when a figure will arrive.

## Pairs
- Before: "Great news, we can offer you 15% off if you sign this week."
- After: "The plan covers [scope] for [price]. If [budget] is the limit, we can drop [item] and [item], which brings it to [price]. If you would rather keep the full scope, [longer term or payment option] changes the total to [price]."
- Before: "Our pilot is risk-free."
- After: "A [length] pilot with [team], measured by [result the buyer chose]. We review on [dates]. On [end date] it stops unless you confirm in writing."
