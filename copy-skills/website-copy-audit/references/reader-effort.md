# Reader effort and wording

Load when the audit turns on how hard the words are to follow or how plainly they say what happens to the reader: pricing, terms, cancellation, refund, incident or policy pages; a hero that defines the product and gives no reason to care; "why does this feel corporate, pompous or hard to follow"; sections where the point comes last. Every example is invented and generic. Wording findings follow the same format as any other finding: quote, reader consequence, severity, fix.

## Pages that bind the reader: who acts

Default to a subject scan for every sentence about money, access, data, cancellation or a failure. Name the subject of each. Mark who is missing (usually the company) and which consequence sits inside a noun or a passive.

- Quote the sentence, say in plain words who does what to whom, and rewrite with the company as subject and the act as a verb. "Rates are subject to revision" becomes "We will raise the monthly rate from $8 to $10 on 1 March." Where the owner gave no amount or date, put a bracket in its place.
- A stacked negative ("you will not be charged unless you do not cancel before the end of the term") becomes the instruction: "Cancel before 3 March and nothing is charged." Keep the negative when the denial is the message ("We never sell your address").
- Severity: wording that hides a cost, a renewal or a data use is a blocker when the page elsewhere shows the owner knows it (a renewal charge in a sentence that never says "charge"). It is a major when it reads as house style rather than concealment. Say "reads as evasive", never "they are hiding it"; you judge the page, not the intent.
- Change when the passive is the honest choice: the actor is unknown ("the file was moved while we investigate") or the reader is the point ("your order was packed"). Do not flag those.
- Legal wording the owner must keep stays as it is. Suggest a one-sentence plain summary beside it instead of a rewrite.

## The opening: does the reader get a reason

Default to reading the hero and first section as a stranger and asking, after each sentence, what the reader gains or risks. The common failure is an opening that defines the product and names no stake: what goes wrong without it, and for whom.

- Quote the opening, name the missing stake, and rewrite using only supplied facts; a bracket stands in for a cost the owner did not give.
- The point (what changes for the reader) should land by the end of the first section, not after three feature lines or a story.
- Change when the delay is deliberate: a personal essay, an About page whose story works, a launch teaser the owner chose. Say the delay is on purpose and leave it.

## Point position and order inside a section

- Read the first sentence of each paragraph. When the point sits in the last sentence, quote both and say the reader must wade through detail to learn why it matters. Minor for a story section; major when the buried point is a price, a term or the next step.
- A paragraph that serves no promise on the page (it fits no heading above it) is a cut candidate. Name it and say what the reader loses if it goes: usually nothing.
- Steps go in the order the reader does them; features go in the order the reader cares, not the order the company built them.
- Label headings ("Details", "Features", "Our approach") get a rewrite that states the section's point in the page's own words. A heading that uses a different term from the paragraph below it (one says "workspace", the other "project") is a consistency finding.

## Long sentences and noun strings

- Quote a sentence when the reader must hold a long opening clause in mind before reaching the verb. Say which part they hold and where to move it. A word count is a flag, not a rule; the ear decides.
- Noun strings ("customer engagement optimization capabilities") name no action. Ask what the reader does or gets and use that. If the page never says, it is a message problem, not a wording one.
- A page of uniformly short fragments is also a finding when it reads as choppy. Do not recommend short sentences as the cure for everything.

## Certainty and hedges

- Sweep for "clearly", "obviously", "proven", "revolutionary" and "of course". Each either has support on the page (a number with its basis, a named source) or is padding. Unsupported certainty about a result belongs with the claims findings and takes their severity; the fix is the plain fact or a placeholder, not a stronger word.
- Check the opposite: a core promise buried under "may", "might", "up to" and "in some cases". Say which hedge carries real scope (keep it) and which is reflex (cut it). A rewrite never drops a true qualifier.

## Register

- Quote abstract-noun and euphemism wording where a plain word exists: "service interruption" for an outage, "suboptimal outcome" for it failed, "utilization" for use. Give the plain word.
- Show pompousness by the quoted line, not by the adjective. Keep terms the audience shares (an engineering page may use engineering words).

## What not to flag

- A deliberate voice that works for its audience.
- Grammar folklore: split infinitives, ending a sentence on a preposition, starting with "But" or "And", "data is". Fix a real error (wrong word, broken agreement, a dangling phrase that changes the meaning) only when it confuses the reader or costs trust, and spend one line on it.
- A short, clean, specific page. Say it works and change at most a word.

## Checks

- Each wording finding quotes the sentence and says what the reader has to infer, wait for or do because of it.
- Every rewrite uses the page's own facts; each missing amount, date or cause is a visible bracket.
- Nothing flagged is a voice choice, a fitting passive or folklore grammar.
