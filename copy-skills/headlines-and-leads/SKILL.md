# Headlines and leads

The first words decide whether the rest gets read. A good line says who it is for and what changes for them, makes sense read alone, and uses only facts the user gave.

## Do not produce

- "Unlock", "elevate", "supercharge", "reimagined", "seamless", "the power of", "all-in-one platform for".
- A slogan with no subject, or the company name as the first word to a cold reader.
- "Tired of...?" openers, "X: the Y for Z" colon templates, "not just X, it's Y".
- Three benefits stacked in one line.
- A tease the next line does not pay off, or a pun a skimmer cannot decode.
- Section headings that are only labels on a persuasive page ("Features", "Why us").
- Warm-up first sentences ("In today's fast-paced world...", "We're excited to...").

When one of these is tempting, replace it with the specific thing it stood in for, not a synonym.

## When not to use

- Email subject lines and preview text: email-and-sequences if installed; otherwise treat the subject as its own small task and do not apply page rules.
- Buttons, links and interface headings: ux-microcopy if installed. Those stay literal, never teasing.
- What the product is or who it is for: positioning-and-messaging if installed; otherwise work from the facts given and state the assumption in a clause.
- The rest of the page: landing-page-copy if installed. Size and weight of a heading: visual-hierarchy if installed.

A brand guide, voice sample or house style beats every default below.

## Method

Small tasks (polish one line, choose between options) can start at step 3.

1. **Name the reader and the one idea**, as a sentence someone could disagree with. Two ideas joined by "and": keep the one this reader cares about most; the other goes lower on the page.
2. **Match the opening to what the reader already knows**, judged from the traffic source and page, not taste. Someone about to buy needs the offer and its terms. Someone who knows the category needs the outcome and the one difference. Someone who feels a problem but not a fix needs the problem in their words, then the hope. Someone who has not noticed the problem needs a true, surprising fact or a very short scene tied to the product by the next line. Details in [openings-and-surfaces.md](references/openings-and-surfaces.md).
3. **Check what the surface does with the line.** A page or article headline only has to earn the next sentence. A search result, ad unit, notification or title tag is the whole exposure, so it must also say what it is and who it is for, with the brand when the context does not supply it. Note truncation points and any cap.
4. **Draft options that differ in approach, not wording:** the outcome stated plainly, the reader's problem in their words, the contrast with the old way, the reader's real question, a customer phrase the user supplied, the category plus one difference. Draft more than you show.
5. **Cut any option that** needs the body to make sense, claims more than the facts support, could carry a rival's name unchanged, depends on a pun, or opens a question the next line does not answer.
6. **Write the first sentence** short, carrying the news or the reader's situation, so the second sentence is necessary.
7. **Check the thread.** The body delivers what the headline promised; the page repeats the promise of the ad that sent the reader.

## Judgment calls

- **Clear or clever:** default clear and specific. Wordplay is fine when the brand voice is playful and the line still reads alone, or the subhead supplies the meaning.
- **Outcome or curiosity:** default outcome. Curiosity only when the next line on the same page pays it off.
- **Product name first:** default the reader first. Lead with the product when the reader already knows it (pricing, renewal, brand search) or the name says the category.
- **Problem-first:** for readers who feel the problem; blame the situation or the old tool, never the reader. Outcome-first for readers already comparing.
- **Length:** as short as the idea allows, subhead carrying the specifics in one sentence. Longer is fine when the specifics are the hook. Never trim a line until its meaning goes vague.
- **Questions:** statements by default; a question only when it is the reader's real question, or a yes that truthfully selects the audience.
- **Numbers:** only ones the user gave, with their basis kept close. Never pick a number for how it looks.
- **Section headings:** state the section's point, so the headings read in order give the page's argument. Plain labels for navigation; the reader's questions for help content.
- **Persuasive or transactional:** the rules above are for persuasive surfaces. Receipts, errors, settings and consent get the answer first, plain words, no teasing.
- **Devices** (mirrored pair, triple, rhyme): taglines and closing lines only, one per line, after a plain version exists, and never to make a weak claim feel true.

## Missing facts

Never invent a number, quote, review, rating, customer, award, deadline, stock limit, comparison or result the user did not give, and never turn a measured result into a claim that the product caused it. When proof is missing, write the strongest true line without it, or use a visible bracket such as [real figure] where the user would add it, and still deliver the lines. Ask one question only when the session is interactive and the answer changes the copy; otherwise deliver. With no brief at all, write for the most likely reader and state that assumption in a clause. For the full procedure on claims, use honest-claims if installed.

## If the line already works

Say so and change at most a word. A strong line is kept, not rewritten for taste. When choosing among supplied options, pick one, give the reason in a sentence, and do not write new ones unless asked.

## Output

Deliver the lines first, with nothing before them. When a count or set is asked for (ten options, one headline, a pair), deliver exactly that. Otherwise give two or three strong options, each a different approach. Add at most two one-line notes, and only when they change what the user does (a pick and why, or a test pair judged on sign-ups or sales, not clicks). Honor any stated limit, and count characters or words rather than asserting that you did.

## Quick checks

- Read alone, headline and subhead say what it is and who it is for.
- A rival could not sign it without changing it.
- No fact is added that the user did not give, and no "always", "never" or superlative the facts do not support.
- Within any stated length, counted.
- Options differ in approach; headings in order tell the argument.
