# Openings and surfaces

## Match the opening to the reader

Decide from where the reader came from and what the page is, then lead accordingly.

| The reader | Lead with | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Came looking for this product (pricing page, brand search, renewal) | The offer, its terms and what is included | Re-explaining the category |
| Came with the problem | The problem in their words, then what changes | Features, price |
| Came from elsewhere (an ad, a feed, a link) | One concrete result or fact, tied to the product in the next line | Mentioning price or product in the headline |

A line that works for one reader fails for another. When the audience is mixed, write for the traffic source that sends most visitors and say so in a clause.

When rivals all make the same promise, lead with the proof a reader can check, not a stronger word. If the user gave no such difference, say the line is generic rather than inventing one.

## Surfaces

- **Page headline and subhead:** one unit. One picks out the reader or states the promise; the other gives the specifics.
- **Search results and title tags:** roughly 60 characters before truncation (check the current limit); put the keyword and the point first, the brand last unless it is the draw.
- **Ads:** the headline is often the whole exposure. Say who it is for and what they get; keep the brand where the surface does not show it. Count against the hard cap and cut the lowest-value word first.
- **Articles and case studies:** a title that promises something specific, then a first sentence that delivers news or the reader's situation. Do not open by defining the topic.
- **Section headings:** each states the section's point; read only the headings and the page's argument should survive.
- **Taglines:** one idea, short, usable out of context. Offer a plain version beside any clever one.

## Short pairs (generic, for the pattern only)

- "Elevate your scheduling experience" -> "Book a groomer from your phone" (only if the facts say so).
- "Why choose us?" -> a heading that states the one reason, if the user gave it; otherwise a plain label.
- "Tired of messy spreadsheets?" -> the outcome, in plain words.
