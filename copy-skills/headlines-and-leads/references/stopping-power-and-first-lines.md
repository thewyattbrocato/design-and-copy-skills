# Stopping power and first lines

Load when a line must work at a glance, when choosing among forms, or when writing the sentence under a headline.

## Check the literal line before any wordplay

Write the plain version before any clever one: who it is for, the one benefit, a believable size. A second benefit goes in the subhead, never the same line. Only then try wordplay: the key word last, or a paired opposition. Keep the plain line beside it when the turn is not obvious on first read.

## Three tests, run and shown

- **Glance:** the line plus the sender or logo alone must say what it is, for whom, and what they get. If a reader needs the body to decode it, rewrite.
- **Delete:** remove each word group in turn. If the idea survives, the group was decoration; leave it out. A line of fourteen words with two benefits usually loses one.
- **Second meaning:** read it for an unintended meaning, and as a stranger who knows nothing about the product. An intended double meaning must make sense in both readings, or it is a pun to cut.

For posters, ads and notifications, aim for about seven words as a starting budget, not a rule; count them, and test the figure when a test is possible.

## Forms and when each fits

- Direct benefit: the default.
- News: only if it is new to this reader.
- How-to or number list: when the content really is a procedure or a list.
- Question: the reader's own question, never one that flatters the advertiser.
- Curiosity: only attached to a benefit or news the next line delivers. Never withhold the product itself.
- Warning or high-stakes topic: state the real stakes and the remedy. No shaming, no invented alarm, no number unless supplied.

## Division of labor

- Clever headline: pair with a plain subhead or straight visual. Do not make the visual repeat the words. A visual that contradicts the line can carry the idea when the line still reads alone; default to the straight visual.
- Subhead adds the specifics; it does not restate the headline.
- The first body line continues the headline and states the benefit. It never explains the headline, and the warm-up is deleted. A story or surprising fact may open only an article-style page.

## Failure patterns to catch in your own drafts

Power-word soup, hype superlatives ("best", "world's finest") with nothing behind them, wordplay that hides what the product is, questions about the advertiser. When a user insists on a superlative the facts do not support, say so in one line and deliver the specific true version.

## Testing a pair

Hold offer, placement and timing constant; split traffic concurrently; judge on the downstream result (sign-ups, sales), not clicks. Small samples prove little; say so once.

## Before and after (generic)

- "Reimagine your mornings" (breaks the glance test) -> "Hot coffee at your desk by nine" (only if the facts say so).
- "Do you want better sleep?" (advertiser question) -> "[the real benefit] for [the reader]" until the facts arrive.
