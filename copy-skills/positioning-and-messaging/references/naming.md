# Naming

Load when the task is to generate, rank, screen, rename or defend names for a product, company, feature, plan or tool. A name is one to three words that a stranger meets cold: in a store listing, a spoken prompt, a notification, a handle. The position says what the name must do; this file says how to find candidates and which to drop.

## Set the strictness by surface

- **Commands, settings, features, plan labels.** Default literal and short, one name per thing, the same grammatical shape across a set. Change to a plain modifier on a familiar word when rivals call their equivalent the same thing and buyers need to tell yours apart.
- **Product and company names.** Default to a familiar word used with a twist, a short phrase or a plain compound. Go literal when the buyer must grasp the category from the name alone (a local service, something found by search). Go suggestive when every rival's name describes the category in the same words.
- **Never a coinage with no hook.** A made-up string that sounds like nothing the listener knows is forgotten within a day; the SKILL.md rule on defining a coined name still applies.

## Work from a mini-brief, shown only if asked

Settle these for yourself, with assumptions for what is missing, and show them as one line of assumptions. Write them out as a brief only when the user wants one to hand to someone else.

- Who meets the name first, and where (spoken, listed, printed, typed).
- The action or remark the name should provoke, stated as something a person would do, not as an adjective.
- Three to five personality words, imagining the product as a person.
- Themes and styles to avoid, each with its reason, including who the name must not sound like.
- Two or three rival names, and what about each not to copy.
- One sentence that uses the name the way a customer would.

## Generate wide, show few

Default: work through thirty or more candidates and show five, or the number asked. A feature or plan label needs only five to eight honest variants.

Take candidates from at least four of these sources, not from staring at the category:

- The verb the customer performs, and the unglamorous thing they do now.
- The result, in their plain words.
- An object, place or role from the world where the product is used.
- An everyday phrase or idiom, bent a little.
- Vocabulary from an unrelated trade that has the same shape of problem.
- Half-ideas that failed on the first pass. Keep them; the winner often grows from one.

Check the shown list: no more than two names share a construction (the same ending, the same prefix, the same trick). Ten vowel-dropped or "-ly" and "-io" coinages is one idea ten times. Most candidates will fail the screen, including the ones you like first.

## Screen: run each test, report only the failures

1. **Heard once, typed right.** Say the name as on a phone call. If the listener would have to ask how it is spelled, it fails: respelled words, dropped vowels, swapped letters, a numeral standing in for a word.
2. **One reading.** Write it in capitals, with no spaces, and as a handle. Any second pronunciation, odd break or accidental word fails. So do camel case, stray capitals, accents, dots and symbols.
3. **Room to grow.** List what the business could add or change in five years: product line, material, place, price point, audience. A name that contains one of them fails.
4. **Not interchangeable.** Put a named rival's product under the name. If nothing feels wrong, the name is tame. It also fails if it copies a rival's formula (a shared ending, colour plus noun).
5. **Outsider reading.** Someone in the audience but outside the trade should guess roughly what it is for, or at least not guess wrong. Trade slang, founders' shorthand, foreign words and codenames fail.
6. **No unwanted reading.** Check slang, a rude word, a rhyme with something unpleasant, and a picture that clashes with the product (a name that suggests smoke on a baby-care brand). Name the languages you checked and say you cannot vouch for others.

Then picture the name where it first appears: the store title, the notification, the spoken prompt, the invoice line. A name that truncates into something else fails.

## Rank, with the weakness

Default a ranked shortlist, best first. Each entry has the name, one clause on why it fits the brief, the name inside a sentence a customer would say, and its biggest weakness or the test it nearly failed. Change to a plain list when the user asks only for a list.

When the user supplies the names, rank those. Drop the failures and name the test each one fails. Add new names only when asked, or when none survive.

## What you cannot check

Trademark, domain, social handles, app-store collisions, live search results and meaning in other languages are outside what you can run. Say once, in one line, which of these matter for the top names, and send trademark questions to a lawyer. Never write "available", "clear" or "unique". Leave these checks until after the ranking: a blocked name often has a clear neighbour.

Default to keeping the best name even when you cannot confirm its exact-match domain; a modifier word or a short phrase in the address is normal. Change when the name only works if the bare address is theirs, such as a spoken-only product. Owning a domain does not mean owning the name.

## Plans and features

Name from what the buyer gets or who the plan is for, in parallel shapes, so a reader can tell the ladder without opening it. A ladder of rank words ("Basic", "Plus", "Max") tells nobody who each plan suits; add the situation in the label or the line beside it. Tier contents and price belong to offers-and-value-propositions if installed.

## Renaming

Default to keeping the name and adding a plain descriptor beside it. Change when one of these holds: the name now describes a smaller business than the real one, every first conversation needs an explanation, there is a conflict, or only insiders understand it. Costs to count: customer notice, search standing, reprinted and redesigned material, retraining, and attachment inside the team.

A "should we rename?" answer opens with the decision (keep, add a descriptor, or rename) and the trigger behind it. Step up in order: descriptor line, then a sub-brand for the new line, then a full rename. When recommending a rename, give candidates built for the new scope and a short transition: what changes first, and what stays until customers have caught up.

## Several reviewers

Each reviewer reads the full list alone, over days, and picks ten to twenty promising names, not perfect ones. The meeting is for arguing for picks, not against others, and the question is "does it fit the brief", not "do I like it". Refuse the hybrid of two proposals; it usually keeps the flaws of both. Do not ask friends or strangers which name they like. Ask three to five people from the audience to hear it, spell it, say what it does, and recall it the next day.

## Keep it safe

No names leaning on edgy, religious or bodily jokes, or on a real person's name, unless asked. A pun is fine once; do not carry it onto every plan, feature and email subject.
