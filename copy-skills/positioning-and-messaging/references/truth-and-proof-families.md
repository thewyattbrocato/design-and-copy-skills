# Truth, proof and voice from evidence

Load when the position must be rooted in what buyers feel, when a real weakness has to be stated, when a rank or superlative is requested, or when a voice must be derived from supplied material.

## Start from a buyer truth, then prove it with facts

A feature list says what the product has. A buyer truth says what the buyer already believes or dreads about their own day. Root the controlling idea in the truth; the features become its proof.

Find a truth only in supplied material: a repeated phrase in reviews or tickets, the thing buyers do before they find you, the worry they voice before buying, or the reason a customer gives for staying. With none of these, write the position from the facts and add [add: a customer sentence that shows the underlying worry]. Do not write a plausible truth yourself.

> Feature-rooted: "Shared calendars with timezone display and booking links."
> Truth-rooted (from three supplied tickets): "Nobody on the team has to ask when someone is actually free. The calendar shows it." Features follow as proof.

## State the catch

When a real limit follows from the product's strength (slower but exact, small but personal, no mobile app yet), say it once, plainly, with the supplied reason it is worth it. The catch tells the reader the rest is believable, and it sends the wrong buyer elsewhere early.

- Use only a limit the user gave or the facts show. Do not invent a weakness to look candid.
- Never claim the opposite strength. A trade-off line states the figure and the reason, not "fast enough".
- Flag it for the owner to confirm before it goes public.

## Ranks, superlatives and thin proof

When asked for "number one", "best", "most trusted" or "leading", check what the supplied facts support. If they support something smaller (twelve reviews averaging a high score), write that, with the count and a [source and date] placeholder. A true small number beats an untrue big one, and say so in one line. Never compare against a rival's numbers you were not given.

## Messages come in three families

- **Practical**: what it does, costs or saves, from checkable facts.
- **Felt**: how it feels to use or who it is for, from the buyer truth.
- **Vouched for**: what others say, which only comes from real customer or third-party words.

Lead with two or three practical messages and at most one felt one. If the user supplied no quote, review or rating, leave the vouched-for slot as a visible placeholder. A brief with only practical facts is complete, not weak.

## One word the buyer would use

If the idea has to land in a single word or phrase, use a word the buyer already says (taken from supplied customer language) and keep it provable: if the product stops being that, the word goes. Do not offer a word that needs a decoder.

## Voice from evidence

Compare two supplied vocabularies: what customers write (reviews, tickets, emails) and what the company writes about itself. The gap is the finding. Words customers use that the company never does are the ones to adopt; company words customers never use are the ones to drop.

Produce three or four habit-and-limit pairs from that gap, each with a say / don't-say pair drawn from the supplied lines. If asked to settle a disputed voice among a team, record the choices as a short decision log (trait, the rejected alternative, why) rather than a list of adjectives.

## Internal compass versus public line

A mission line is for the team to decide by. Make it short, plain, true and give one concrete example of the work. It is not a slogan; do not polish it into one, and avoid "leading provider", "empower" and "transform".

## Bios and pitches

Short (one or two sentences): who, what they do, for whom. Long (one paragraph): adds the reason and one specific proof that was supplied. The same fact, never a new one, in both.
