# When difference is not why people buy

Load when the product sits in an established category beside look-alike rivals, when the brief asks for a differentiating story the facts do not support, or when a gap against the leader is being read as a message problem. Difference-first stays the default in SKILL.md; this file is the alternative mode and the test for switching.

## The disagreement

One school holds that people choose what is meaningfully different, so a narrow, focused claim wins. Another, built on large studies of what people actually buy, holds that in mature categories rivals sell to mostly the same people, few buyers call any brand different, and growth follows being recognised, remembered at the moment of need and easy to get. Neither is settled. The second school's evidence comes mainly from frequently bought consumer categories with many brands; its authors argue it reaches further, on thinner data. It is least tested for new categories, lock-in, subscriptions and one-off, high-consideration purchases.

## Context test

Difference-first when any of these holds: the product or category is new; the product is small with no recognition to build on; the difference is functional and a stranger can check it (a trial, a spec, a listing); the purchase is rare, costly or hard to leave.

Parity mode only when all hold: the category is established, with several rivals selling similar things at similar prices; the supplied facts show no difference a buyer can see or verify, or the one claimed is one rivals also claim; buyers pick among known names more or less routinely.

Mixed signals: run difference-first.

When the answer departs from the default, say so in one clause inside the recommendation ("no checkable difference was supplied, so the position rests on X"), because the user expects a differentiator. Add nothing more.

## Check the difference before building on it

Ask what shows that buyers perceive it: customer words, review or ticket phrases, a trial result, a switching reason. A founder's belief or a survey score is not. With none, keep the claim marked [unproven] and do not invent one. A "premium", "eco" or "innovative" story with no fact behind it is the mistake this mode exists to catch.

A score that trails the market leader is partly size, since a smaller brand shows weaker numbers almost automatically. Compare with brands of similar size before writing a plan to close a gap. This holds best in repeat-purchase consumer categories; for one-off purchases it is one input among others.

## What the message does in this mode

Each rule carries its limit, because the evidence behind it is uneven.

1. **Lead with the category word and the moment of need.** Name the situations when the need arises, as several short moments, not one persona. Strongest for frequent, unplanned purchases; for planned ones the moment is the research trigger.
2. **Remove what puts buyers off before adding reasons to choose.** A missing option rivals have, an awkward way to order, an unclear price, a hard-to-find shop or page. Write about a fix only from supplied facts.
3. **Be recognisable with the name covered.** Pick a repeated phrase, sentence shape, colour, character or sound, use it everywhere, and keep it across campaigns. Choose cues for being recognised, not for what they mean. Visual marks belong to brand-identity.
4. **Keep the group as wide as the real buyers.** If rivals sell to the same people, keep any skew as one situation among others rather than shrinking to a persona. This weakens where the product has a functional constraint (a children's cereal, a luxury item), and the narrowest-group default still applies to an early product with no recognition.
5. **Expect a feature lead to be matched.** If the only difference is a feature rivals can copy, use it while it lasts and do not build the position on it.

## One real difference, said plainly

When a verifiable difference exists, state it once, with its proof, in the buyer's words. Do not stretch it into a personality or a lifestyle.

The two modes fit together: a position can say one thing and still reach widely. Keep the claim single, and keep the work recognisable without the name, which is why the controlling idea is repeated rather than rotated.

## Worked pair (invented product)

A tyre-fitting chain sells the same brands at the same prices as two rivals.

Before: "Premium, innovative tyre care for discerning drivers."

After: "Tyres fitted while you wait, at the shop nearest your commute. [add: number of shops, hours and wait time, each from the user]" It names the category and the moment (a worn tread, a warning light), removes a put-off (a day without the car) only if the user confirms the wait, and carries one repeatable phrase. Any claim of better fitting or longer-lasting tyres stays [unproven: needs a fact the user can show].

## Limits

Media reach, shop availability and price are outside this skill; name one only when the position depends on it. Never state a study result as a number.
