# Levels and structure

Load when the request names a level ("just fix typos", "is the structure okay", "what would you cut"), when the text is long enough to have an order problem, or when a cut keeps costing meaning.

## Which level was asked

Answer only the level asked. Doing more is the common failure: a typo check that returns a new draft.

- **Errors only** ("proofread", "fix typos"): fix spelling, grammar, punctuation, obvious slips. Leave word choice, rhythm and order alone. If you see a bigger problem, say it in one line and do not fix it.
- **Line edit** ("tighten", "clearer", "less corporate"): sentences and words, author's voice kept.
- **Structure** ("is the order right", "what would you cut", a long draft with no instruction): order, duplicates, what to drop. Work on blocks, not words.
- **Idea** ("does this work", "is this the right angle"): say whether the point is clear and worth making. Do not polish sentences of a piece that has no point yet.

Unclear and cheap to ask in an interactive session: ask once. Otherwise take the lightest level that fits the words used, and say the level in a clause.

## Paragraph outline

For anything past a few paragraphs, write one line per paragraph, "this paragraph does X", before you change anything. Then flag:

- two paragraphs doing the same job (keep the stronger);
- the point sitting below the first third;
- a paragraph that answers no question the reader has;
- an order the reader has to hold in their head (background after the ask, a condition after the instruction).

Fix those by moving or cutting whole blocks first. Report the outline only when the request is a critique; for an edit, use it silently.

## Cutting tests

- **Delete test:** remove a sentence, paragraph or element and reread. If nothing the reader needs is missing, it stays out. The strongest cut is usually a whole piece, not a word.
- **Shortest-carry test:** say the point in one sentence. If the draft's opening does not say it, the opening is the problem.
- **Large-cut trial:** when "tighten" has produced a small trim and the text is still slack, cut about a third on purpose, then restore only what the reader would miss. Better than shaving words evenly.
- **Aloud and in place:** read it as the reader will meet it (in the email, on the page, in the slot), and aloud. Breath-length problems and pile-ups of short sentences show there.

## Voice: mismatch or preference

Change a line for voice only when it breaks the author's own pattern or the stated audience, not because you would phrase it differently. Preference edits are the first thing to drop when the author pushes back. Explain a change with a principle in one sentence ("the condition comes before the step it governs"), never "sounds better".

## Pairs

- **Level respected.** Request: "fix typos only." Before: "We recieve your order on monday and it ship's within 2 days." After: "We receive your order on Monday and it ships within 2 days." One line after: "Structure reads fine." Not a reworded sentence.
- **Structure fix.** Before: three paragraphs of background, then "Please send the signed form by Friday." After: the ask and date first; the background cut to the one sentence the reader needs to act. The point moved up, and no new words were added.
- **Over-cut restored.** Before: "Refunds take 5 to 7 business days after we receive the return." Over-cut: "Refunds: 5-7 days." The reader cannot tell from when or in what days. Restored: "Refunds take 5 to 7 business days after we get the return." Short, and nothing the reader needs is lost.
- **Voice kept.** Before: a founder note with a joke and two deliberate fragments that works. After: the same note with one repeated word removed, and one line saying what was left alone and why.
